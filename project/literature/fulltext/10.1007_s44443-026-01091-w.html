Abstract

Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models for Internet of Things (IoT) malware detection may experience performance degradation in non-stationary data streams due to evolving malware behavior, system updates, and dynamic network conditions. In this study, we present a statistically based drift-adaptive framework that integrates a Gamma–Weibull log-likelihood-ratio CUSUM detector with lightweight, fully automated fine-tuning of LSTM and CNN classifiers. The Gamma–Weibull pairing is adopted not because it is claimed to provide the closest statistical fit under every condition, but because it offers a favorable balance between distributional modeling capability and the analytical tractability required for a closed-form, ARL\(_0\)-calibrated decision rule, providing an interpretable alternative to generic distribution-drift detectors; adaptation is performed via pseudo-labeling and clustering only after a statistically significant drift alarm. We evaluate the proposed framework on simulated data streams exhibiting abrupt, gradual, distribution-mismatched, mixed, noisy, and recurring drift, as well as on real-world IoT-23 malware traffic, and compare it with four established drift detectors using repeated Monte Carlo experiments and hyperparameter ablation studies. Furthermore, we performed an extended goodness-of-fit analysis against Lognormal and Pareto alternatives, which shows that although the Lognormal distribution provides the closest unconditional fit to the IoT-23 duration streams, the Weibull distribution yields a better-fitting member of the analytically tractable Gamma–Weibull model underlying the proposed detector. The results demonstrate accurate drift detection with zero false alarms in noise-free simulations, a favorable sensitivity-false-alarm trade-off relative to established detectors, and consistent improvements in classification accuracy and F1-score through drift-aware adaptation that one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, applied across repeated Monte Carlo and repeated-seed trials, confirm are statistically significant. These findings show that the proposed Gamma–Weibull CUSUM detector captures heavy-tailed distributional shifts in malware-driven IoT traffic, while selective, statistically gated adaptation improves classification robustness under non-stationary conditions, particularly for underrepresented attack classes, albeit with dataset-dependent trade-offs between accuracy and F1-score for the more class-imbalance-sensitive CNN architecture.

1 Introduction

The fast adoption of Internet of Things (IoT) devices, mobile platforms, and interconnected cyber-physical systems has led to a continuous generation of large-scale, high-velocity data streams. These streams, derived from sensors, software logs, and network traffic systems, support a wide range of modern applications but are increasingly vulnerable to malware, anomalous behavior, and evolving cyber threats. Consequently, machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques have become useful tools for automated malware detection and monitoring in IoT and mobile environments.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been widely adopted to extract hierarchical and spatial representations from malware binaries and network features (LeCun et al. 2015). In contrast, long short-term memory (LSTM) networks have been explored for modeling temporal dependencies in sequential data, including IoT telemetry and network traffic (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997). Additionally, unsupervised anomaly detection methods, such as One-Class SVM, Isolation Forest, Local Outlier Factor, and robust covariance estimators, have exhibited promising performance in identifying abnormal patterns, particularly in unlabeled or simulated environments (Agyemang 2024). Despite their efficiency, most of these approaches either explicitly or implicitly depend on the assumption that the statistical properties of incoming data remain consistent with those observed during training.

In real-world IoT and mobile deployments, however, this assumption may not hold. Data streams are inherently non-stationary and often undergo gradual or abrupt distributional changes or drift driven by evolving malware behavior, firmware updates, network configuration changes, and shifting user activity. These changes are commonly referred to as distributional drift, which alters the underlying statistical probability distributions of observed features and labels. Thus, static ML and DL models often experience a decline or degradation in detection performance, leading to higher false-negative rates, delayed threat identification, and reduced system reliability (Quinonero-Candela et al. 2009; Gama et al. 2014; Jordaney et al. 2017; Sommer and Paxson 2010). In security-critical IoT applications, these limitations may also increase operational and safety risks.

Therefore, detecting and responding to distributional drift in malware data streams remain significant challenges. Drift may occur abruptly, gradually, or recurrently, making it difficult to identify using fixed decision boundaries. Moreover, retraining deep models from scratch is computationally expensive and often impractical for resource-constrained edge devices. The limited availability of labeled samples for newly emerging malware further complicates continuous supervised learning, while naïve incremental updates may lead to catastrophic forgetting and unstable model behavior. These challenges are particularly evident in malware-dominated data streams, where heavy-tailed distributions, extreme observations, and class imbalance are commonly observed.

Existing research on drift detection and adaptive learning has mainly focused on monitoring changes in data statistics to determine when model updates are required (Gama 2024; López Lobo 2018). Ensemble-based and non-parametric approaches have been proposed to address gradual drift (Nguyen et al. 2023), while hybrid frameworks attempt to accommodate distributional drift and the emergence of previously unseen classes (Masud et al. 2010; Žliobaitė et al. 2015). Nevertheless, many of these methods remain sensitive to noise, offer limited interpretability, and may not be well-suited to modeling the heavy-tailed and highly skewed distributions commonly observed in malware-related IoT datasets.

Hence, these limitations motivate the development of a statistically based, parametric drift detection framework that is interpretable and computationally efficient. By explicitly modeling the probabilistic characteristics of benign and malicious behavior, such a framework has the potential to identify meaningful distributional changes at an early stage and trigger model adaptation only when necessary. Combining statistical drift detection with lightweight DL adaptation further enables selective updates of LSTM and CNN classifiers using a small buffer of post-drift samples, thereby reducing computational cost while avoiding unnecessary retraining.

In this study, we propose a unified drift-adaptive malware detection framework that integrates a Gamma–Weibull statistical drift detector with adaptive LSTM and CNN models. The proposed framework enables early and reliable detection of distributional changes in non-stationary IoT malware streams while performing automated, resource-efficient model adaptation using post-drift samples. Extensive experiments conducted on simulated data, where the detector produces zero false alarms with respect to a known ground-truth change point, and on the real-world IoT-23 malware dataset demonstrate that the proposed framework provides reliable drift detection under controlled, known-change-point conditions and statistically grounded indications of distributional deviation on real malware traffic, together with consistent improvements in classification performance under realistic and evolving malware conditions.

Although the proposed framework is built upon established components, including parametric distribution modeling, the CUSUM test, pseudo-labeling, clustering, and deep sequence classifiers, its primary contribution lies in the statistically principled integration of these components rather than in the introduction of a new component. First, the Gamma–Weibull log-likelihood-ratio CUSUM statistic provides a closed-form, ARL\(_0\)-calibrated decision rule tailored to the heavy-tailed, non-negative characteristics of IoT malware traffic, in contrast to generic, distribution-agnostic drift detectors that make minimal assumptions about the underlying data-generating process. Second, drift detection serves as an explicit statistical gatekeeper for model adaptation, initiating fine-tuning only after a statistically significant CUSUM alarm. This strategy helps avoid unnecessary retraining and reduces the instability that may result from continuously triggered updates. Third, the adaptation stage is fully automated and does not require manual labels, combining confidence-based pseudo-labeling with unsupervised clustering of low-confidence samples to efficiently incorporate post-drift data. Collectively, these design choices provide a lightweight, interpretable, and edge-deployable alternative to existing drift-adaptive learning approaches. The practical benefits of the proposed framework, relative to established drift detectors and unconditional adaptation strategies, are demonstrated experimentally in Sections 6 and 7.

2 Problem statement

Let \(\{X_t\}_{t=1}^{\infty }\) denote a continuous data stream generated by an IoT or mobile system, where each observation \(X_t \in \mathbb {R}^d\) arrives sequentially in real time and represents a feature vector derived from sensor readings or network traffic. A pre-trained ML or DL classifier, such as an LSTM or CNN model with parameters \(\theta \), maps each observation to a probability distribution over K malware or attack classes. Formally, the classifier outputs the probabilities:

$$\begin{aligned} f_{\theta }(x_t) = [\, p_1(x_t),\, p_2(x_t),\, \ldots ,\, p_K(x_t) \,]^{\top }, \end{aligned}$$
(1)

where

$$\begin{aligned} p_k(x_t) \in [0,1], \qquad \sum _{k=1}^{K} p_k(x_t) = 1, \end{aligned}$$
(2)

and \(p_k(x_t)\) denotes the posterior probability that \(x_t\) belongs to class k.

In operational IoT environments, the data-generating process is rarely stationary. At an unknown time \(\tau \), the underlying distribution may change or drift due to evolving malware behavior, system updates, or environmental variations. This situation can be modeled as a transition from a pre-change distribution \(p_0(x)\) to a post-change distribution \(p_1(x)\):

$$\begin{aligned} X_t \sim {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} p_0(x), & t < \tau , \\ p_1(x), & t \ge \tau . \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$
(3)

Such distributional drift undermines the validity of a classifier trained exclusively on samples from \(p_0(x)\). Observations drawn from \(p_1(x)\) may fall outside the model’s learned decision regions, leading to incorrect posterior estimates, misclassification of emerging malware behaviors, and increased false negatives. In security-sensitive IoT applications, these failures may substantially compromise the reliability of detection and the timeliness of responses.

The core problem addressed in this study is the development of a statistically principled and computationally efficient framework for detecting distributional changes in streaming malware data as they occur, trigger adaptation only when statistically justified, and update ML/DL classifiers using limited post-change data under resource-constrained scenarios. Specifically, the interest is to identify the transition from \(p_0(x)\) to \(p_1(x)\) or stability in \(p_1(x)\) in real time and to adapt the classifier in a lightweight manner that preserves performance, avoids catastrophic forgetting, and remains feasible for deployment in edge and mobile IoT environments.

3 Statistical drift detection model

This section presents a statistically based drift-detection framework using parametric probability modeling. The proposed approach employs the Gamma and Weibull distributions to characterize the pre- and post-change regimes, respectively. These statistical distributions are particularly well-suited to IoT and malware-related data streams, which are inherently non-negative, highly skewed, and heavy-tailed. By embedding these models within a sequential CUSUM test, the approach allows early, interpretable, and computationally efficient detection of distributional changes in streaming environments.

3.1 Choice of the probability distributions

A key requirement for reliable drift detection is selecting statistical probability distributions that can adequately capture the structure of the data before and after a change while remaining analytically convenient for online, sequential monitoring. The Gamma–Weibull pairing is adopted with this dual requirement in mind: rather than being selected because it is claimed to provide the closest statistical fit in every setting, it is adopted because it offers a favorable balance between distributional modeling capability, for non-negative, right-skewed, and heavy-tailed IoT and malware traffic, and the analytical tractability required to obtain a closed-form log-likelihood ratio that can be evaluated efficiently at each incoming observation. As shown by the extended goodness-of-fit analysis in Sections 6.1.1, 7.1, and 7.2.1, alternative distributions such as the Lognormal family can provide a closer unconditional fit to some of the evaluated data streams; the Gamma–Weibull pairing is retained not because it is universally the best-fitting model, but because it achieves a satisfactory approximation of the underlying behavior while preserving the closed-form structure on which the proposed CUSUM detector depends. In this study, the pre-change (baseline) behavior is modeled using a Gamma distribution parameterized by shape \(\alpha _0\) and rate \(\beta _0\):

$$\begin{aligned} f_0(x; \alpha _0, \beta _0) = \frac{\beta _0^{\alpha _0}}{\Gamma (\alpha _0)} x^{\alpha _0 - 1} e^{-\beta _0 x}, \quad x>0. \end{aligned}$$
(4)

The Gamma distribution provides a flexible model for non-negative and right-skewed observations. It has been shown to effectively represent benign or historically observed IoT behavior, where variability is present but extreme values occur infrequently.

For the post-change (abnormal) situation, we adopt a Weibull distribution with shape \(k_1\) and scale \(\lambda _1\):

$$\begin{aligned} f_1(x; k_1, \lambda _1) = \frac{k_1}{\lambda _1} \left( \frac{x}{\lambda _1}\right) ^{k_1 - 1} \exp \left[ -\left( \frac{x}{\lambda _1}\right) ^{k_1}\right] , \quad x>0. \end{aligned}$$
(5)

The Weibull distribution is well known for modeling heavy-tailed and extreme-valued observations, which are commonly observed in malware-driven network traffic and anomalous sensor behavior. Its flexibility allows it to capture bursty activity and long-duration events that lighter-tailed models may poorly represent. Therefore, the pairing of Gamma and Weibull distributions provides a principled parametric foundation for differentiating normal behavior from malware-induced deviations in real-world IoT streams, chosen for its favorable trade-off between distributional flexibility and the closed-form tractability required for real-time sequential monitoring, rather than for providing the closest fit under every possible data-generating scenario.

3.2 Log-Likelihood Ratio (LLR)

To enable sequential change detection, we compute the log-likelihood ratio (LLR) for each incoming observation \(X_t\):

$$\begin{aligned} \ell (X_t) = \log \frac{f_1(X_t; k_1, \lambda _1)}{f_0(X_t; \alpha _0, \beta _0)}. \end{aligned}$$
(6)

Expanding this expression yields:

$$\begin{aligned} \ell (X_t)&\!=\! \log \frac{k_1}{\lambda _1} \!+\! (k_1 \!-\! 1)\log X_t \!-\! (k_1 \!-\! 1)\log \lambda _1 \!-\! \left( \frac{X_t}{\lambda _1}\right) ^{k_1} \\&\quad - \alpha _0 \log \beta _0 + (\alpha _0 - 1)\log X_t + \log \Gamma (\alpha _0) + \beta _0 X_t \\&= [(k_1 - 1) + (1 - \alpha _0)] \log X_t - \left( \frac{X_t}{\lambda _1}\right) ^{k_1} + \beta _0 X_t \\&\quad + \!\log \frac{k_1}{\lambda _1} \!-\! (k_1 \!-\! 1)\log \lambda _1 - \alpha _0 \log \beta _0 + \log \Gamma (\alpha _0). \end{aligned}$$

The LLR provides a scalar measure of evidence indicating whether the current observation is more likely to have come from the post-change distribution than from the pre-change distribution. Under normal operating conditions, the expected LLR is negative, whereas persistent positive contributions indicate a deviation from the baseline system.

3.3 CUSUM sequential test

To detect distributional changes in real time, we embed the LLR sequence within the CUSUM test. Let \(\ell (X_t)\) denote the LLR at time t. The CUSUM statistic is defined recursively as:

$$\begin{aligned} S_0 = 0, \qquad S_t = \max \big (0, S_{t-1} + \ell (X_t)\big ), \quad t \ge 1. \end{aligned}$$
(7)

A drift is declared when the statistic exceeds a predefined threshold h:

$$\begin{aligned} S_t \ge h. \end{aligned}$$
(8)

The threshold h governs the trade-off between sensitivity to changes and robustness against false alarms.

3.3.1 Threshold selection and Average Run Length (ARL) approximation

Under the pre-change distribution \(f_0\), the expected value of the LLR is negative:

$$\begin{aligned} \mu _0 = \mathbb {E}_{X \sim f_0}[\ell (X)] < 0. \end{aligned}$$
(9)

Because the LLR increments follow a Gamma–Weibull structure, the exact in-control average run length (ARL\(_0\)) does not admit a closed-form solution. To facilitate practical threshold selection, we adopt the following first-order approximation:

$$\begin{aligned} \text {ARL}_0 \approx \frac{e^h - 1}{|\mu _0|}. \end{aligned}$$
(10)

This approximation captures the intuitive relationship that larger thresholds or smaller negative drift in the LLR increase the expected time to a false alarm. It provides an efficient, computationally simple guideline for configuring the CUSUM detector for real-time deployments.

In practice, the threshold h is selected via the following procedure: (i) collect a sample from the pre-change (in-control) system; (ii) estimate \((\hat{\alpha }_0,\hat{\beta }_0)\) by maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) and compute the empirical mean LLR \(\hat{\mu }_0\) on this sample; (iii) fix a target in-control average run length ARL\(_0\) reflecting the tolerable false-alarm rate for the deployment (ARL\(_0=2000\) throughout this study); and (iv) solve (10) for \(h=\ln (1+\text {ARL}_0|\hat{\mu }_0|)\). As shown by the noisy-environment scenario in Section 6.5, this calibration should be performed on data that reflects the expected measurement noise of the deployment environment, since a threshold calibrated on idealized noise-free data can under-estimate h and increase the false-alarm rate in practice.

3.3.2 Detection delay

After a true change occurs at time \(t = \tau \), the expected detection delay can be approximated by

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {E}[D] \approx \frac{h}{\mu _1}, \qquad \mu _1 = \mathbb {E}_{X \sim f_1}[\ell (X)], \end{aligned}$$
(11)

where \(\mu _1\) denotes the expected LLR under the post-change Weibull distribution. This expression highlights the fundamental trade-off between rapid detection and conservative threshold selection.

3.4 Parameter estimation

Accurate estimation of distribution parameters is essential for reliable drift detection. For the pre-change Gamma distribution \(f_0(x; \alpha _0, \beta _0)\), the MLE is used:

$$\begin{aligned} (\hat{\alpha }_0, \hat{\beta }_0) = \arg \max _{\alpha _0, \beta _0} \prod _{t=1}^{n_0} f_0(X_t; \alpha _0, \beta _0), \end{aligned}$$
(12)

where \(n_0\) denotes the number of historical pre-change samples.

Similarly, for the post-change Weibull distribution \(f_1(x; k_1, \lambda _1)\), parameter estimates are obtained as

$$\begin{aligned} (\hat{k}_1, \hat{\lambda }_1) = \arg \max _{k_1, \lambda _1} \prod _{t=1}^{n_1} f_1(X_t; k_1, \lambda _1), \end{aligned}$$
(13)

using a buffer of post-change observations.

During operation, a buffer of size b is maintained:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {B} = \{X_\tau , X_{\tau +1}, \dots , X_{\tau +b-1}\}. \end{aligned}$$
(14)

The post-change parameters \((k_1, \lambda _1)\) are updated incrementally using this buffer, allowing the detector to refine its representation of the new system while controlling computational overhead.

4 Integration of Statistical Drift Detection with ML/DL Models

Once the Gamma–Weibull CUSUM drift detector signals a statistically significant deviation at time \(t_0\), we extract a buffer of consecutive post-change samples,

$$ \mathcal {B} = \{X_{t_0}, X_{t_0+1}, \dots , X_{t_0+b-1}\}. $$

The buffer \(\mathcal {B}\) is designed to provide a compact yet representative snapshot of the post-change system, enabling rapid, computationally efficient model updates. In the proposed framework, drift detection serves as a gatekeeper: adaptation is triggered only when the detector crosses its decision threshold, thereby avoiding unnecessary retraining under normal fluctuations and preserving stability during in-control operation.

Figure 1 illustrates the overall workflow. The CUSUM statistic monitors incoming observations; once drift is detected, the buffered samples are converted into sequence representations, and lightweight adaptation is applied to the classifier components of LSTM/CNN models. In practice, post-change labels may be unavailable in real streaming deployments; hence, the adaptation stage is designed to operate with automatically generated labels, obtained either from high-confidence predictions (pseudo-labels) or from unsupervised clustering when prediction confidence is low.

4.1 LSTM Model adaptation

4.1.1 LSTM representation of sensor sequences

For each sequence in the buffer \(\mathcal {B}\), the LSTM updates its internal hidden and cell states as

$$\begin{aligned} i_t&= \sigma (W_i x_t + U_i h_{t-1} + b_i), \end{aligned}$$
(15)
$$\begin{aligned} f_t&= \sigma (W_f x_t + U_f h_{t-1} + b_f), \end{aligned}$$
(16)
$$\begin{aligned} o_t&= \sigma (W_o x_t + U_o h_{t-1} + b_o), \end{aligned}$$
(17)
$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{c}_t&= \tanh (W_c x_t + U_c h_{t-1} + b_c), \end{aligned}$$
(18)
$$\begin{aligned} c_t&= f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t, \end{aligned}$$
(19)
$$\begin{aligned} h_t&= o_t \odot \tanh (c_t), \end{aligned}$$
(20)

where \(x_t \in \mathbb {R}^d\) denotes the input vector at time t, ‘ \(h_t \in \mathbb {R}^h\) is the hidden state, and \(\odot \) denotes elementwise multiplication.

4.1.2 Buffer embeddings

For each buffered sequence \(X_j \in \mathcal {B}\), embeddings are computed using frozen LSTM parameters:

$$\begin{aligned} h_j = \Phi _{\theta _{\text {frozen}}}(X_j), \qquad j = 1,\dots ,B, \end{aligned}$$
(21)

where \(B = |\mathcal {B}|\) denotes the buffer size. To meet online and edge constraints, only a small subset of parameters, denoted by \(\psi \) (typically the classifier head), is updated during adaptation, while the main recurrent backbone remains fixed.

4.1.3 Automatic adaptation

Let

  • \(\tilde{y}_j\) represents the pseudo-label predicted by the pre-change classifier,

  • \(u_j\) denotes an unsupervised cluster assignment (e.g., K-means or DBSCAN),

  • \(p_j = \max _k \textrm{softmax}_k(W h_j + \textbf{c})\) is the model confidence.

The automatic adaptation label is defined as

$$\begin{aligned} y^*_j = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \tilde{y}_j, & \text {if } p_j \ge \tau , \\ u_j, & \text {otherwise}, \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$
(22)

where \(\tau \) is a predefined threshold.

4.1.4 Adaptation loss

Let \(\mathcal {D}_0 = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_0}\) denote the pre-change labeled dataset and \(\ell (\hat{y}, y)\) be the cross-entropy loss function. Let \(\widehat{w}(x_i)\) be an importance weight correcting for distributional drift.

The adaptation objective is given by

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {adapt}}(\psi )&= \frac{1}{n_0} \sum _{i=1}^{n_0} \widehat{w}(x_i)\, \ell \!\left( \textrm{softmax}(W h_i + \textbf{c}),\, y_i \right) \nonumber \\&\quad + \frac{1}{B} \sum _{j=1}^{B} \ell \!\left( \textrm{softmax}(W h_j + \textbf{c}),\, y^*_j \right) + \Omega (\psi ), \end{aligned}$$
(23)

where \((W, \textbf{c}) \subset \psi \) are trainable classifier parameters and \(\Omega (\psi )\) denotes a regularization term (e.g., \(\ell _2\) regularization).

4.1.5 LSTM adaptation procedure

  1. 1)

    Freeze most LSTM parameters; only \(\psi \) is trainable.

  2. 2)

    Compute embeddings \(h_j\) for all \(X_j \in \mathcal {B}\).

  3. 3)

    Assign pseudo-labels and unsupervised labels to obtain \(y^*_j\).

  4. 4)

    Minimize \(\mathcal {L}_{\text {adapt}}(\psi )\) via gradient descent.

  5. 5)

    Resume normal inference using the updated classifier.

  6. 6)

    Update Weibull parameters \((k_1, \lambda _1)\) for improved drift characterization.

4.2 CNN Model adaptation

4.2.1 Feature extraction

For CNNs (see also Chen et al. (2019)), each buffered sample produces a feature representation through the convolutional layers:

$$ h_j = \Phi _{\theta _{\text {conv}}}(X_j). $$

4.2.2 Automated adaptation loss

The CNN adaptation objective mirrors the LSTM case:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {CNN-adapt}}(\phi )&\!=\! \frac{1}{n_0}\sum _{i=1}^{n_0}\widehat{w}(x_i)\, \ell (\textrm{softmax}(W h_i + b), y_i) \nonumber \\&\quad + \frac{1}{b} \sum _{j=1}^{b} \ell (\textrm{softmax}(W h_j \!+\! b), y^*_j) \!+\! \Omega (\phi ), \end{aligned}$$
(24)

where \(\phi \) denotes the trainable final CNN layers or adapter blocks.

4.2.3 CNN adaptation steps

  1. 1)

    Freeze convolutional layers except the final classifier/adapter block.

  2. 2)

    Compute features \(h_j\) for all samples in \(\mathcal {B}\).

  3. 3)

    Generate automatic labels \(y^*_j\) using pseudo-labeling and clustering.

  4. 4)

    Update \(\phi \) by minimizing \(\mathcal {L}_{\text {CNN-adapt}}\).

  5. 5)

    Update batch normalization statistics.

4.3 Integration summary

  • The Gamma–Weibull CUSUM detector acts as a gatekeeper, triggering adaptation only when a statistically significant drift is detected.

  • LSTM modules exploit temporal dependencies in the stream; CNNs capture local temporal structure through convolutional feature extraction.

  • The post-change buffer \(\mathcal {B}\) supplies representative samples for fast, lightweight model updating under online constraints.

  • No human labels are required: adaptation relies on automated labels obtained from pseudo-labels and clustering when confidence is low.

  • Updating Weibull parameters \((k_1, \lambda _1)\) supports improved characterization of the evolving post-change system.

5 Performance metrics and experimental design

To assess the effectiveness of the proposed drift-adaptive approach, we evaluate (i) the quality of statistical drift detection and (ii) the downstream benefit of triggered model adaptation. The selected metrics jointly quantify detection reliability, classification performance under non-stationarity, and the practical efficiency of buffer-based adaptation for LSTM and CNN models.

5.1 Classification metrics

For multi-class malware detection, we compute standard metrics per class \(k = 1, \dots , K\):

$$\begin{aligned} \text {Accuracy}&= \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN},\end{aligned}$$
(25)
$$\begin{aligned} \text {Precision}_k&= \frac{TP_k}{TP_k + FP_k},\end{aligned}$$
(26)
$$\begin{aligned} \text {Recall}_k&= \frac{TP_k}{TP_k + FN_k},\end{aligned}$$
(27)
$$\begin{aligned} F1_k&= 2 \cdot \frac{\text {Precision}_k \cdot \text {Recall}_k}{\text {Precision}_k + \text {Recall}_k}, \end{aligned}$$
(28)

where \(TP_k\), \(FP_k\), and \(FN_k\) denote the number of true positives, false positives, and false negatives for class k.

Multi-class performance is summarized using:

$$\begin{aligned} F1_{\text {macro}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum _{k=1}^{K} F1_k, \qquad F1_{\text {weighted}} = \sum _{k=1}^{K} \frac{n_k}{N} F1_k, \end{aligned}$$
(29)

where \(n_k\) is the number of samples in class k and \(N = \sum _{k=1}^K n_k\). In addition to overall metrics, we report macro- and weighted-averaged scores to capture performance under severe class imbalance, which is common in benign-dominated monitoring and malware-focused captures.

5.2 Drift detection metrics

The effectiveness of the Gamma–Weibull CUSUM drift detector is evaluated using:

  • Detection Delay (D):

    $$\begin{aligned} D = \hat{\tau } - \tau , \end{aligned}$$
    (30)

    where \(\tau \) is the true drift time and \(\hat{\tau }\) is the time at which drift is detected. This metric is computed in simulated streams where \(\tau \) is known.

  • False Alarm Rate (FAR): Number of false drift signals per unit time (or per stream), measuring conservativeness under in-control conditions.

  • Detection Accuracy: Probability of correctly detecting a drift within a tolerance window \(\delta \):

    $$\begin{aligned} \text {Acc}_{\text {drift}} = \Pr (|\hat{\tau } - \tau | \le \delta ). \end{aligned}$$
    (31)

For real malware traffic where ground-truth change points are unavailable, FAR and the qualitative behavior of the CUSUM statistic are used to characterize robustness, while alarms are interpreted as statistically significant deviations from the estimated in-control system.

5.3 Adaptation metrics

Adaptation efficiency is quantified using:

  • Adaptation Convergence Time ( \(T_{\text {adapt}}\) ): Time required to fine-tune the model on the post-change buffer \(\mathcal {B}\) until performance stabilizes.

  • Label Efficiency: Number of automatically labeled samples used per drift event (high-confidence pseudo-labels and, when required, cluster-based labels). No human labels are required in this framework.

  • Computational Overhead: Additional floating-point operations (FLOPs) or runtime incurred by drift detection and buffer-based adaptation relative to normal inference.

5.4 Experimental design

Experiments are designed to evaluate the proposed framework under controlled and realistic non-stationary conditions, emphasizing sequential processing, low-latency detection, and lightweight adaptation.

  • Datasets:

    • Simulated Data: Synthetic IoT sensor streams with controlled drift points to measure detection delay, false alarms, and post-drift recovery under known distributional shifts.

    • Real-World Data:

      • IoT Malware: IoT-23 dataset containing labeled attack flows (see Garcia et al. (2020) and Amin et al. (2023)).

  • Baseline Models: Pre-trained LSTM and CNN classifiers trained on pre-change distribution \(p_0(x)\) serve as non-adaptive baselines.

  • Drift Scenarios:

    • Abrupt Drift: Sudden change from \(p_0(x)\) to \(p_1(x)\) at time \(\tau \).

    • Gradual Drift: Smooth transition over a window \([\tau , \tau +\Delta ]\).

  • Evaluation Procedure for Each Sample \(X_t\) :

    1. 1)

      Apply Gamma–Weibull CUSUM to detect drift.

    2. 2)

      Freeze buffer \(\mathcal {B} = \{X_t, \dots , X_{t+b-1}\}\) upon detection.

    3. 3)

      Fine-tune LSTM/CNN using automated pseudo-labels derived from model predictions (and clustering when confidence is low).

    4. 4)

      Record classification performance on post-change samples and over the full stream.

    5. 5)

      Record drift detection metrics: detection delay, FAR, and adaptation time.

  • Repetitions: Each experiment is repeated multiple times to account for stochastic or random effects in initialization, drift detection trajectories, and automated labeling.

5.4.1 Comparison scenarios

We evaluate the LSTM and CNN under two conditions:

  1. 1)

    Baseline ML/DL: Pre-trained model with no drift detection.

  2. 2)

    Proposed Drift-Adaptive Framework: LSTM/CNN integrated with Gamma–Weibull drift detection and automated buffer-based adaptation.

  • Pre-change: \(X_t \sim \text {Gamma}(\alpha _0, \beta _0)\)

  • Post-change: \(X_t \sim \text {Weibull}(k_1, \lambda _1)\)

  • Sequence length: \(T = 10000\) samples per stream

  • Number of streams: \(N = 1000\)

  • Monte Carlo repetitions: \(MC = 30\)

  • Known drift points \(\tau \) enable precise measurement of detection delay and adaptation performance.

5.4.3 Real-world data preparation

  • Normalize features and standardize inputs.

  • For LSTM: extract sequential feature vectors per time step.

5.4.4 Experimental procedure

  1. 1)

    Train baseline LSTM/CNN on pre-change data.

  2. 2)

    Sequentially feed incoming data streams.

  3. 3)

    Apply Gamma–Weibull CUSUM for drift detection (proposed framework only).

  4. 4)

    Upon drift detection:

  5. 5)

  6. 6)

5.5 Numerical comparison

To provide a comprehensive and fair assessment of the proposed drift-adaptive framework, we evaluate and compare the baseline and adaptive LSTM and CNN models across multiple dimensions reflecting classification accuracy, drift-detection quality, and adaptation efficiency. For each experimental setting, the following quantitative metrics are recorded.

  • Pre-change Classification Performance: The predictive capability of the baseline LSTM and CNN models is first evaluated on pre-change data generated from the in-control distribution \(p_0(x)\). Performance is measured using overall accuracy and F1-based metrics, providing a reference level of model behavior before any distributional shift.

  • Drift Detection Performance: For the proposed Gamma–Weibull CUSUM detector, we assess the quality of change-point detection using:

    • Detection delay:

      $$ D = \hat{\tau } - \tau , $$

      where \(\tau \) denotes the true change point and \(\hat{\tau }\) is the detected alarm time.

    • FAR: The number of spurious drift alarms raised per unit time under the pre-change system.

    • Drift detection accuracy within a tolerance window:

      $$ \text {Acc}_{\text {drift}} = \Pr \!\left( |\hat{\tau } - \tau | \le \delta \right) , $$

      which measures the probability of detecting a drift within an acceptable temporal margin \(\delta \).

  • Post-change Adaptation Performance: Following drift detection, the classification performance of LSTM and CNN models is re-evaluated after automated adaptation using the buffered post-change samples \(\mathcal {B}\). Accuracy, macro F1-score, and weighted F1-score are reported to quantify the impact of drift-aware fine-tuning in the presence of class imbalance.

  • Adaptation Efficiency: The efficiency of the adaptation process is quantified by:

    • Adaptation convergence time \(T_{\text {adapt}}\), defined as the number of fine-tuning epochs required for the adaptation loss to stabilize on \(\mathcal {B}\).

    • Label efficiency, measured as the number of pseudo-labeled samples utilized per detected drift event, reflecting the absence of manual annotation.

5.6 Performance visualization

We present:

  • Time series plots: Detection signal \(S_t\) and actual drift \(\tau \).

  • Accuracy/F1 curves: Compare baseline vs. adaptive LSTM/CNN.

  • Convergence plots: Adaptation loss over buffer fine-tuning iterations.

All figures use consistent axis labeling, legends, and color coding throughout the manuscript (true change points in red, detected alarms in green, baseline and adaptive curves in distinct colors) to ensure they remain readable at single-column width.

6 Simulation results

In this section we evaluate the proposed Gamma–Weibull CUSUM drift detector and the drift-adaptive LSTM/CNN framework on a controlled synthetic IoT stream. The stream comprises \(T=10{,}000\) observations with a true distributional change from a pre-change Gamma distribution to a post-change Weibull distribution at time \(\tau =4000\).

6.1 Drift simulation and parameter recovery

To evaluate the behavior of the proposed Gamma–Weibull CUSUM detector, we simulate an IoT data stream consisting of \(T = 10\,000\) observations with a single distributional change at time \(\tau = 4000\). The pre-change segment is generated from a Gamma distribution with shape \(\alpha _0 = 2.0\) and rate \(\beta _0 = 1.0\), while the post-change segment follows a Weibull distribution with shape \(k_1 = 1.5\) and scale \(\lambda _1 = 3.0\). The resulting time series is shown in Fig. 2, where the red dashed line marks the true drift time and the green dashed line denotes the detected drift time \(\hat{\tau }\) obtained from the Gamma–Weibull CUSUM statistic.

The MLEs of the Gamma and Weibull model parameters are computed using the pre-change and post-change segments, respectively. The estimated parameters, together with the empirical mean of the pre-change log-likelihood ratio \(\hat{\mu }_0\) and the corresponding CUSUM decision threshold \(h\), are summarized in Table 1. The estimated values closely match the true parameters, indicating that the assumed distributional forms accurately represent both scenarios. For visual confirmation, Fig. 3 displays histograms of the pre-change Gamma and post-change Weibull samples, illustrating clear differences in distributional shape before and after the drift.

6.1.1 Goodness-of-fit against alternative distributions

To validate the choice of Gamma and Weibull as the pre-change and post-change models, we additionally fit two widely used alternative positive-valued distributions, the Lognormal and Pareto families, to the same pre-change and post-change segments and compare all four candidates using log-likelihood, Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) statistic with associated p-value, and the Anderson–Darling (AD) statistic. The AD statistic places greater emphasis on tail discrepancies and therefore complements the KS test, which is more sensitive to deviations near the distribution’s median. Table 2 reports the results for both segments, and Fig. 4 visualizes the corresponding AIC values.

For the pre-change segment, the Gamma distribution achieves the lowest AIC, BIC, and AD statistic and the highest KS p-value (\(p=0.976\)), indicating that it is the best-supported model among the four candidates and validating its use as \(f_0(x)\). For the post-change segment, the Weibull distribution again attains the lowest AIC, BIC, and AD statistics and the highest KS p-value (\(p=0.937\)), indicating its suitability as \(f_1(x)\). In both segments, the Lognormal distribution is the closest competitor but is nonetheless outperformed by the true generating family, whereas the Pareto distribution provides a markedly poorer fit, as reflected in its substantially larger AIC and AD values. These controlled results, in which the ground-truth generating distributions are known, establish that the proposed model-selection procedure reliably recovers the true distributional family in both systems and provides a reliable methodological basis for the goodness-of-fit analysis subsequently applied to the real-world IoT-23 data in Section 7.

6.2 Drift detection performance

Using the estimated parameters and the approximate ARL\(_0\) formula in (10), the CUSUM threshold is set to \(h=5.204\). The evolution of the CUSUM statistic is shown in Fig. 5. Under the pre-change distribution, the increments have a negative mean \(\hat{\mu }_0=-0.0905<0\), so \(S_t\) fluctuates around zero and does not cross the threshold.

At the true drift time \(\tau =4000\), the log-likelihood ratio becomes positive on average, causing the CUSUM statistic to grow rapidly and trigger an alarm at \(\hat{\tau }=4006\). This corresponds to a detection delay of

$$ D = \hat{\tau }-\tau = 6 \text { samples}, $$

with no false alarms before the true drift. This behavior aligns with the theoretical expectations of the Gamma–Weibull CUSUM and demonstrates its ability to detect system changes in a timely and reliable manner.

6.3 Pre-change classification performance

The baseline LSTM and CNN classifiers are trained only on pre-change data, operating on sliding-window sequences of length \(L = 20\) to expose the models to short-term temporal patterns while keeping the input dimension and inference latency modest. In practice, this window length captures local trends and bursts in the scalar sensor stream without requiring a deeper or heavier network; shorter windows (\(L < 20\)) were found to degrade pre-change validation performance, whereas longer windows yielded no meaningful gains, but increased memory and delay.

Table 3 reports the validation performance on a held-out pre-change segment (Table 3 and Fig. 6). The LSTM achieves near-perfect performance, with accuracy (\(0.992\)) and macro F1 (\(0.977\)). The CNN attains comparable accuracy (\(0.909\)) but a much lower macro F1 (\(0.485\)) because it almost never predicts the minority (attack) class, leading to very low recall for that class.

6.4 Impact of drift-adaptive learning

Once the CUSUM detector crosses the threshold at \(\hat{\tau } = 4006\), a post-change buffer of size \(b = 300\) sequences is collected. The buffer \( \mathcal {B} = \{X_{t_0}, \ldots , X_{t_0 + b - 1}\} \) is formed from the first \(300\) overlapping sequences whose end times are at or after \(\hat{\tau }\). This size is chosen as a compromise between having enough post-change examples to obtain stable gradient updates while keeping the adaptation latency and memory footprint small: with \(L = 20\), a buffer of \(b = 300\) sequences corresponds to approximately \(3\%\) of the full stream and is sufficient to drive the adaptation losses down (see Fig. 7).

Automatic labels for the buffer are generated based on Section IV: high-confidence predictions (softmax confidence \(\ge 0.8\)) are used directly as pseudo-labels, whereas low-confidence samples are grouped by K-means and assigned cluster-based labels. Only the final classification layers of the LSTM/CNN are then fine-tuned on the union of the historical pre-change sequences and the buffered post-change sequences, while keeping the backbone parameters fixed.

For the time-resolved accuracy plots, we use a sliding window of size 200 sequences. We believe that this window is large enough to smooth short-term stochastic fluctuations yet short enough to localize the change around \(\tau \) without averaging pre- and post-drift distributional behavior into a single, misleading performance estimate.

The performance of the baseline and adaptive models on the entire simulated stream (pre- and post-change combined) is summarized in Table 4. The corresponding time-resolved accuracies (computed using a sliding window of size 200) are shown in Fig. 6 for the LSTM (top panel) and CNN (bottom panel). The adaptation loss on the buffer is displayed in Fig. 7, and macro F1 scores are compared in Fig. 8. For the LSTM, the baseline model already generalizes well across the drift, achieving an overall accuracy of \(0.987\) and macro F1 of \(0.977\). Nevertheless, the drift-adaptive LSTM consistently improves all metrics, increasing macro F1 to \(0.981\) and weighted F1 to \(0.991\). The sliding-window accuracy curves in Fig. 6 show that the adaptive LSTM closely tracks or slightly exceeds the baseline accuracy both before and after the drift, with a visible gain in the post-change system.

The benefit of adaptation is even more pronounced for the CNN. The baseline CNN suffers from class imbalance: its recall for the minority (attack) class across the full stream is only \(0.0056\), yielding a macro F1 of \(0.457\). After drift adaptation using the buffered samples, the attack-class recall increases substantially, and the macro F1 rises to \(0.576\) (Table 4). The adaptive CNN maintains or improves accuracy over time (Fig. 6), particularly in the post-change region where the baseline performance gradually degrades.

The adaptation losses for both models decrease monotonically over a small number of epochs (Fig. 7), confirming that the fine-tuning procedure is stable and computationally lightweight. The macro-F1 bar plot in Fig. 8 summarizes the overall effect: the proposed drift-adaptive framework never harms performance and provides noticeable gains, especially for the weaker CNN classifier. Thus, the synthetic experiments demonstrate that:

  • The Gamma–Weibull CUSUM detector reliably recovers the change point with a small detection delay (\(D=6\)) and no false alarms.

  • The proposed integration of statistical drift detection with LSTM/CNN models yields consistent improvements in accuracy and F1 scores, with particularly large gains for the CNN baseline that is more sensitive to distributional shift.

  • The entire procedure operates in an online, fully automated fashion, using only pseudo-labeled and clustered post-change samples and incurring modest computational overhead due to selective parameter updates and buffer-based adaptation.

6.5 Robustness across additional drift scenarios

The abrupt Gamma\(\rightarrow \)Weibull scenario evaluated above generates synthetic data using the same parametric families assumed by the detector, which may favor the proposed method under idealized conditions. To assess robustness under more challenging and practically relevant settings, we evaluate four additional scenarios using identical detector and adaptation hyperparameters: (i) distribution mismatch, in which the post-change segment is generated from a Lognormal distribution rather than the assumed Weibull family; (ii) gradual drift, in which the transition from \(p_0(x)\) to \(p_1(x)\) occurs progressively over a window of 500 samples rather than instantaneously; (iii) mixed post-change distributions, in which the post-change segment is an equal mixture of Weibull and Lognormal samples; and (iv) a noisy environment, in which independent Gaussian observation noise is superimposed on the abrupt Gamma–Weibull stream. Table 5 reports the detection delay and false-alarm count obtained for each scenario, together with the corresponding full-stream macro-F1 scores of the drift-adaptive LSTM and CNN models, and Figs. 9, 10, 11 and 12 present the resulting accuracy curves and macro-F1 comparisons for the mismatch and gradual scenarios, respectively.

Across the distribution-mismatch, gradual-drift, and mixed-distribution scenarios, the detector continues to identify the change point with zero false alarms and a detection delay that remains small relative to the stream length (\(T=10{,}000\)). Under distribution mismatch, the detector achieves a delay identical to the abrupt baseline (\(D=6\)), confirming that the Gamma–Weibull CUSUM statistic remains effective even when the true post-change family departs from the assumed Weibull model, because the LLR continues to capture a meaningful shift in central tendency and tail behavior. Under gradual drift, the delay increases to \(D=80\) samples, reflecting the expected trade-off between detection speed and the smoothness of the underlying transition: because the post-change scenario is reached only progressively, the CUSUM statistic accumulates evidence more slowly than under an instantaneous change. The mixed-distribution scenario yields an intermediate delay (\(D=35\)) while preserving zero false alarms (Table 5).

The noisy-environment scenario exhibits qualitatively different behavior and is reported here without adjustment for transparency. When additive Gaussian observation noise is superimposed on the abrupt Gamma–Weibull stream, the detector raises its first alarm at \(t=59\), well before the true change point at \(\tau =4000\), resulting in a negative detection delay (\(D=-3941\)) and 28 false alarms before \(\tau \). This behavior indicates that, at the considered noise level, the added perturbation alone is sufficient to repeatedly drive the CUSUM statistic above the detection threshold. This occurs because the threshold in (10) is calibrated using the mean pre-change log-likelihood ratio (LLR) estimated from the noise-free pre-change data and therefore does not account for the additional observation noise. Rather than indicating a limitation of the underlying detection framework, this result emphasizes the importance of calibrating the threshold to reflect the expected noise conditions in the deployment environment. In practice, this can be achieved by re-estimating \((\hat{\alpha }_0,\hat{\beta }_0)\) and the corresponding \(\hat{\mu }_0\) directly from noisy in-control data instead of assuming a noise-free pre-change system. More generally, this scenario illustrates the fundamental sensitivity-false-alarm trade-off inherent to CUSUM-type detectors: a threshold calibrated to be highly sensitive to small, persistent shifts in the log-likelihood ratio, as needed to achieve the short detection delays reported elsewhere in this study, is correspondingly less able to distinguish a genuine distributional change from transient noise-induced excursions of similar magnitude, and will therefore raise more false alarms when the deployment environment is noisier than assumed during calibration. Conversely, setting a higher threshold to suppress such false alarms under noisy conditions would increase the detection delay for genuine drift, reproducing the same trade-off from the opposite direction. We report this outcome to highlight an important limitation of the current threshold calibration procedure under observation noise, which is discussed further in Section 8.1.

Adaptive LSTM macro-F1 scores remain consistently high (0.981–0.989) across all five scenarios, including the noisy case, indicating that the buffer-based adaptation mechanism itself is not narrowly tuned to the idealized abrupt Gamma–Weibull setting and continues to provide effective post-alarm fine-tuning even when the upstream detector’s alarm timing is degraded by noise (Table 5).

6.6 Recurring drift and adaptation stability

A further consideration for practical deployment is the behavior of the framework under recurring drift, in which the data-generating process reverts to a previously observed system after an intervening change. To examine this scenario, we simulate a three-segment stream that transitions from Gamma to Weibull at \(\tau _1=2500\) and reverts to Gamma at \(\tau _2=7000\), with the drift-adaptive LSTM model re-adapted at each detected alarm. Table 6 reports the classification accuracy evaluated on the original (first-segment) data distribution at three stages: prior to any drift, immediately after adaptation to the first (Weibull) drift, and after a second adaptation triggered by the reversion to the Gamma system, compared against a static (non-adaptive) baseline evaluated on the same final segment.

The drift-adaptive model successfully tracks both transitions, achieving its highest accuracy (0.9873) after re-adapting to the reverted Gamma system, an improvement of 2.3 percentage points over the static baseline evaluated on the same segment (0.9640). This shows that sequential buffer-based adaptation does not lead to catastrophic forgetting in this two-transition setting and that the proposed gatekeeping mechanism, whereby adaptation is triggered only upon a statistically significant CUSUM alarm, correctly re-engages each time a system change occurs, including a reversion to a previously observed distribution.

6.7 Comparison with established drift detectors

To contextualize the performance of the proposed Gamma–Weibull CUSUM detector, we compare it against four established drift-detection methods widely used in the data-stream literature: the Page–Hinkley test (Page 1955), ADWIN (Gomes et al. 2022), KSWIN, and the drift detection method (DDM) (Gomes et al. 2022). Each detector is calibrated independently using the same in-control segment and is evaluated over 20 independent realizations of the abrupt and gradual drift streams described above. For each detector, we report the mean and standard deviation of the detection delay (computed only from alarms occurring at or after the true change point, so as to avoid conflating false alarms with detection latency), the mean number of false alarms raised prior to the true change point, and the empirical detection rate across replications. Table 7 summarizes the results, and Figs. 13 and 14 present the corresponding detection-delay comparisons.

All five detectors achieve a \(100\%\) detection rate across the replicated experiments, although they differ substantially in the trade-off between detection delay and false-alarm frequency. ADWIN exhibits the shortest detection delay (1–2 samples) but also produces the highest number of false alarms (more than 1200 per stream on average), suggesting that its high sensitivity is accompanied by frequent triggering in this type of heavy-tailed, non-stationary stream. The Page–Hinkley test produces no false alarms, consistent with the proposed detector, although its detection delay is noticeably longer, particularly under gradual drift (549.5 vs. 257.4 samples). KSWIN and DDM produce moderate-to-high false-alarm counts, along with relatively large variability in detection delay, indicating sensitivity to the heavy-tailed Weibull post-change distribution and, for DDM, to the cold-start behavior associated with online error-rate estimation. The proposed Gamma–Weibull CUSUM detector provides a balanced compromise between these characteristics, achieving a relatively short detection delay while maintaining a very low false-alarm rate comparable to that of the Page–Hinkley test. This behavior is consistent with the use of the Gamma–Weibull log-likelihood ratio within the CUSUM framework, where the decision threshold is calibrated using the target ARL\(_0\), rather than relying solely on window-based statistical tests that do not explicitly model the underlying data distribution.

6.8 Pseudo-label quality and sensitivity analysis

Because the adaptation procedure relies on automatically generated labels rather than manual annotation, the quality of the resulting pseudo-labels and their dependence on the confidence threshold \(\tau \), introduced in Section 4, are evaluated. The threshold \(\tau \) is varied over the range [0.50, 0.99] using the post-alarm buffer from the abrupt-drift scenario. Taking advantage of the known buffer labels available in the simulation, two performance measures are computed: (i) pseudo-label accuracy, defined as the agreement between the automatically assigned labels and the true labels for high-confidence samples, and (ii) coverage, defined as the proportion of buffer samples assigned pseudo-labels directly rather than through clustering. Figure 15 illustrates the resulting trade-off between these two measures.

At the default operating threshold of \(\tau =0.80\), pseudo-label accuracy is 1.000, with a coverage of 0.863. This indicates that \(86.3\%\) of the buffered samples receive pseudo-labels directly from the pre-change classifier with high confidence, while the remaining \(13.7\%\) are assigned through K-means clustering. As \(\tau \) increases beyond 0.80, pseudo-label accuracy for the retained high-confidence samples remains at or close to 1.000, whereas coverage decreases because fewer samples satisfy the more stringent confidence criterion and a larger proportion is assigned through clustering. Conversely, reducing \(\tau \) increases coverage, although with a modest reduction in pseudo-label accuracy, as a small number of lower-confidence predictions are accepted directly. These results illustrate the expected trade-off between label accuracy and coverage, indicating that the confidence threshold provides a transparent means of balancing the proportion of directly pseudo-labeled samples against the reliability of those labels. Under the evaluated conditions, \(\tau =0.80\) provides a practical compromise, maintaining high pseudo-label accuracy while allowing most buffered samples to be labeled directly without requiring clustering.

6.9 Ablation study

To examine the sensitivity of the proposed framework to its main hyperparameters and design choices, a systematic ablation study is conducted in which each parameter is varied independently while all remaining parameters are held at their default values (Table 8). The reported results are averaged over 5–10 independent replications. Table 9 summarizes the results for each ablation experiment, while the complete results for the CUSUM threshold ablation, expressed in terms of the target ARL\(_0\), are presented in Fig. 16.

The buffer-size and confidence-threshold ablation studies indicate that the adaptation procedure is relatively stable across a broad range of parameter settings. The adaptive LSTM macro F1 score varies by less than one percentage point for buffer sizes ranging from 50 to 500 sequences and for confidence thresholds between 0.50 and 0.95, indicating that the framework is not highly sensitive to these hyperparameters. In contrast, the number of adaptation epochs has a greater influence on performance, with the macro F1 score increasing from 0.965 after one epoch to 0.983 after ten epochs. These results could suggest that a relatively small number of fine-tuning iterations, such as the default value of five epochs, captures much of the observed improvement while maintaining a computationally efficient adaptation process. The clustering ablation shows a small difference between K-means and DBSCAN (0.973 vs. 0.965), supporting the use of K-means as the default approach for assigning labels to low-confidence samples.

The ARL\(_0\) ablation, summarized in Fig. 16, illustrates the statistical trade-off associated with the CUSUM threshold. Smaller ARL\(_0\) targets produce a lower decision threshold h and, consequently, a higher number of false alarms. In this ablation, the detection-delay metric is computed from the first alarm overall, rather than from the first post-change alarm used elsewhere in this study. As a result, the reported detection delay becomes increasingly negative at lower ARL\(_0\) values because false alarms occur before the true change point, rather than because the detector identifies the change earlier. As the ARL\(_0\) target increases toward 10, 000, the false-alarm count decreases to zero, and the detection delay approaches a small positive value. This behavior is consistent with the operating point corresponding to ARL\(_0=2000\), which is used as the default throughout this study. These results indicate that the ARL\(_0\)-based threshold-selection rule in (10) provides a practical way to balance detection sensitivity against false-alarm frequency in sequential drift detection.

6.10 Statistical significance across repeated trials

To evaluate whether the performance improvements associated with drift-adaptive fine-tuning are consistent across different stream realizations, the abrupt and gradual drift experiments are repeated over \(MC=30\) independent Monte Carlo replications, each using an independently generated data stream and independently initialized models. For each replication, the full-stream accuracy and macro F1 score are recorded for the baseline and drift-adaptive LSTM and CNN models. A one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test is then applied to the paired (baseline, adaptive) results to evaluate the null hypothesis that drift-adaptive fine-tuning does not improve performance. Table 10 summarizes the corresponding mean values, standard deviations, and p-values.

In both scenarios, the drift-adaptive LSTM and CNN models achieve higher accuracy and macro F1 scores than their non-adaptive counterparts, with all one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test p-values below the conventional significance level of 0.05 (the largest being \(p=5.7\times 10^{-5}\)). The largest improvement is observed for the LSTM macro F1 score, which increases by approximately two percentage points in both scenarios (\(p<3\times 10^{-7}\)). Although the CNN exhibits substantially lower absolute macro F1 scores due to severe class imbalance, it also shows a consistent improvement after adaptation. The standard deviations reported in Table 10 indicate that the variability associated with stream resampling and model reinitialization is small relative to the observed performance differences. These results show that the improvements associated with the proposed drift-adaptive framework are not limited to the particular stream realization considered in Sections 6.A–6.B and are observed consistently across repeated Monte Carlo replications.

6.11 Computational complexity and runtime profile

To assess the suitability of the proposed framework for resource-constrained IoT and edge deployments, we measure the runtime of each major pipeline stage on a standard CPU (no GPU acceleration), averaged over multiple repetitions. Table 11 reports the results.

The complete drift detection and adaptation cycle, from parameter estimation through CUSUM monitoring of the entire stream to buffer-based fine-tuning, completes in approximately 1.4 seconds on standard CPU hardware, with the dominant cost (483.5 ms) attributable to the five-epoch adaptation fine-tuning step and a comparable one-time cost for initial baseline training. Critically, drift detection itself (parameter estimation plus the full-stream CUSUM recursion) requires under 100 ms, and post-alarm pseudo-labeling of the buffer requires only 2.4 ms, confirming that the monitoring component of the framework imposes negligible overhead relative to typical IoT sampling intervals. Because adaptation updates only the final classifier layers while keeping the LSTM/CNN backbone frozen, the resulting parameter and memory footprint is small, supporting the framework’s suitability for deployment on resource-constrained edge devices, a property further examined using real malware traffic in Section 7.3.3.

7 Real-life applications

This section presents the numerical applications of the proposed framework using the IoT-23 benign and malware-infected datasets (Garcia et al. 2020; Amin et al. 2023).

7.1 Application to benign data

To construct a reliable pre-change model for the drift-detection methodology, we first analyze benign traffic from the IoT-23 dataset. IoT-23 contains labeled captures of Internet of Things (IoT) network activity, each providing Zeek-formatted flow logs (conn.log.labeled) with fields such as timestamps, protocol information, message sizes, and connection durations. Because the change-point formulation assumes an initial malware-free period, only benign scenarios are used when modeling the pre-change distribution \(p_{0}(x)\).

Among the available benign captures, CTU-Honeypot-Capture-4-1 is selected for its long recording period and stable device behavior, which yield sufficient samples for statistical estimation. The dataset is automatically retrieved and parsed through a Python extraction procedure. From this capture, the duration field is isolated and filtered, producing 451 positive observations that form the benign data stream \(\{X_t\}\). Connection duration is a natural modeling choice because it is non-negative, continuous, and known to shift under anomalous or malicious activity.

To parameterize \(p_0(x)\), the Gamma and Weibull families are fitted to the benign durations using method-of-moments initialization followed by MLE (see Table 13). Between these two parametric families, model-selection criteria favor the Weibull model, with a considerably higher log-likelihood, lower AIC, and lower BIC than the Gamma fit, indicating that it provides a comparatively better approximation to the heavy-tailed benign duration behavior within the Gamma–Weibull modeling family adopted for the CUSUM statistic. The KS statistics indicate that neither two-parameter model achieves an exact fit (KS \(=0.5165\) for Gamma and 0.2766 for Weibull), reflecting the extreme variability of IoT benign traffic durations, which span nearly five orders of magnitude (from \(4.79\times 10^{-4}\) to \(7.88\times 10^{4}\) seconds).

To assess whether the constrained two-parameter Gamma–Weibull family represents the best available description of this heavy-tailed behavior, we additionally fit the Lognormal and Pareto distributions to the same benign duration sample and compare all four candidates using log-likelihood, AIC, BIC, the KS statistic with p-value, and the AD statistic. Table 12 reports the results, and Fig. 17 visualizes the corresponding AIC comparison.

Among the four candidate distributions, the Lognormal model achieves the lowest AIC and BIC values, as well as the smallest KS and AD statistics, suggesting that it provides the closest overall fit to the empirical benign duration distribution. This observation is consistent with previous studies reporting that network connection durations often exhibit heavy-tailed behavior and are frequently modeled using Lognormal distributions. The Weibull distribution remains the preferred choice among the two-parameter families considered for the CUSUM formulation and is therefore retained as the operational pre-change model \(f_0(x)\) throughout the remainder of this study for two reasons. First, the Gamma–Weibull pairing introduced in Section III is selected because the resulting log-likelihood ratio in (6) has a closed-form expression that can be evaluated efficiently in an online setting, a property that is not shared by the Lognormal and Pareto models considered here for comparison. Second, although the Weibull distribution has higher AIC and BIC values than the Lognormal model, its KS statistic suggests that it still provides a satisfactory approximation of both the central region and the right tail of the benign distribution. The comparison presented in Table 12 is included to illustrate the characteristics of the two-parameter Gamma–Weibull approximation and to motivate future extensions based on Lognormal or semi-parametric drift statistics, which are discussed further in Section 8.

In addition to the statistical modeling, benign-only temporal baselines are constructed using univariate LSTM and CNN models. Applying a sliding window of length \(L=20\) to the benign duration stream produces 432 overlapping sequences. A threshold corresponding to the 90th percentile of benign durations is used to identify relatively infrequent high-duration events, thereby producing the class imbalance typically observed in benign IoT traffic. The objective of these models is not to perform classification analysis, but to learn the temporal patterns of benign flows. A reduction in predictive performance after the introduction of malware traffic is then used as an additional indicator of distributional drift.

The evaluation results for both models are summarized in Table 14. The LSTM baseline achieves higher overall performance than the CNN baseline, with an accuracy of 0.915 and a weighted F1-score of 0.905, indicating that it captures the temporal structure of benign traffic well. The CNN baseline achieves an accuracy of 0.815 and a weighted F1-score of 0.841, indicating comparatively lower performance in modeling temporal dependencies using convolutional representations. The macro F1-scores of both models (LSTM: 0.688, CNN: 0.613) are lower than their corresponding weighted F1-scores, reflecting the greater difficulty of identifying the relatively infrequent high-duration events present in the benign data.

Thus, the benign IoT duration data exhibit heavy-tailed behavior. Among the four candidate distributions considered, the Lognormal distribution provides the closest overall statistical fit, whereas the Weibull distribution remains the preferred member of the analytically tractable Gamma–Weibull family adopted for CUSUM monitoring. The LSTM and CNN baseline models also learn the temporal characteristics of benign traffic to varying degrees and serve as neural reference models for subsequent drift analysis. Together, the statistical and neural baselines define the pre-change system for assessing malware-induced distributional drift in the following section.

7.2 Application to malware data

The proposed drift-adaptive framework is next evaluated using real malware traffic from the IoT-23 dataset, specifically the CTU-IoT-Malware-Capture-33-1 scenario. This capture contains Zeek-formatted network flow logs (conn.log.labeled) collected from IoT devices during a malware infection, providing a realistic, non-stationary environment in which traffic characteristics evolve due to malicious activity and changing device behavior. Consistent with the statistical assumptions underlying the Gamma–Weibull detector, the duration feature (the \(\{X_t\}\) observation) is selected as the primary streaming variable because it is non-negative, highly skewed, and heavy-tailed.

Connection durations are extracted directly from the conn.log.labeled file and restricted to malicious flows, with benign and background entries excluded. To improve computational efficiency while preserving the statistical characteristics of the heavy-tailed stream, reservoir sampling is applied with a fixed random seed, yielding 14, 784 duration samples. The sampled stream exhibits a pronounced heavy-tailed distribution, with a minimum value of \(3\times 10^{-6}\), a maximum value of 6278.8483, a mean of 4.9288, and a variance of 9072.27. The fixed-seed reservoir sample used in the present analysis replaces the sample used in the original submission, whose summary statistics (mean 5.0416 and variance 11062.30) differed slightly because reservoir sampling had not yet been performed with a fixed random seed. As discussed below, this refinement does not affect the overall conclusions of the statistical analysis. The wide dynamic range of the sampled durations, spanning nearly nine orders of magnitude between the minimum and maximum values, is also consistent with the presence of both very short and exceptionally long connection durations commonly observed in malware-related IoT traffic.

Gamma and Weibull distributions are fitted to the sampled malware stream using method-of-moments initialization followed by MLE. The resulting parameter estimates define the log-likelihood ratio used by the CUSUM-based detector to identify deviations from the reference distribution during online monitoring. Because real malware captures do not provide annotated change points, detector alarms are interpreted as data-driven indications of departures from the estimated in-control behavior of the observed malware stream rather than detections of a known transition time.

To examine the effect of these distributional changes on learning performance, the drift detector is integrated with univariate LSTM and CNN models trained on sliding windows of length \(L=20\) constructed from the duration stream. The baseline models operate without adaptation, whereas the drift-adaptive variants fine-tune only the lightweight classifier components using a buffered set of post-alarm samples over a small number of training epochs, consistent with online and edge-computing constraints. Consequently, some differences from the controlled simulation results are expected, as real malware streams exhibit overlapping behavioral systems, measurement noise, and gradual temporal variations rather than a single, explicitly defined distributional change.

7.2.1 Goodness-of-fit against alternative distributions

Following the analysis in Section 6.1.1, the statistical characterization of the malware duration stream is extended by fitting Lognormal and Pareto distributions in addition to the Gamma and Weibull models. The four candidate distributions are then compared using the log-likelihood, AIC, BIC, KS statistic, and AD statistic. The corresponding results are presented in Table 16.

Consistent with the benign-data analysis in Section 7.1, the Lognormal distribution yields the lowest AIC, BIC, KS statistic, and AD statistic among the four candidate models fitted to the malware duration stream, suggesting that it provides the closest overall statistical fit to this highly heavy-tailed dataset. Within the two-parameter Gamma–Weibull family used to derive the analytically tractable CUSUM log-likelihood ratio, the Weibull distribution provides a better fit than the Gamma distribution, as reflected by its higher log-likelihood (\(-12640.24\) vs. \(-18673.87\)), lower AIC and BIC values, and smaller KS statistic (0.1633 vs. 0.2820). A similar pattern is observed in both the benign and malware IoT-23 datasets: although the Lognormal distribution provides the best overall fit among the four candidate models, the Weibull distribution remains the better-fitting member of the Gamma–Weibull pair. The same behavior is also observed in the controlled simulation presented in Section 6.1.1, where the generating distributions are known. Together, these results indicate that IoT connection-duration data may exhibit heavier tails than can be fully represented by either the Gamma or Weibull distribution alone. Nevertheless, within the Gamma–Weibull framework adopted for its closed-form and computationally efficient log-likelihood ratio, the Weibull distribution provides the more suitable model for the post-change distribution and, in the malware-only stream, for the overall duration distribution. Accordingly, the CUSUM formulation presented in Section 4 continues to use the Gamma–Weibull pairing as its operational parametric model.

7.3 Malware drift detection and model adaptation results

The fitted statistical models for the malware duration stream are summarized in Table 15, while the extended goodness-of-fit comparison is presented in Table 16. As discussed in Section 7.2.1, the Lognormal distribution provides the closest overall fit among the four candidate models. Within the analytically tractable Gamma–Weibull framework adopted for the CUSUM log-likelihood ratio, however, the Weibull distribution provides a better fit than the Gamma distribution, with a higher log-likelihood (\(-12640.24\) vs. \(-18673.87\)), lower AIC and BIC values, and a smaller KS statistic (0.1633 vs. 0.2820). These results support the continued use of the Weibull distribution as the post-change (or, for the malware-only stream, overall) model within the operational Gamma–Weibull framework.

Using the fitted Gamma–Weibull model, the resulting LLR sequence has a positive mean (\(\hat{\mu }=0.4081\)) and a standard deviation of 3.348, indicating that a sufficient proportion of malware observations differ from the fitted in-control Gamma model to produce an upward trend in the cumulative statistic. With the CUSUM threshold set to \(h=6.706\), calibrated using the ARL\(_0\approx 2000\) approximation in (10), the detector raises its first alarm at time index \(t=31\). Because the stream contains only malicious traffic, the CUSUM statistic continues to accumulate evidence after each reset, resulting in a total of 714 alarms over the full stream. These subsequent alarms reflect the persistent deviation of the malware-only traffic from the benign-derived in-control model rather than the single transition considered in the simulated experiments of Section 6. Since the IoT-23 malware capture does not provide annotated change-point locations, the false-alarm rate cannot be evaluated with respect to a known transition time. Accordingly, and following the definition in Section 5.2, we report that no additional alarm occurs before the initial alarm at \(t=31\). Each alarm is therefore interpreted as a statistical indication of departure from the fitted in-control Gamma–Weibull model rather than as the verified detection of a labeled drift event.

Figure 18 illustrates the evolution of the Gamma–Weibull CUSUM statistic over time, showing the repeated reset-and-reaccumulate pattern associated with a persistently malicious stream. The threshold (\(h=6.706\)) and first-alarm time (\(t=31\)) are used throughout the remainder of this analysis. To provide additional context, the Page–Hinkley test, ADWIN, KSWIN, and DDM are also applied to the same malware duration stream using the same benign-derived in-control statistics. Figure 24 compares the first-alarm times of the five detectors. The proposed Gamma–Weibull CUSUM raises its first alarm at \(t=31\), compared with \(t=7\) for ADWIN, \(t=75\) for the Page–Hinkley test, and \(t=251\) for KSWIN, whereas DDM does not raise an alarm under its default parameter settings. This behavior is similar to that observed in the simulation study described in Section 6.7, where ADWIN also detected changes earlier but produced a substantially larger number of false alarms. These observations suggest that detection speed alone may not provide a sufficient basis for comparing drift detectors in heavy-tailed, non-stationary malware streams.

Following drift detection, the malware duration stream is partitioned into sliding-window sequences of length \(L=20\), yielding 14, 765 sequences, including 13, 288 benign-like and 1, 477 attack-like. This class distribution reflects the characteristics of the captured malware. A post-alarm buffer containing \(b=300\) sequences (indices 12–311) is used for model adaptation. With the default confidence threshold of \(\tau =0.80\), pseudo-labeling assigns labels directly to 297 of the 300 buffered sequences (\(99.0\%\)), with a mean confidence of 0.984, while the remaining three sequences are assigned through K-means clustering. Figures 19 and 20 present the resulting accuracy of the LSTM and CNN models, respectively. The alarm marker shown in these figures corresponds to an earlier detector calibration with an alarm time near \(t\approx 6500\); however, the overall accuracy trends and the quantitative performance values reported in Table 17 correspond to the final analysis. For both architectures, the drift-adaptive models maintain accuracy comparable to or slightly higher than their baseline counterparts after the CUSUM alarm, suggesting that buffer-based fine-tuning helps preserve performance as malware behavior evolves.

The effect of post-alarm adaptation is further illustrated by the loss trajectories shown in Fig. 21. The LSTM exhibits a largely monotonic reduction in cross-entropy loss across successive adaptation epochs, whereas the CNN displays a less regular but generally decreasing loss profile, reflecting differences in model architecture and the limited size of the adaptation buffer. Both models reach stable loss values within five adaptation epochs, indicating that only a small number of fine-tuning iterations is required.

Aggregate classification performance is summarized in Table 17. For the LSTM, the drift-adaptive model achieves slightly higher accuracy, macro F1, and weighted F1 than the baseline model, with accuracy increasing from 0.9063 to 0.9075, macro F1 from 0.8130 to 0.8148, and weighted F1 from 0.9186 to 0.9196. For the CNN, adaptation produces a different pattern: accuracy decreases from 0.8953 to 0.8492, whereas macro F1 increases from 0.6347 to 0.6480. This result suggests that adaptation shifts the decision boundary toward a more balanced representation of the majority and minority classes, accompanied by a modest reduction in overall accuracy under the pronounced class imbalance present in this malware-dominated dataset.

To evaluate the consistency of these performance differences, baseline training and model adaptation are repeated using \(n=10\) independent random seeds. The resulting mean values, standard deviations, and 95% confidence intervals are reported, together with the results of a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test evaluating the hypothesis that adaptation improves performance (Table 18).

The LSTM shows higher performance after adaptation on accuracy and macro F1, with one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test p-values of 0.00195 for both measures. These results indicate that the observed improvements are consistent across repeated random initializations for the LSTM architecture on the evaluated malware dataset. For the CNN, the improvement in macro F1 is also associated with a p-value below the conventional significance level (\(p=0.0244\)), consistent with the balance between macro F1 and accuracy discussed above. In contrast, the difference in accuracy is not statistically significant (\(p=0.688\)) and is accompanied by greater variability across random seeds, suggesting that the CNN is more sensitive to buffer composition and model initialization under severe class imbalance.

Macro F1 scores are further illustrated in Fig. 23. The discriminative performance of the models is evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and precision-recall curves, shown in Fig. 22. For the LSTM, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) increases from 0.288 for the baseline model to 0.968 after adaptation. The CNN also exhibits an increase in AUC, from 0.505 to 0.564. These results suggest that drift-adaptive fine-tuning is associated with improved class discrimination, particularly for the LSTM, in addition to the changes observed in the point-wise classification metrics (Figs. 23). Figure 24 shows the drift-detector alarm-time comparison, not classification/ROC metrics as in Fig. 23.

7.3.1 Pseudo-label quality on real malware data

Following the sensitivity analysis presented in Section 6.8, the pseudo-label confidence-threshold sweep is repeated on the real malware adaptation buffer using the pre-change LSTM classifier. Across the range \(\tau \in [0.50,0.99]\), pseudo-label accuracy remains high, and the observed trade-off between accuracy and coverage is similar to that obtained in the simulation study. These results are consistent with the choice of \(\tau =0.80\) as the default operating threshold listed in Table 8. At this threshold, \(99.0\%\) of the buffered samples are assigned pseudo-labels with high confidence (mean confidence 0.984), indicating that the pre-change classifier provides reliable predictions for most benign-like and attack-like sequence samples.

The effect of pseudo-label noise is also examined by introducing controlled label corruption into the adaptation buffer before fine-tuning. Table 19 summarizes the resulting LSTM macro F1 scores as the corruption rate increases from \(0\%\) to \(30\%\), together with a comparison of K-means and DBSCAN for assigning labels to low-confidence samples.

Macro F1 decreases gradually as the level of label corruption increases, declining by only 0.0114 (from 0.8149 to 0.8035) even at a \(30\%\) corruption rate. These results show that the adaptation procedure is relatively insensitive to moderate levels of pseudo-label noise. The choice between K-means and DBSCAN for assigning labels to low-confidence samples has little effect on performance on this dataset, with both methods yielding a macro F1 score of 0.8148. This differs from the small advantage observed for K-means in the simulation study (Section 6.9) and is consistent with the fact that fewer than \(1\%\) of the buffered samples require clustering because of the high pseudo-label coverage obtained at \(\tau =0.80\).

7.3.2 Ablation study on real data

To examine whether the hyperparameter sensitivity observed in the simulation study (Section 6.9) is also reflected in the real malware dataset, the buffer-size, confidence-threshold, ARL\(_0\), and adaptation-epoch ablation studies are repeated on the malware stream, with each configuration averaged over five independent replications. Across buffer sizes ranging from 50 to 500, confidence thresholds between 0.50 and 0.95, and ARL\(_0\) targets from 500 to 5000, the adaptive LSTM macro F1 score varies only slightly, remaining between 0.8150 and 0.8152. This limited variation implies that the framework is not highly sensitive to these hyperparameter settings on the evaluated malware dataset. Increasing the number of adaptation epochs from one to ten produces a small, nearly monotonic increase in macro F1, from 0.8142 to 0.8154. This behavior is similar to that observed in the simulation study and is consistent with selecting five adaptation epochs as the default setting, providing a practical compromise between adaptation performance and computational cost.

7.3.3 Computational complexity and edge deployment suitability

Table 20 summarizes the runtime of the main stages of the proposed pipeline on the malware stream (CPU only), together with the model parameter counts and memory footprint. This analysis extends the runtime evaluation presented in Section 6.11 from the simulation study to the real-world malware dataset.

The complete online monitoring stage of the proposed framework, including parameter estimation, LLR computation, and the full-stream CUSUM recursion, requires approximately 133 ms on the malware stream when executed on a CPU. Pseudo-labeling of the post-alarm buffer requires an additional 35 ms. Because adaptation updates only the 66-parameter classifier head while the LSTM and CNN feature extractors remain frozen, the combined model and buffer memory footprint remains below 50 KB. This memory requirement is compatible with the resources available on representative IoT edge platforms, such as a Raspberry Pi 4B and similar embedded devices. These measurements are consistent with the runtime characteristics observed in the simulation study (Section 6.11) and indicate that the proposed framework can be deployed in resource-constrained, low-latency environments.

7.3.4 Validation on a second malware family

To examine whether the statistical characterization presented above is specific to the malware scenario considered, the same goodness-of-fit analysis is applied to a second, independent IoT-23 malware capture (CTU-IoT-Malware-Capture-1-1) using a reservoir sample of 5000 duration observations. Unlike CTU-IoT-Malware-Capture-33-1, the Gamma distribution achieves the lowest AIC (21, 058.79) for this malware family, slightly outperforming the Weibull model (21, 224.50). Both models provide substantially better AIC values than the Pareto distribution, although none of the four candidate distributions achieves a close fit according to the KS statistic (KS \(\approx 0.49\)–0.50 for all four models). These findings indicate that the relative performance of the candidate distributions varies across malware families and depends on the underlying traffic characteristics of each dataset. They also highlight a limitation of the present evaluation, namely that the parametric model supporting the Gamma–Weibull CUSUM framework may not be equally suitable for all malware families. This observation motivates future investigation of adaptive model selection or non-parametric extensions of the proposed detector, as discussed in Section 8.

The experimental results indicate that the proposed Gamma–Weibull CUSUM detector provides a statistically motivated approach for identifying departures from the reference behavior in real malware traffic. When combined with lightweight LSTM and CNN adaptation, the framework is associated with consistent improvements for the LSTM model in accuracy and F1-based metrics, while the CNN exhibits an increase in macro F1, accompanied by a reduction in overall accuracy. The additional experiments also indicate limited sensitivity to moderate levels of pseudo-label noise, relatively stable performance across a broad range of hyperparameter settings, and a computational footprint compatible with edge-oriented deployment. At the same time, the evaluation highlights several limitations, including severe class imbalance, the absence of annotated change points in the real malware data, and variation in the distribution that provides the best statistical fit across malware families. These considerations imply that, while the proposed framework performs well under the evaluated conditions, additional validation across a broader range of malware datasets and distributional settings would further strengthen its general applicability.

8 Conclusion

This study presented a statistically motivated drift-adaptive malware detection framework for non-stationary IoT data streams, combining a parametric Gamma–Weibull CUSUM detector with a lightweight LSTM/CNN adaptation stage that fine-tunes only selected classifier components using automatically generated pseudo-labels and cluster-based labels, eliminating the need for manual annotation while remaining compatible with resource-constrained edge environments.

In the simulation study, the detector identified the change point with a six-sample delay and no false alarms in the primary abrupt-drift scenario, with similarly small delays and no false alarms across the distribution-mismatch, gradual-drift, and mixed-distribution settings. The noisy-environment scenario exposed a limitation of the current threshold-calibration procedure, as additive observation noise produced premature alarms, indicating that calibration should account for the expected noise characteristics of the deployment environment. The recurring-drift experiment showed no evidence of catastrophic forgetting across two adaptation cycles. Comparisons with four established drift detectors indicated that the proposed method offers a competitive balance between detection delay and false-alarm frequency, and a 30-replication Monte Carlo study confirmed that the performance improvements from drift-adaptive learning were statistically consistent across repeated trials, with Wilcoxon signed-rank test p-values below the conventional significance level in all evaluated cases.

On the real IoT-23 dataset, an extended goodness-of-fit analysis indicated that the Lognormal distribution provides the closest overall fit to both duration streams, while the Weibull distribution remains the preferred member of the analytically tractable Gamma–Weibull framework. The detector raised its first malware-stream alarm at \(t=31\) and continued to produce alarms throughout the capture, consistent with persistently anomalous traffic rather than a single distributional transition. Drift-adaptive fine-tuning improved LSTM accuracy and macro F1 across repeated experiments, with a substantial increase in the area under the ROC curve from 0.288 to 0.968, while runtime and memory profiling confirmed an online monitoring time of less than 150 ms and a total model footprint below 50 KB, consistent with edge deployment.

8.1 Limitations and future work

Several limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgment. The Gamma–Weibull parametric pairing, while analytically tractable, does not achieve the closest unconditional fit to the IoT-23 duration streams, where the Lognormal distribution consistently provides a better fit; extending the detector to a Lognormal-based or semi-parametric log-likelihood ratio is therefore a natural direction for future work. The relative ranking of candidate distributions also varied across malware families, indicating that a family-adaptive or non-parametric extension may be needed for robust deployment in heterogeneous settings. Because IoT-23 captures do not provide ground-truth change-point timestamps, the reported real-data alarm statistics should be interpreted as indicators of statistically significant deviation from the fitted in-control model rather than as verified detections of labeled drift events. The noisy-environment simulation further showed that the present threshold-calibration procedure is sensitive to additive observation noise, so practical deployment should calibrate the threshold directly from representative, noisy in-control data rather than from idealized noise-free samples. The current evaluation is also limited to single-feature streams and abrupt-to-persistent transitions; extending the framework to multivariate features and simultaneous drift types remains an open direction, as does the long-run stability of repeated buffer-based adaptation over many drift cycles and its potential interaction with techniques such as elastic weight consolidation.

Considered together with the limitations discussed above, the overall results demonstrate that coupling a principled parametric drift detector with selective deep-model adaptation yields a computationally efficient and, under the evaluated conditions, robust approach to distributional drift in malware detection. Heavy-tailed parametric modeling provides an interpretable mechanism for drift monitoring in cybersecurity streams, and drift-triggered, buffer-based fine-tuning offers a practical, statistically validated pathway to continual learning without requiring continuous retraining or human labels, supporting the framework’s applicability as a deployable, drift-aware component for real-time IoT malware monitoring, subject to the scope and limitations outlined above.
