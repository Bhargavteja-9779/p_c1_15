Abstract

Empirical Bernstein bounds provide a variance-dependent view of generalization by relating the expected loss to the empirical risk and the empirical variance of the loss distribution. From this perspective, variance-penalized SVMs control both the empirical mean and variance of the loss, but under class imbalance and noisy observations, the original loss distribution may be dominated by majority-class samples and unreliable observations. To address this issue, a robust intuitionistic fuzzy variance-penalized SVM (VRIF-VPSVM) is proposed for imbalanced classification. VRIF-VPSVM first constructs a reliability- and imbalance-aware intuitionistic fuzzy membership from local distribution information, class imbalance, and sample unreliability. The resulting memberships reweight the hinge losses before their empirical mean, and the variance is evaluated, so that variance control is performed on a reliability-adjusted loss distribution rather than on the original loss distribution. An empirical Bernstein-type argument motivates variance control on the weighted losses. Theoretical analysis establishes key properties of the proposed membership and weighted objective, and a tailored optimization scheme is developed for the resulting problem. Experiments on artificial datasets and 22 benchmark imbalanced datasets, including feature-contaminated training settings, show that VRIF-VPSVM achieves the highest mean G-Mean and IBA among the compared methods on both clean and contaminated benchmarks, indicating favorable balance-oriented classification performance under class imbalance and noisy observations.

1 Introduction

Support vector machines (SVMs) provide a widely used margin-based framework for classification and have solid theoretical foundations Cortes and Vapnik (1995); Vapnik (1998). In the standard SVM formulation, the empirical loss is mainly controlled through its average value. However, the distribution of losses across training samples may also affect the learned decision function, particularly when the data are imbalanced or contain unreliable observations. Compared with Hoeffding-type bounds, whose widths mainly depend on the loss range, sample size, and confidence level Hoeffding (1963), empirical Bernstein-type bounds additionally involve the empirical loss variance and may become tighter when this variance is small Maurer and Pontil (2009). This observation motivates variance-penalized learning, in which the empirical mean and variance of the loss are controlled simultaneously. Related variance-dependent learning approaches have also been investigated from different perspectives, including moments-penalized risk-averse support vector classification Fu et al. (2022), variance-dependent risk-averse learning machines Fu et al. (2023b), and variance-regularized risk minimization Holland (2024).

Figure 1 schematically illustrates this variance-dependent motivation. The empirical Bernstein-type confidence width may become narrower when the empirical loss variance is sufficiently small and the sample size is sufficiently large. This observation is used only as a variance-dependent motivation for the proposed learning framework, rather than as a formal or uniformly tighter generalization guarantee for VRIF-VPSVM.

Although variance penalization controls loss fluctuation, directly applying it to the original hinge-loss distribution may be problematic when class imbalance and unreliable observations coexist. Majority-class samples may dominate both the empirical mean and variance, whereas minority-class samples may have insufficient influence on the learning objective. In addition, boundary noise, outliers, and mislabeled samples may generate large or unstable losses, causing the variance term to reflect unreliable observations rather than useful distributional information. It is therefore necessary to construct a more reliable loss distribution before variance penalization is applied.

Existing class-imbalance learning methods mainly address majority-class dominance through sampling, class-dependent penalties, margin adjustment, or sample reweighting. Sampling-based and cost-sensitive methods improve class-level balance through data redistribution or unequal penalties Alkhawaldeh et al. (2023); Elreedy and Atiya (2019); Fernández et al. (2018); van den Goorbergh et al. (2022); Wang et al. (2024); Zhou et al. (2024), but they may not adequately characterize sample reliability in noisy or locally sparse regions Song et al. (2023). Recent work has also explored neighborhood-based ensemble strategies for imbalanced classification, including methods that perturb sample and feature spaces, construct diverse feature representations, and combine neighborhood information with hybrid imbalance-handling strategies Jiang et al. (2025, 2026). These studies illustrate the usefulness of neighborhood information and ensemble diversification for addressing class imbalance from data- and feature-space perspectives. Fuzzy SVMs instead assign sample-dependent memberships to reduce the contribution of unreliable observations Lin and Wang (2002). Class-aware, entropy-based, relative-density-based, affinity-based, projection-based, and learning-behavior-based memberships have subsequently been developed for imbalanced or noisy classification Batuwita and Palade (2010); Fan et al. (2017); Hazarika et al. (2023); Tanveer et al. (2024, 2025); Yu et al. (2019).

Intuitionistic fuzzy sets further describe sample uncertainty through membership, non-membership, and hesitation degrees Atanassov (1986). Relative density-based intuitionistic fuzzy SVMs use local distribution information to evaluate sample reliability in imbalanced classification Fu et al. (2023a), while other intuitionistic fuzzy SVM variants have been developed for noisy learning scenarios Lou and Xie (2023). Nevertheless, most fuzzy and intuitionistic fuzzy SVMs use memberships primarily to weight the empirical mean loss. Conversely, existing variance-penalized SVM-type models generally evaluate the variance of the original loss distribution without first accounting for class imbalance and sample reliability.

Motivated by this gap, this paper proposes a robust intuitionistic fuzzy variance-penalized SVM, termed VRIF-VPSVM, for class-imbalanced and noisy classification. First, relative-density information is used to construct a sample reliability score. Second, class-wise scale correction, class-imbalance adjustment, and an unreliability-degree-based correction are combined to obtain the VRIF membership. Third, the hinge losses are weighted by the resulting memberships, and their empirical mean and variance are jointly controlled. The memberships are constructed before classifier optimization and remain fixed throughout the optimization process.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

  • A robust intuitionistic fuzzy membership is constructed by integrating relative-density-based reliability, class-wise scale correction, class-imbalance adjustment, and unreliability-degree-based correction, thereby incorporating sample reliability and class-imbalance information into the loss distribution.

  • A VRIF-weighted variance-penalized SVM framework is developed, in which the empirical mean and variance are evaluated on the reliability-adjusted weighted loss distribution. This establishes a sequential relationship between sample-level reliability adjustment and distribution-level variance control. A tailored optimization scheme is developed for the resulting variance-penalized problem.

  • The proposed formulation is theoretically analyzed in terms of membership validity, monotonicity, sensitivity, and sufficient convexity conditions, and is systematically evaluated on artificial, benchmark imbalanced, and noisy imbalanced datasets against representative sampling-based, cost-sensitive, fuzzy-weighted, and variance-penalized methods.

2 Related work

Variance-dependent learning extends empirical risk minimization by controlling both the average loss and its variation across training samples. Representative studies include moments-penalized risk-averse support vector classification, variance-dependent risk-averse learning machines, and variance-regularized risk minimization Fu et al. (2022, 2023b); Holland (2024). Variance-based regularization has also been studied from a distributionally robust learning perspective Duchi and Namkoong (2019). These methods provide a direct mechanism for controlling loss fluctuation. In representative variance-penalized SVM formulations, however, the empirical mean and variance are computed from the original sample losses before class imbalance and sample reliability are taken into account.

Class-imbalance learning methods mainly address majority-class dominance through data resampling, class-dependent penalties, margin adjustment, or sample reweighting. Sampling-based methods modify the training distribution through majority-class under-sampling or minority-class over-sampling Elreedy and Atiya (2019); Fernández et al. (2018); Wang et al. (2024); Zhou et al. (2024), whereas cost-sensitive methods assign different penalties to the two classes Alkhawaldeh et al. (2023); Song et al. (2023); van den Goorbergh et al. (2022). Related strategies include example reweighting, class-balanced objectives, label-distribution-aware margins, and logit adjustment Cao et al. (2019); Cui et al. (2019); Menon et al. (2021); Ren et al. (2018); Zhang et al. (2023). These methods improve class-level balance, but they do not always explicitly distinguish reliable samples from noisy, isolated, or ambiguous observations.

Fuzzy SVM introduces sample-dependent memberships to reduce the contribution of less reliable observations to the empirical loss Lin and Wang (2002). Class-aware and entropy-based memberships have been developed for imbalanced classification Batuwita and Palade (2010); Fan et al. (2017), while relative density, affinity information, class probability, projection information, and learning behavior have also been used to characterize sample reliability Hazarika et al. (2023); Tanveer et al. (2024, 2025); Yu et al. (2019). Intuitionistic fuzzy sets further describe uncertainty through membership, non-membership, and hesitation degrees Atanassov (1986). Based on this framework, RIFSVM uses local relative density to evaluate sample reliability in imbalanced classification Fu et al. (2023a), while other intuitionistic fuzzy SVM variants have been developed for noisy data Lou and Xie (2023). More recent studies have incorporated intuitionistic fuzzy scores and projection-based memberships into least-squares twin SVMs Tanveer et al. (2024), and have combined intuitionistic fuzzy twin SVMs with ensemble training Xie et al. (2026). These approaches improve the representation of sample reliability and class-imbalance information, but their objectives mainly focus on fuzzy-weighted classification or ensemble decision making rather than controlling the empirical variance of the weighted losses.

These studies address loss fluctuation, class imbalance, and sample reliability from complementary perspectives. However, limited attention has been paid to adjusting the loss distribution according to both sample reliability and class imbalance before its empirical mean and variance are evaluated. VRIF-VPSVM addresses this gap by constructing a reliability- and imbalance-adjusted loss distribution prior to variance penalization.

3 Preliminaries

This section presents the basic formulations of SVM, variance-penalized SVM, and intuitionistic fuzzy weighting, which provide the foundation for the proposed VRIF-VPSVM. For notational convenience and consistency with the variance-penalized formulations used below, SVM-type models for learning a homogeneous hyperplane are considered throughout this paper; that is, the explicit bias term is omitted. This bias-free formulation is commonly adopted in related variance-penalized SVM models, since SVMs without an offset term have been shown to have comparable convergence rates and classification performance to SVMs with an offset, while providing more flexibility in algorithm design Steinwart et al. (2011).

3.1 SVM and kernel representation

Let \({\mathcal D }=\{({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i},{y}_{i}),\,i=1,\ldots ,n\}\) be a binary training set, where \({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{d}\) is the input vector and yi ∈ { + 1, − 1} is the class label. Under the bias-free formulation, the decision function in a feature space is written as

$$f({{\boldsymbol{x}}})={{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}^{T}\phi ({{\boldsymbol{x}}}),$$
(1)

where ϕ( ⋅ ) maps the input data into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. The corresponding soft-margin SVM can be formulated in the regularized empirical-risk form as

$${\min }_{{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}\ \frac{1}{2}\parallel {{\boldsymbol{w}}}{\parallel }^{2}+\frac{\lambda }{n}{\sum}_{i=1}^{n}{(1-{y}_{i}f({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i}))}_{+},$$
(2)

where λ > 0 is a trade-off parameter and \({(u)}_{+}=\max \{u,0\}\).

By the representer theorem, the solution can be expressed as

$${{\boldsymbol{w}}}={\sum}_{j=1}^{n}{\alpha }_{j}\phi ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{j}).$$
(3)

Therefore, the decision function can be written in the kernel representation as

$$f({{\boldsymbol{x}}})={\sum}_{j=1}^{n}{\alpha }_{j}K({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{j},{{\boldsymbol{x}}}),$$
(4)

where \(K({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{j},{{\boldsymbol{x}}})=\phi {({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{j})}^{T}\phi ({{\boldsymbol{x}}})\) is the kernel function.

Let K be the kernel matrix with Kij = K(xi, xj), and let Ki denote the ith row of K. Then the kernel SVM objective can be written as

$${\min }_{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\ \frac{1}{2}{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{T}K{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}+\frac{\lambda }{n}{\sum}_{i=1}^{n}{(1-{y}_{i}{K}_{i}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})}_{+}.$$
(5)

For convenience, the kernel hinge loss of the ith sample is denoted by

$${\ell }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})={(1-{y}_{i}{K}_{i}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})}_{+}.$$
(6)

This notation will be used in the variance-penalized and weighted formulations below.

3.2 Variance-penalized SVM

Empirical Bernstein-type bounds motivate variance-dependent learning by relating the learning performance to both the empirical mean and empirical variance of the loss, Maurer and Pontil (2009). In this paper, the SVM-type risk-averse model with variance penalization is referred to as RASVM-VP. A representative RASVM-VP objective is formulated as

$${\min }_{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\ \frac{1}{2}{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{T}K{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}+{\lambda }_{1}\hat{{{ \mathcal R }}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})+\gamma \sqrt{\frac{{V}_{n}(\ell )}{n}},$$
(7)

where λ1 > 0 and γ ≥ 0 control the empirical mean term and the square-root variance term, respectively.

The empirical mean loss is defined as

$$\hat{{{ \mathcal R }}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})=\frac{1}{n}{\sum}_{i=1}^{n}{\ell }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}),$$
(8)

and the empirical loss variance is given by

$${V}_{n}(\ell )=\dfrac{1}{n-1}{\sum }_{i=1}^{n}{\left({\ell }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})-\widehat{{{\mathcal{R}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})\right)}^{2}.$$
(9)

When γ = 0, (7) reduces to a regularized kernel SVM objective based on the empirical mean hinge loss. When γ > 0, the square-root empirical variance term is additionally penalized.

3.3 Fuzzy and intuitionistic fuzzy weighting

In class-imbalance learning, the minority class is denoted by  + 1 and the majority class by  − 1. Let

$${{{ \mathcal I }}}_{+}=\{i:{y}_{i}=+1\},\qquad {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{-}=\{i:{y}_{i}=-1\}$$

be the index sets of the minority and majority classes, with cardinalities n+ and n−, respectively. The imbalance factor used in this paper is defined as

$$IR=\frac{{n}_{+}}{{n}_{-}},$$
(10)

where n+ ≤ n−.

Fuzzy SVM assigns a membership value si ∈ [0, 1] to each training sample and incorporates it into the empirical loss term Lin and Wang (2002). Under the kernel representation, a generic fuzzy weighted SVM is formulated as

$${\min }_{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\ \frac{1}{2}{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{T}K{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}+\frac{\lambda }{n}{\sum}_{i=1}^{n}{s}_{i}{\ell }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}).$$
(11)

Samples with smaller memberships therefore have less influence on the empirical loss.

An intuitionistic fuzzy description of the ith sample is characterized by a membership degree μi, a non-membership degree νi, and a hesitation degree πiAtanassov (1986), satisfying

$$0\le {\mu }_{i}\le 1,\qquad 0\le {\nu }_{i}\le 1,\qquad 0\le {\mu }_{i}+{\nu }_{i}\le 1,$$
(12)

and

$${\pi }_{i}=1-{\mu }_{i}-{\nu }_{i}.$$
(13)

These quantities provide the basic uncertainty representation used in the construction of the proposed intuitionistic fuzzy membership.

4 The proposed VRIF-VPSVM method

This section presents the proposed robust intuitionistic fuzzy variance-penalized support vector machine, termed VRIF-VPSVM. The method assigns a reliability- and imbalance-aware membership to each training sample and uses the resulting memberships to adjust the hinge-loss distribution before variance penalization. The motivating analysis is presented first, followed by the VRIF-VPSVM framework, the construction of the VRIF membership, and the formulation and optimization of VRIF-VPSVM.

4.1 Motivation

As described in Section 3.2, RASVM-VP controls both the empirical mean and empirical variance of the hinge loss. However, these two quantities are computed directly from the original loss distribution. When class imbalance and unreliable observations coexist, the empirical loss distribution may be dominated by majority-class samples, while noisy or atypical samples may produce unusually large losses. Consequently, such samples can affect not only the empirical mean but also the empirical variance in the variance-penalized objective.

To illustrate this limitation, a two-dimensional artificial binary classification problem is considered. The class-conditional distributions are defined as

$${{\boldsymbol{x}}}| Y=c\sim{{ \mathcal N }}({{{\boldsymbol{\mu }}}}_{c},{\rm\Sigma }),\qquad c\in \{1,2\},$$
(14)

where

$${{{\boldsymbol{\mu }}}}_{1}={(-1.2,-1.2)}^{T},\qquad {{{\boldsymbol{\mu }}}}_{2}={(1.2,1.2)}^{T},\qquad \Sigma =\left[\begin{array}{cc}0.1&0\\ 0&0.1\end{array}\right].$$
(15)

The Bayes classifier associated with the generating distribution is used as the reference decision boundary.

Four training settings are generated from the same underlying distribution. Figure 2(a) presents the clean balanced setting, in which neither class imbalance nor label noise is introduced. Figure 2(b) presents the class-imbalanced setting, obtained by undersampling the minority class from the clean training data. The resulting training set contains 31 positive samples and 94 negative samples, corresponding to an imbalance ratio of approximately 1: 3.03. Figure 2(c) presents the noisy setting, in which a proportion of the training labels is changed. Figure 2(d) presents the noisy class-imbalanced setting. In this case, label noise is first introduced, after which class imbalance is imposed according to the resulting training labels. The final training set contains 35 positive-labeled samples and 105 negative-labeled samples, with an effective imbalance ratio of approximately 1: 3.00. The test set remains clean so that the learned decision boundaries can be evaluated with respect to the underlying data distribution.

As shown in Fig. 2, RASVM-VP generally produces a more favorable decision boundary than the standard SVM, supporting the usefulness of variance penalization. Nevertheless, its performance still deteriorates when class imbalance and noisy observations occur simultaneously. In particular, the G-Mean of RASVM-VP decreases from 97.06% in the clean balanced setting to 90.35% in the noisy class-imbalanced setting. This result suggests that directly penalizing the variance of the original hinge-loss distribution does not fully account for majority-class dominance or differences in sample reliability. The loss distribution should therefore be adjusted according to sample reliability and class imbalance before its empirical mean and variance are evaluated.

4.2 VRIF-VPSVM framework

Motivated by the preceding analysis, VRIF-VPSVM adjusts the hinge-loss distribution according to sample reliability and class imbalance before variance penalization is applied. Let \({s}_{i}^{{\mbox{VRIF}}}\in [0,1]\) denote the VRIF membership assigned to the ith training sample. The membership is constructed from the training data before model optimization, and its detailed definition is presented in Section 4.3.

Using the hinge loss ℓi(α) defined in (6), the VRIF-weighted loss of the ith sample is defined as

$${\widetilde{\ell }}_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})={s}_{i}^{{{\rm{VRIF}}}}{\ell }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}),\qquad i=1,\ldots ,n.$$
(16)

Accordingly, the empirical mean of the weighted losses is

$${\widehat{{{\mathcal{R}}}}}_{{{\mbox{VRIF}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})=\dfrac{1}{n}{\sum }_{i=1}^{n}{\widetilde{\ell }}_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}),$$
(17)

and their empirical variance is

$${V}_{n}^{{{\rm{VRIF}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})=\dfrac{1}{n-1}{\sum }_{i=1}^{n}{\left({\widetilde{\ell }}_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}){\widehat{{{\mathcal{R}}}}}_{{{\rm{VRIF}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})\right)}^{2}.$$
(18)

Based on the weighted loss distribution, the VRIF-VPSVM objective is formulated as

$${\min }_{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{T}K{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}+{\lambda }_{1}{\hat{{{ \mathcal R }}}}_{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})+\gamma \sqrt{\frac{{V}_{n}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})}{n}},$$
(19)

where λ1 > 0 and γ ≥ 0 control the empirical mean term and the square-root variance term of the weighted losses, respectively. Unlike RASVM-VP, which evaluates these quantities from the original hinge losses, VRIF-VPSVM evaluates both terms after sample reliability and class imbalance have been incorporated through \({s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}\). Consequently, the losses of samples with smaller memberships are attenuated before both the empirical mean and empirical variance are evaluated.

The proposed framework contains three main stages. First, local relative-density information is used to evaluate the within-class support and between-class ambiguity of the training samples. Second, class-wise scale correction, class-imbalance adjustment, and an unreliability-degree-based correction are combined to construct the VRIF membership. Third, the resulting weighted loss distribution is incorporated into the variance-dependent objective in (19), and the corresponding optimization problem is solved.

When \({s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}=1\) for all i, the weighted loss distribution reduces to the original hinge-loss distribution, and (19) reduces to the RASVM-VP formulation. When γ = 0, the model reduces to a fuzzy weighted SVM-type formulation based only on the empirical mean of the VRIF-weighted losses. The construction of \({s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}\) and the optimization of (19) are presented in the following subsections.

4.3 Construction of the VRIF membership

This subsection presents the detailed construction of the VRIF membership \({s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}\) introduced in (16). The membership construction is guided by the following three objectives:

  • to evaluate local sample reliability using within-class neighborhood information and, for majority-class samples, proximity to the minority class;

  • to alleviate class imbalance by reducing the aggregate contribution of majority-class samples without further suppressing minority-class samples;

  • to reduce the influence of noisy, isolated, and ambiguous samples near class-overlapping regions.

4.3.1 Relative-density-based intuitionistic fuzzy reliability

The first stage constructs a base reliability score from local neighborhood information. It evaluates the support that a sample receives from its own class and, for a majority-class sample, its ambiguity with respect to the minority class.

Let \({{{ \mathcal I }}}_{+}\) and \({{{ \mathcal I }}}_{-}\) denote the index sets of the minority and majority classes, respectively, with cardinalities n+ and n−. Assuming that each class contains at least two training samples, the neighborhood sizes are defined as

$${k}_{+}=\min \{\lceil \sqrt{{n}_{+}}\rceil ,{n}_{+}-1\},\qquad {k}_{-}=\min \{\lceil \sqrt{{n}_{-}}\rceil ,{n}_{-}-1\}.$$
(20)

For a training sample xi, define its k+-nearest minority-class distance and k−-nearest majority-class distance as

$${d}_{i}^{+}={{\mathrm{kth}}}_{{k}_{+}}\{\parallel {{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i}-{{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{j}{\parallel }_{2}:j\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{+},\,j\,\ne\, i\},$$
(21)

and

$${d}_{i}^{-}={{\mathrm{kth}}}_{{k}_{-}}\{\parallel {{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i}-{{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{j}{\parallel }_{2}:j\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{-},\,j\,\ne\, i\}.$$
(22)

Here, kthk( ⋅ ) denotes the kth smallest value in the corresponding distance set. For a minority-class sample, \({d}_{i}^{+}\) measures the distance to its k+th nearest minority-class neighbor. For a majority-class sample, \({d}_{i}^{-}\) measures the distance to its k−th nearest majority-class neighbor, whereas \({d}_{i}^{+}\) measures the distance to its k+th nearest minority-class neighbor.

Based on these distances, two local geometric characteristics are considered. The first is within-class local support. For a minority-class sample, \({d}_{i}^{+}\) characterizes the support provided by nearby minority-class samples, while for a majority-class sample, \({d}_{i}^{-}\) characterizes the support provided by nearby majority-class samples. A smaller within-class distance indicates that the sample lies in a denser region of its own class and should therefore receive a larger membership degree.

The second characteristic is the between-class ambiguity of majority-class samples. For a majority-class sample xi, the distance \({d}_{i}^{+}\) describes its proximity to the minority class. A smaller value of \({d}_{i}^{+}\) indicates stronger overlap or ambiguity with respect to the minority class and should therefore result in a larger non-membership degree.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the same-class and opposite-class neighborhoods play different roles in the reliability evaluation. The same-class neighborhood measures the local support for the observed class label, whereas the opposite-class neighborhood measures the ambiguity of a majority-class sample with respect to the minority class. These quantities are used below to define the membership and non-membership degrees.

For a minority-class sample xi, \(i\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{+}\), the membership degree is defined as

$$\mu ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i})=\frac{2}{1+\exp ({d}_{i}^{+}/({d}_{\max }^{+}+\varepsilon ))},\qquad i\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{+},$$
(23)

where

$${d}_{\max }^{+}={{\max }_{j\in {{{\mathcal{I}}}}_{+}}}{d}_{j}^{+}$$
(24)

is the maximum within-class neighborhood distance among the minority-class samples, and ε > 0 is a small constant introduced for numerical stability.

For a majority-class sample xi, \(i\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{-}\), the within-class membership degree is defined as

$$\mu ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i})=\frac{2}{1+\exp ({d}_{i}^{-}/({d}_{\max }^{-}+\varepsilon ))},\qquad i\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{-},$$
(25)

where

$${d}_{\max }^{-}={\max }_{j\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{-}}{d}_{j}^{-}$$
(26)

is the maximum within-class neighborhood distance among the majority-class samples.

To characterize the proximity of a majority-class sample to the minority class, the between-class ambiguity factor is defined as

$$\rho ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i})=\frac{2}{1+\exp ({d}_{i}^{+}/({d}_{\max }^{b}+\varepsilon ))},\qquad i\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{-},$$
(27)

where

$${d}_{\max }^{b}={\max }_{j\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{-}}{d}_{j}^{+}$$
(28)

is the maximum distance from a majority-class sample to its k+th nearest minority-class neighbor. A majority-class sample closer to the minority class obtains a larger value of ρ(xi), indicating stronger local ambiguity.

The non-membership degree of a majority-class sample is then defined as

$$\nu ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i})=\left(1-\mu ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i})\right)\rho ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i}),\qquad i\in {{{\mathcal{I}}}}_{-}.$$
(29)

Since 0 ≤ ρ(xi) ≤ 1, this definition ensures that μ(xi) + ν(xi) ≤ 1.

The hesitation-based reliability score of a majority-class sample is defined as

$$H({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i})=\left\{\begin{array}{cc}0, \hfill & \mu ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i}) < \nu ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i}),\\ \frac{1-\nu ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i})}{2-\mu ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i})-\nu ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i})+\varepsilon }, & \mu ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i})\ge \nu ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i}),\end{array}\right.\qquad i\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{-}.$$
(30)

Here, μ(xi) represents the local support for the observed majority-class label, ν(xi) represents the evidence against that label, and H(xi) gives the resulting reliability score.

Finally, the base reliability score is defined as

$${r}_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}{cc}\mu ({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i}), & i\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{+},\\ H({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{i}), & i\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{-}.\end{array}\right.$$
(31)

The membership degree is used directly for minority-class samples to avoid additional suppression of the minority class. For majority-class samples, the hesitation-based score is used to account for both within-class support and local ambiguity with respect to the minority class.

4.3.2 Class-wise scale correction and imbalance-aware IRIF membership

The base reliability score ri defined in (31) characterizes the local reliability of each training sample. However, the reliability scores of the minority and majority classes may have different numerical distributions. Moreover, even after sample-level reliability is considered, the majority class may still dominate the weighted loss distribution because it contains more samples. To address these issues, a class-wise scale correction is first applied to ri, followed by a class-imbalance adjustment.

For each class y ∈ { + 1, − 1}, the mean reliability is defined as

$${\overline{r}}_{y}=\frac{1}{{n}_{y}}{\sum}_{j:{y}_{j}=y}{r}_{j},$$
(32)

where ny denotes the number of training samples in class y. The class-wise scale-corrected reliability of the ith sample is then defined as

$${r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{scale}}}=\min \left\{\frac{{r}_{i}}{{\overline{r}}_{{y}_{i}}+\varepsilon },1\right\},$$
(33)

where ε > 0 is used to avoid numerical instability. This correction evaluates the reliability of each sample relative to the average reliability of its own class. Samples with relatively high reliability retain large values, whereas samples whose reliability is below the class average receive smaller values.

The original and scale-corrected reliability scores are combined as

$${r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}=(1-\tau ){r}_{i}+\tau {r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{scale}}},\qquad 0\le \tau \le 1,$$
(34)

where τ controls the contribution of the class-wise scale correction. When τ = 0, \({r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}\) reduces to the base reliability ri. As τ increases, the reliability of a sample relative to the reliability distribution of its own class receives greater emphasis.

To reduce the aggregate contribution of majority-class samples, the class factor is defined as

$${b}_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}{cc}1, & i\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{+},\\ IR, & i\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{-},\end{array}\right.$$
(35)

where the imbalance factor IR = n+/n− is defined in (10). Since 0 < IR ≤ 1, majority-class samples are assigned a smaller class factor under class imbalance, while minority-class samples are not additionally suppressed. When the two classes are balanced, IR = 1, and the class factor does not alter the reliability scores.

The imbalance-aware IRIF membership is finally defined as

$${s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}={b}_{i}{r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}.$$
(36)

Thus, \({s}_{i}^{{{\mbox{IRIF}}}}\) incorporates local sample reliability, class-wise reliability scale correction, and class-imbalance information. It serves as the base membership for the subsequent unreliability-degree-based robustness correction.

4.3.3 Unreliability-degree-based VRIF robustness correction

Although \({s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}\) incorporates local reliability, class-wise scale correction, and class-imbalance information, samples with relatively low reliability may still exert an undesirable influence on the weighted loss distribution. To further reduce the contribution of such samples, an unreliability-degree-based correction is introduced.

The unreliability degree of the ith sample is defined as

$${q}_{i}=1-{r}_{i}^{{{\mbox{IRIF}}}},$$
(37)

where \({r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}\) is given in (34). Since \(0\le {r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}\le 1\), it follows that 0≤qi ≤ 1. A sample with a large adjusted reliability has a small unreliability degree, whereas a sample with a small adjusted reliability has a large unreliability degree.

The corresponding robustness correction factor is defined as

$${\psi }_{i}=\frac{1}{1+\eta {q}_{i}},\qquad \eta \ge 0,$$
(38)

where η controls the strength of the robustness correction. For a fixed η, ψi decreases as qi increases. Consequently, samples with lower adjusted reliability receive stronger attenuation, whereas highly reliable samples retain correction factors close to one. When η = 0, ψi = 1 for all samples, and no additional robustness correction is applied.

The final VRIF membership is defined as

$$s_i^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}} = s_i^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}} \psi_{i} = \frac{{b_i} r_i^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}} {1+\eta \left ({1} r_i^{{\mbox{IRIF}}}\right)}.$$
(39)

Thus, the final membership combines the class-imbalance factor bi, the adjusted sample reliability \({s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}\), and the robustness correction factor ψi. A sample with high reliability retains a relatively large membership, whereas a sample with low reliability is further downweighted.

The unreliability degree is computed from \({r}_{i}^{{{\mbox{IRIF}}}}\) rather than from \({r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}\). This distinction prevents the class factor bi from being interpreted as sample unreliability. In particular, a majority-class sample is not regarded as unreliable merely because its class factor is smaller under class imbalance. Instead, bi controls the aggregate contribution of the majority class, while ψi adjusts the contribution of individual samples according to their reliability.

The resulting membership \({s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}\) is used in (16) to construct the reliability- and imbalance-adjusted loss distribution. Its boundedness and monotonicity properties are examined in the following subsection.

4.3.4 Properties of the VRIF membership

The following results establish the validity, class-wise aggregate control, monotonicity, and scalar sensitivity of the proposed VRIF membership.

Proposition 1

(Validity and class-wise aggregate control) Assume that ε > 0, 0 < IR ≤ 1, 0 ≤ τ ≤ 1, and η ≥ 0. Then

$$0\le {r}_{i}\le 1,\qquad 0\le {r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{scale}}}\le 1,\qquad 0\le {r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}\le 1,$$
(40)

and

$$0 \leq s_i^{{\mbox{VRIF}}} \leq s_i^{{\mbox{IRIF}}} \le b_i \le 1.$$
(41)

Furthermore, the class-wise aggregate memberships satisfy

$$\sum\limits_{i\in {{{\mathcal{I}}}}_{+}}{s}_{i}^{{{\mbox{VRIF}}}}\le {n}_{+},\qquad {\sum }_{i\in {{{\mathcal{I}}}}_{-}}{s}_{i}^{{{\mbox{VRIF}}}}\le {n}_{+}.$$
(42)

The bounds in (42) show that the majority class does not obtain a larger aggregate membership upper bound solely because it contains more training samples. These bounds do not imply that the realized aggregate memberships of the two classes are equal, because the sample reliabilities may differ within and between the classes.

Proposition 2

(Monotonicity and scalar sensitivity) For fixed bi ∈ (0, 1] and η ≥ 0, define

$${g}_{i}(r)=\frac{{b}_{i}r}{1+\eta (1-r)},\qquad r\in [0,1].$$
(43)

Then:

  1. 1.

    gi(r) is strictly increasing with respect to r;

  2. 2.

    for fixed r, gi(r) is non-increasing with respect to η;

  3. 3.

    gi is Lipschitz continuous on [0, 1] with Lipschitz constant Li = bi(1 + η).

In particular, for any ra, rb ∈ [0, 1],

$$| {g}_{i}({r}_{a})-{g}_{i}({r}_{b})| \le {b}_{i}(1+\eta )| {r}_{a}-{r}_{b}| \le (1+\eta )| {r}_{a}-{r}_{b}| .$$
(44)

Proposition 2 shows that a larger adjusted reliability produces a larger final membership, whereas increasing η strengthens the attenuation of samples whose adjusted reliability lies strictly between zero and one. The Lipschitz bound quantifies how perturbations in the adjusted reliability affect the final membership when bi and η are fixed.

The proofs of Propositions 1 and 2 are provided in Appendix A.

Remark 1

Several limiting cases clarify the roles of the membership components. When η = 0, the robustness correction is inactive and

$${s}_{i}^{{{\mbox{VRIF}}}}={s}_{i}^{{{\mbox{IRIF}}}}.$$

When IR = 1, the class factor satisfies bi = 1 for both classes, so no class-imbalance adjustment is applied. When τ = 0, the adjusted reliability reduces to the base reliability, whereas τ = 1 retains only the class-wise scale-corrected reliability.

At the endpoints of the adjusted reliability,

$${s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}=\left\{\begin{array}{cc}0, & {r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}=0,\\ {b}_{i}, & {r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}=1.\end{array}\right.$$
(45)

For any fixed \(0\le {r}_{i}^{{{\mbox{IRIF}}}} < 1\),

$${{\mbox{lim}}}_{{\eta \to \infty }}{s}_{i}^{{\mbox{VRIF}}}=0,$$

whereas a fully reliable sample with \({r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}\) retains the class-adjusted membership bi for every η ≥ 0.

For a majority-class sample, bi = IR. Therefore, for fixed \({r}_{i}^{{{\mbox{IRIF}}}}\) and η,

$${{\mbox{lim}}}_{IR\to 0}{s}_{i}^{{\mbox{VRIF}}}=0.$$

This behavior suppresses the sample-count-based contribution of the majority class under extreme imbalance, but may also attenuate informative majority-class samples when IR is very small.

The asymmetric class factors are deliberate: minority-class samples are not subjected to additional class-level attenuation, whereas the aggregate membership of the majority class is bounded by n+.

Remark 2

The Lipschitz property in (44) concerns only the scalar mapping from the adjusted reliability \({r}_{i}^{{\mathrm{IRIF}}}\) to the final membership \({s}_{i}^{{{\mbox{VRIF}}}}\) for fixed bi and η.

It does not establish global Lipschitz continuity of the complete mapping from the training data to the final memberships. In particular, the identities and ordering of the nearest neighbors may change under data perturbations. The piecewise definition of H(xi) may also change branches when μ(xi) = ν(xi). A stability result for the complete neighborhood-based construction would therefore require additional assumptions on neighborhood separation and the distance from the branch boundary.

4.4 VRIF-VPSVM formulation and optimization

The VRIF-VPSVM objective has been introduced in (19). Once the VRIF memberships have been constructed, they remain fixed throughout model training, and the optimization is performed only with respect to the coefficient vector α.

The square-root variance term in (19) reflects the empirical Bernstein-type motivation of the proposed model. Direct optimization of the resulting nonsmooth square-root formulation is difficult. A direct variance-penalized computational formulation is therefore derived through a constrained representation, after which a smooth Newton-type procedure is developed.

4.4.1 Direct variance-penalized computational formulation

Define

$${{ \mathcal G }}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})=\frac{1}{2}{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{T}K{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}+{\lambda }_{1}{\hat{{{ \mathcal R }}}}_{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}).$$
(46)

The square-root formulation in (19) can then be written as

$$\mathop{\min }_{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{n}}{{ \mathcal G }}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})+\gamma \sqrt{\frac{{V}_{n}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})}{n}}.$$
(47)

For a prescribed variance level B ≥ 0, consider the constrained problem

$$\begin{array}{c}{\min }_{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\quad {{ \mathcal G }}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})\\ {{\rm{s}}}.{{\rm{t}}}.\quad \sqrt{\frac{{V}_{n}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})}{n}}\le B.\end{array}$$
(48)

Let \({{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}_{\gamma }^{* }\) be a minimizer of (47), and set

$$B=\sqrt{\dfrac{{V}_{n}^{{{\mbox{VRIF}}}}({{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}_{\gamma }^{* })}{n}}.$$
(49)

Then \({{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}_{\gamma }^{* }\) is also a minimizer of (48). Indeed, the existence of a feasible coefficient vector with a smaller value of \({{\mathcal{G}}}\) would contradict the optimality of \({{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}_{\gamma }^{* }\) in (47).

Since \({V}_{n}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})\ge 0\), the constraint in (48) is equivalent to

$$\dfrac{{V}_{n}^{{{\mbox{VRIF}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})}{n}\le {B}^{2}.$$
(50)

Introducing a Lagrange multiplier λ2 ≥ 0 for this constraint gives

$${{ \mathcal G }}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})+{\lambda }_{2}\left[\frac{{V}_{n}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})}{n}-{B}^{2}\right].$$
(51)

Because  − λ2B2 is independent of α, it can be removed when minimizing with respect to α. The resulting computational formulation is

$${\min }_{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{T}K{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}+{\lambda }_{1}{\hat{{{ \mathcal R }}}}_{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})+\frac{{\lambda }_{2}}{n}{V}_{n}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}).$$
(52)

Under suitable constraint qualifications, the constrained formulation can be characterized through its Lagrangian form with a nonnegative multiplier λ2. In particular, under standard KKT regularity conditions, a local solution of the constrained problem satisfies the corresponding first-order optimality conditions for an appropriate λ2. After removing terms that are independent of the optimization variable, the resulting objective leads to Equation (52), which is adopted as the direct variance-penalized computational model used in the subsequent optimization and experiments.

4.4.2 Properties of the VRIF-weighted objective

This subsection examines how the fixed VRIF memberships affect the direct variance-penalized objective. Let

$${\ell }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})=\max \left\{1-{y}_{i}{K}_{i}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}},0\right\},\qquad {\widetilde{\ell }}_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})={s}_{i}^{{{\rm{VRIF}}}}{\ell }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}),$$
(53)

and define

$$\overline{\widetilde{\ell }}=\dfrac{1}{n}{\sum }_{j=1}^{n}{\widetilde{\ell }}_{j}.$$
(54)

The loss-dependent component of (52) is

$${{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{{{\mbox{MV}}}}=\dfrac{{\lambda }_{1}}{n}{\sum }_{i=1}^{n}{\widetilde{\ell }}_{i}+\dfrac{{\lambda }_{2}}{n(n-1)}{\sum }_{i=1}^{n}{\left({\widetilde{\ell }}_{i}-\overline{\widetilde{\ell }}\right)}^{2}.$$
(55)

The memberships \({\{{s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}\}}_{i=1}^{n}\) are constructed before optimization and remain fixed throughout model training.

Proposition 3

(Sample-wise first-order sensitivity) For fixed VRIF memberships,

$$\dfrac{\partial {{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{{{\rm{MV}}}}}{\partial {\ell }_{i}}=\dfrac{{s}_{i}^{{{\rm{VRIF}}}}}{n}\left[{\lambda }_{1}+\dfrac{2{\lambda }_{2}}{n-1}\left({\widetilde{\ell }}_{i}-\overline{\widetilde{\ell }}\right)\right].$$
(56)

If 0 ≤ ℓj ≤ L for all j, where L > 0 is finite, then

$$\left\vert \dfrac{\partial {{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{{{\mbox{MV}}}}}{\partial {\ell }_{i}}\right\vert \le \dfrac{{s}_{i}^{{{\mbox{VRIF}}}}}{n}\left({\lambda }_{1}+\dfrac{2{\lambda }_{2}L}{n-1}\right).$$
(57)

Equation (57) shows that the first-order sensitivity associated with the ith loss is explicitly scaled by its VRIF membership. Thus, a small membership limits the magnitude of the sample’s local effect on the weighted mean–variance term.

Let

$${C}_{L}={\lambda }_{1}+\frac{2{\lambda }_{2}L}{n-1}.$$
(58)

Combining (57) with the aggregate membership bounds in (42) gives

$${\sum}_{i\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{+}}\left| \frac{\partial {{{ \mathcal L }}}_{{\mathrm{MV}}}}{\partial {\ell }_{i}}\right| \le \frac{{n}_{+}}{n}{C}_{L},\qquad {\sum }_{i\in {{{ \mathcal I }}}_{-}}\left| \frac{\partial {{{ \mathcal L }}}_{{\mathrm{MV}}}}{\partial {\ell }_{i}}\right| \le \frac{{n}_{+}}{n}{C}_{L}.$$
(59)

Hence, the majority class does not obtain a larger class-level sensitivity upper bound solely because it contains more samples. These inequalities are upper bounds and do not imply equal realized sensitivities for the two classes.

Proposition 4

(Lower boundedness and existence) Assume that K is symmetric positive semidefinite and λ1, λ2 ≥ 0. Then the objective in (52) is continuous, bounded from below, and admits at least one global minimizer.

Proposition 4 establishes the well-posedness of the direct variance-penalized objective. For the finite-p smoothed formulation used in computation, sufficient conditions for convexity are established in Proposition 5 below.

The proofs of the preceding propositions are provided in Appendix B.

4.4.3 Smooth newton-type solution

Since the hinge loss is nondifferentiable, it is approximated by the numerically stable softplus function

$${\phi }_{p}(\xi )=\max\{\xi ,0\}+\frac{1}{p}{{\mathrm{log}}}(1+\exp (-p|\xi |)),\,p > 0.$$
(60)

For any fixed ξ, ϕp(ξ) approaches \(\max \{\xi ,0\}\) as p increases.

For the ith training sample, define the margin residual and the smooth VRIF-weighted loss as

$${\xi }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})=1-{y}_{i}{K}_{i}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}},\qquad {z}_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})={s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}{\phi }_{p}({\xi }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})).$$
(61)

Let

$${{\boldsymbol{z}}}={({z}_{1},\ldots ,{z}_{n})}^{T},\qquad Q=I-\frac{1}{n}{{\bf{1}}}{{{\bf{1}}}}^{T}.$$
(62)

The smoothed direct-variance objective corresponding to (52) is

$${F}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})=\frac{1}{2}{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{T}K{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}+\frac{{\lambda }_{1}}{n}{{{\bf{1}}}}^{T}{{\boldsymbol{z}}}+\frac{{\lambda }_{2}}{n(n-1)}{{{\boldsymbol{z}}}}^{T}Q{{\boldsymbol{z}}}.$$
(63)

To avoid conflict with the hesitation degree πi defined in Section 3.3, denote the first and second derivatives of the softplus function by

$${\sigma }_{i}=\phi ^{\prime} ({\xi }_{i})=\frac{1}{1+\exp (-p{\xi }_{i})},\qquad {\kappa }_{i}=\phi ^{\prime\prime} ({\xi }_{i})=p{\sigma }_{i}(1-{\sigma }_{i}).$$
(64)

Furthermore, define

$$\overline{z}=\dfrac{1}{n}{\sum }_{j=1}^{n}{z}_{j},\qquad {\delta }_{i}={z}_{i}-\overline{z},\qquad {\vartheta }_{i}={\lambda }_{1}+\dfrac{2{\lambda }_{2}}{n-1}{\delta }_{i}.$$
(65)

Let

$${{\boldsymbol{\sigma }}}={({\sigma }_{1},\ldots ,{\sigma }_{n})}^{T},\qquad {{\boldsymbol{\vartheta }}}={({\vartheta }_{1},\ldots ,{\vartheta }_{n})}^{T},$$

and

$${{\boldsymbol{s}}}={({s}_{1}^{{{\mbox{VRIF}}}},\ldots ,{s}_{n}^{{{\mbox{VRIF}}}})}^{T}.$$

Then the gradient of Fp is

$$\nabla {F}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})=K{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}-\frac{1}{n}K({{\boldsymbol{\vartheta }}}\odot {{\boldsymbol{s}}}\odot {{\boldsymbol{\sigma }}}\odot {{\boldsymbol{y}}}),$$
(66)

where  ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication.

For the Hessian, define

$${\omega }_{i}=\frac{{\vartheta }_{i}{s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}{\kappa }_{i}}{n}+\frac{2{\lambda }_{2}{({s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}{\sigma }_{i})}^{2}}{n(n-1)},$$
(67)

and

$${{\mathcal{X}}}=K({s} \odot \sigma \odot {y}).$$
(68)

The analytical Hessian is therefore

$${\nabla }^{2}{F}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})=K+K \, {{\mbox{Diag}}}({{\boldsymbol{\omega }}})K-\dfrac{2{\lambda }_{2}}{{n}^{2}(n-1)}{{\boldsymbol{\chi }}}{{{\boldsymbol{\chi }}}}^{T}.$$
(69)

For each finite p, the objective Fp is twice continuously differentiable. The following proposition gives a sufficient condition for its convexity.

Proposition 5

(Convexity under sufficient curvature conditions) Let \({\rm\Omega }\subseteq {\mathbb{R}}^{n}\) be a convex set and define

$${{\boldsymbol{a}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})={{\boldsymbol{s}}}\odot {{\boldsymbol{\sigma }}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})\odot {{\boldsymbol{y}}},\qquad {c}_{\lambda }=\frac{2{\lambda }_{2}}{{n}^{2}(n-1)}.$$
(70)

Assume that K ≽ 0 and that, for every α ∈ Ω,

$${{\mbox{Diag}}}(\omega(\alpha)) - c_{\lambda}\,a(\alpha)a(\alpha)^{T} \succeq 0.$$
(71)

Then Fp is convex on Ω. If K ≻ 0, then Fp is strictly convex on Ω.

A sufficient scalar condition for (71) is

$${{\min }_{1\le i\le n}}{\omega }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})\ge {c}_{\lambda }{\left\Vert {{\boldsymbol{a}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})\right\Vert }_{2}^{2},\qquad {{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\in \Omega .$$
(72)

Indeed,

$${{\boldsymbol{a}}}{{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}^{T}\preccurlyeq \parallel {{\boldsymbol{a}}}{\parallel }_{2}^{2}I.$$

Moreover, because \(0\le {s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}\le 1\) and 0 < σi < 1, we have \(\parallel {{\boldsymbol{a}}}{\parallel }_{2}^{2}\le n\). Hence, the stronger but simpler condition

$${\omega }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})\ge \dfrac{2{\lambda }_{2}}{n(n-1)},\qquad i=1,\ldots ,n,\quad {{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\in \Omega ,$$
(73)

also guarantees convexity.

When the preceding conditions hold on \({\rm\Omega }={\mathbb{R}}^{n}\), the smoothed objective is globally convex. Consequently, every stationary point of Fp is a global minimizer. If K ≻ 0, the minimizer is unique.

The proof of Proposition 5 is provided in Appendix B.4.

To improve numerical stability, small or nonpositive diagonal curvature coefficients are discarded. At iteration t, define

$${\hat{\omega }}_{i}^{(t)}=\left\{\begin{array}{cc}{\omega }_{i}^{(t)}, & {\omega }_{i}^{(t)} > {\epsilon }_{{\mathrm{curv}}},\\ 0, & \,{\mathrm{otherwise}}\,,\end{array}\right.$$
(74)

where ϵcurv > 0 is a small numerical threshold. The screened Newton matrix is

$${\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}}^{(t)}=K+K\,{{\rm{Diag}}}\left({\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\omega }}}}}^{(t)}\right)K-\dfrac{2{\lambda }_{2}}{{n}^{2}(n-1)}{{{\boldsymbol{\chi }}}}^{(t)}{{{\boldsymbol{\chi }}}}^{(t)T}.$$
(75)

A diagonally stabilized Newton direction is obtained from

$$\left[{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}}^{(t)}+{\varpi }_{t}I\right]{{{\boldsymbol{d}}}}^{(t)}=-\nabla {F}_{p}\left({{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{(t)}\right),$$
(76)

where ϖt > 0 is increased geometrically until a numerically usable system is obtained. If the resulting direction is nonfinite or does not satisfy

$$\nabla {F}_{p}{({{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{(t)})}^{T}{{{\boldsymbol{d}}}}^{(t)} < 0,$$
(77)

the negative gradient direction is used instead.

Starting from ζt = 1, Armijo backtracking is applied by repeatedly halving the trial step. The Armijo condition checked during the line search is

$${F}_{p}({{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{(t)}+{\zeta }_{t}{{{\boldsymbol{d}}}}^{(t)})\le {F}_{p}({{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{(t)})+{c}_{{{\rm{A}}}}{\zeta }_{t}\nabla {F}_{p}{({{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{(t)})}^{T}{{{\boldsymbol{d}}}}^{(t)},$$
(78)

where 0 < cA < 1. The coefficient vector is updated as

$${{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{(t+1)}={{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{(t)}+{\zeta }_{t}{{{\boldsymbol{d}}}}^{(t)}.$$
(79)

A continuation strategy is used for the smoothing parameter. Let nα denote the dimension of the coefficient vector, with nα = n for the full-kernel formulation. After an iteration, if

$${\parallel \nabla {F}_{p}({{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{(t+1)})\parallel }_{2} < \max \left\{\frac{{n}_{\alpha }}{p},1\right\}\quad \,{{\mbox{and}}}\,\quad p < {p}_{\max },$$
(80)

then

$$p\leftarrow \min \{10p,{p}_{\max }\}.$$
(81)

The iterations terminate when

$${\left\Vert \nabla {F}_{p}({{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{(t)})\right\Vert }_{2}\le {\epsilon }_{{{\mbox{tol}}}}\qquad \,{\mbox{and}}\,\qquad p={p}_{\max },$$
(82)

or when the maximum iteration number is reached.

In the implementation, \({p}_{\max }=100\), ϵtol = 10−2, and \({T}_{\max }=300\). The curvature threshold is set to ϵcurv = 10−10, the initial diagonal stabilization is 10−3, and the stabilization coefficient is multiplied by 10 for at most six attempts. The Armijo coefficient is cA = 0.05, and at most fifteen step-halving trials are performed. The optimization procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1.

Reduced-kernel implementation

For relatively large training sets, a reduced-kernel representation can be adopted to decrease the dimension of the optimization variable. Let \({{\mathcal{M}}}=\{1,\ldots ,n\}\) denote the full training index set, and let \({{ \mathcal J }}\subseteq {{ \mathcal M }}\) be a selected basis index set with

$$| {{ \mathcal J }}| =r\le n.$$

The decision function is represented as

$$f({{\boldsymbol{x}}})={\sum}_{j\in {{ \mathcal J }}}{\beta }_{j}K({{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{j},{{\boldsymbol{x}}}),$$
(83)

where \({{\boldsymbol{\beta }}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{r}\) is the reduced coefficient vector.

Let \({K}_{{{ \mathcal J }}{{ \mathcal M }}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{r\times n}\) denote the kernel matrix between the basis samples and all training samples, and let \({K}_{{{\mathcal{J}}}{{\mathcal{J}}}}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{r\times r}\) denote the kernel matrix defined on the basis set. The decision values on the training samples are

$${{\boldsymbol{f}}}={K}_{{{ \mathcal J }}{{ \mathcal M }}}^{T}{{\boldsymbol{\beta }}},$$
(84)

and the margin residual of the ith sample is

$${\xi }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\beta }}})=1-{y}_{i}{K}_{{{ \mathcal J }}i}^{T}{{\boldsymbol{\beta }}},$$
(85)

where \({K}_{{{ \mathcal J }}i}\) denotes the ith column of \({K}_{{{\mathcal{J}}}{{\mathcal{M}}}}\).

The corresponding smooth VRIF-weighted loss is

$${z}_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\beta }}})={s}_{i}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}{\phi }_{p}({\xi }_{i}({{\boldsymbol{\beta }}})),$$
(86)

and the reduced smooth objective is

$${F}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\beta }}})=\dfrac{1}{2}\,{{{\boldsymbol{\beta }}}}^{T}{K}_{{{\mathcal{J}}}{{\mathcal{J}}}}{{\boldsymbol{\beta }}}+\dfrac{{\lambda }_{1}}{n}{{{\bf{1}}}}^{T}{{\boldsymbol{z}}}({{\boldsymbol{\beta }}})+\dfrac{{\lambda }_{2}}{n(n-1)}{{\boldsymbol{z}}}{({{\boldsymbol{\beta }}})}^{T}Q{{\boldsymbol{z}}}({{\boldsymbol{\beta }}}),$$
(87)

where Q = I − 11T/n is the centering matrix defined above.

The empirical mean and variance are evaluated using all n training samples; only the kernel basis and the dimension of the coefficient vector are reduced. The smooth Newton-type procedure described above remains applicable after replacing α by β and using \({K}_{{{ \mathcal J }}{{ \mathcal M }}}\) and \({K}_{{{ \mathcal J }}{{ \mathcal J }}}\) in the corresponding gradient and Hessian expressions.

The full-kernel formulation is recovered when \({{\mathcal{J}}}={{\mathcal{M}}}\), in which case r = n, β = α, \({{ \mathcal J }}={{ \mathcal M }}\), and \({K}_{{{ \mathcal J }}{{ \mathcal J }}}=K\).

4.4.4 Computational complexity

The computational cost of VRIF-VPSVM consists of VRIF membership construction, kernel evaluation, and the smooth Newton-type optimization.

Using direct pairwise-distance computation, constructing the VRIF memberships requires

$$O({n}^{2}d)$$
(88)

time, where n and d denote the number of training samples and the feature dimension, respectively.

For the reduced-kernel formulation, let \(r=| {{ \mathcal J }}| \) denote the number of selected basis samples. Assuming that one kernel evaluation requires O(d) operations, constructing \({K}_{{{ \mathcal J }}{{ \mathcal M }}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{r\times n}\) requires O(nrd) time and O(nr) storage. The basis kernel matrix \({K}_{{{\mathcal{J}}}{{\mathcal{J}}}}\) requires O(r2d) time and O(r2) storage; since r ≤ n, this cost is included in the corresponding bounds below.

At each Newton iteration, evaluating the margin residuals, smooth weighted losses, objective, and gradient requires O(nr) operations. Forming the curvature contribution to the Newton matrix requires O(nr2) operations, while solving the resulting r × r linear system requires O(r3) operations. The bounded numbers of diagonal stabilization attempts and Armijo backtracking trials do not change these asymptotic orders.

Therefore, if the optimization terminates after T iterations, its time complexity is

$$O(T(n{r}^{2}+{r}^{3})),$$
(89)

and its space complexity is

$$O(nr+{r}^{2}).$$
(90)

Including membership construction and kernel evaluation, the total training-time complexity is

$$O({n}^{2}d+nrd+T(n{r}^{2}+{r}^{3})).$$
(91)

When \({{ \mathcal J }}={{ \mathcal M }}\), we have r = n, and the formulation reduces to the full-kernel implementation. Its dominant optimization time and space complexities are O(Tn3) and O(n2), respectively, while the total training-time complexity is

$$O({n}^{2}d+T{n}^{3}).$$
(92)

When r ≪ n, the reduced-kernel representation can decrease the dimension of the Newton system, while the empirical mean and variance of the weighted losses are still evaluated over all n training samples.

5 Experiments and discussion

This section evaluates the effectiveness and robustness of VRIF-VPSVM. The experiments are organized into two parts. First, artificial datasets are used to visually examine the decision-boundary behavior and the membership mechanism of VRIF-VPSVM. Second, benchmark imbalanced datasets are used to evaluate the proposed method under standard imbalanced settings and noisy imbalanced settings.

5.1 Experiments on artificial datasets

Artificial datasets are first used to provide an intuitive illustration of the behavior of VRIF-VPSVM. Two complementary artificial experiments are considered. The first revisits the two-dimensional artificial setting introduced in Section 4.1 and compares SVM, RASVM-VP, and VRIF-VPSVM under clean balanced, class-imbalanced, noisy, and noisy class-imbalanced conditions. The second uses two additional imbalanced datasets with more complex structures, including separated minority-class subclusters, noisy majority samples, outliers, and a non-convex class boundary, to further examine the decision-boundary behavior under noisy and locally ambiguous conditions.

Figure 6 presents the decision boundaries of SVM, RASVM-VP, and VRIF-VPSVM on the first artificial dataset. The four panels correspond to the clean balanced case, class-imbalanced case, noisy case, and noisy class-imbalanced case, respectively.

As shown in Fig. 6, RASVM-VP achieves a higher G-Mean than SVM in all four settings, illustrating the effect of variance penalization in this artificial example. However, the performance of RASVM-VP still decreases noticeably when class imbalance and noisy observations coexist. Specifically, the G-Mean of RASVM-VP decreases from 97.06% in the clean balanced case to 90.35% in the noisy class-imbalanced case. By contrast, VRIF-VPSVM achieves a G-Mean of 96.02% in the noisy class-imbalanced case, which is substantially higher than those of SVM and RASVM-VP. In the class-imbalanced case, VRIF-VPSVM also improves the G-Mean from 92.58% obtained by RASVM-VP to 96.02%. These results suggest that the proposed VRIF-VPSVM framework can better accommodate the combined effects of class imbalance and unreliable noisy samples. By adjusting the loss distribution before variance control, the learned decision boundary becomes more favorable under the considered noisy and imbalanced setting.

To further examine the decision-boundary behavior under more complex imbalanced and noisy distributions, Fig. 7 presents the decision boundaries of SVM, RASVM-VP, and VRIF-VPSVM on two additional artificial datasets.

As shown in Fig. 7, SVM is more sensitive to imbalanced and noisy data distributions, especially when ambiguous samples appear near the class boundary. RASVM-VP improves the decision boundary to some extent by introducing variance penalization, but it still applies the variance penalty to the original hinge-loss distribution and may be influenced by unreliable samples. By contrast, VRIF-VPSVM first assigns reliability-aware memberships and then applies variance penalization to the VRIF-weighted loss distribution. This reduces the influence of low-reliability samples on the separating hyperplane and leads to a more stable decision boundary.

The artificial experiments provide complementary illustrations of the decision-boundary behavior of VRIF-VPSVM under different imbalanced and noisy data structures. In the first experiment, VRIF-VPSVM exhibits a more favorable decision boundary than SVM and RASVM-VP when class imbalance and noisy observations occur simultaneously. In the second experiment, the two artificial datasets further illustrate their behavior under more complex structures involving separated minority-class subclusters, noisy majority samples, outliers, and non-convex class boundaries. Together with the membership analysis in Section 4.3.5, these results provide an intuitive illustration of how reliability-aware weighting and variance penalization influence the learned decision boundary.

5.2 Experiments on benchmark imbalanced datasets

This subsection evaluates VRIF-VPSVM on benchmark imbalanced datasets. Two experimental settings are considered. In the first setting, the benchmark datasets are used without adding artificial noise. In the second setting, artificial feature-space contamination is introduced into the training folds to evaluate the robustness of the compared methods under noisy, imbalanced conditions.

5.2.1 Experimental settings

Twenty-two benchmark imbalanced datasets are used to evaluate the classification performance of the compared methods. These datasets cover different sample sizes, feature dimensions, class distributions, and imbalance degrees. For each dataset, the minority class is treated as the positive class, and the majority class is treated as the negative class. The details of the datasets are summarized in Table 1, where n+ and n− denote the numbers of minority-class and majority-class samples, respectively, and the imbalance ratio is reported in the conventional form 1: (n−/n+).

The benchmark collection ranges from 178 to 17,898 instances and from 3 to 166 features, with imbalance ratios ranging from 1: 1.38 to 1: 42.72. It therefore includes both mildly and highly imbalanced cases. In particular, HTRU2 and MUSK2 extend the evaluation to a larger-scale dataset and a higher-dimensional dataset, respectively. This collection provides a diverse test bed for examining the behavior of the compared methods under different imbalance and data-scale conditions.

The compared methods include SVM Cortes and Vapnik (1995); Vapnik (1998), CS-SVM Ling and Sheng (2008); Rezvani et al. (2024), SMOTE-SVM Chawla et al. (2002), Borderline-SMOTE-SVM Guo et al. (2024); Han et al. (2005) and ADASYN-SVM He et al. (2008); Rao et al. (2024), RASVM-VP Fu et al. (2022, 2023b), GFSVM Batuwita and Palade (2010); Lin and Wang (2002), RIFSVM Fu et al. (2023a), CGFTSVM-ID Hazarika et al. (2023), IRIFSVM, and the proposed VRIF-VPSVM. These methods cover standard, cost-sensitive, oversampling-based, variance-penalized, and fuzzy/intuitionistic-fuzzy classifiers. IRIFSVM uses the IRIF membership within a fuzzy SVM objective and serves as the IRIF-based baseline.

For RBF-kernel methods, the kernel and penalty-related hyperparameters are selected by inner cross-validation. Under the standard search configuration, the kernel parameter is considered over h ∈ {2−7, 2−6, …, 27}, while the penalty-related parameters are searched over logarithmic scales, with λ, λ1 ∈ {10−5, 10−3, …, 105} and λ2 ∈ {0, 10−5, 10−3, …, 105}. For methods or datasets requiring alternative computational configurations, predefined configuration-specific candidate grids are used. Other method-specific parameters follow the candidate settings of the corresponding implementations.

For IRIF-based methods, \({k}_{+}=\lceil \sqrt{{n}_{+}}\rceil \) and \({k}_{-}=\lceil \sqrt{{n}_{-}}\rceil \). For VRIF-VPSVM, τ is fixed at 0.75, whereas the kernel parameter, mean-loss parameter λ1, variance-penalty parameter λ2, and robustness-control parameter η are selected by inner cross-validation. Under the standard search configuration, η is considered over {0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00, 1.50, 3.00}. In particular, η = 0 allows the additional VRIF robustness correction to remain inactive when this setting is favored by the validation data.

All methods are evaluated under the same repeated stratified outer cross-validation protocol. Specifically, two repetitions of stratified five-fold cross-validation are performed. For each outer training fold, hyperparameters are selected by two-fold inner cross-validation using G-Mean as the selection criterion. Hyperparameter selection is conducted exclusively within the outer training data. After parameter selection, the model is retrained on the entire outer training fold and evaluated on the corresponding outer test fold.

The evaluation metrics include sensitivity (Se), specificity (Sp), G-Mean, AUPRC, and IBA. Let TP, TN, FP, and FN denote true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively. Sensitivity, specificity, and G-Mean are defined as

$${{\mbox{Se}}}=\frac{TP}{{TP}+{FN}},\qquad {{\mbox{Sp}}}=\frac{TN}{{TN}+{FP}},\qquad {{\rm{G}}}-{{\mbox{Mean}}}=\sqrt{{\mbox{Se}} \times {Sp}}.$$
(93)

AUPRC denotes the area under the precision–recall curve and is used to evaluate minority-class-oriented precision–recall performance under class imbalance. The index of balanced accuracy is computed as

$${{\mathrm{IBA}}}_{\alpha }=[1+\alpha ({\mathrm{Se}}-{\mathrm{Sp}})]{({{\rm{G}}}-{\mathrm{Mean}})}^{2},$$
(94)

where α = 0.1 is used in this study. In the summary tables, average ranks are computed by first ranking the compared methods on each dataset and then averaging the ranks over all datasets. Therefore, the ordering of average ranks may differ from the ordering of mean metric values.

5.2.3 Robustness on noisy imbalanced datasets

To further evaluate the robustness of the proposed method, experiments are conducted under noisy imbalanced conditions using the same 22 benchmark datasets and 11 methods as in the preceding benchmark experiment. In contrast to the clean benchmark setting, feature-space contamination is introduced only into the training folds, while the corresponding test folds remain clean. The contaminated samples are modified in place without changing their class labels; therefore, the number of samples, class labels, and class-imbalance ratio remain unchanged. This experimental setting evaluates whether the compared methods can maintain reliable classification performance when the training data contain unreliable observations while preserving the original class distribution.

Four contamination ratios are considered:

$$\rho \in \{0.05,0.10,0.15,0.20\}.$$
(95)

For each class c ∈ { + 1, − 1}, let \({n}_{c}^{{\mbox{noise}}}=\max \{1,\lfloor \rho {n}^{{\mbox{tr}}}_{c} \rfloor \}\) denote the number of training samples belonging to class c. The number of samples selected for contamination is defined as

$${n}_{c}^{{\mbox{noise}}}=\max \{1,\lfloor \rho {n}_{c}^{{\mbox{tr}}}\rfloor \}.$$
(96)

Thus, the minority and majority classes are contaminated using the same class-wise proportion. For each outer training fold, a fixed random ordering is used to select contaminated samples, such that the contaminated subset at a lower noise ratio is nested within that at a higher noise ratio.

The mixed feature contamination consists of boundary perturbations and heavy-tailed perturbations. Approximately 70% of the selected samples in each class are subjected to boundary perturbations, while the remaining 30% receive heavy-tailed perturbations. For a selected sample \({x}_{i}^{(c)}\), let \({x}_{j}^{(-c)}\) denote its nearest sample from the opposite class. The boundary perturbation is defined as

$${\widetilde{x}}_{i}={x}_{i}^{(c)}+\lambda \left({x}_{j}^{(-c)}-{x}_{i}^{(c)}\right)+{\epsilon }_{i},$$
(97)

where

$$\lambda \sim {{\mathcal{U}}}(0.10,0.40),\qquad {\epsilon }_{i} \sim {{\mathcal{N}}}\left(0,{(0.05{s}_{c})}^{2}I\right).$$
(98)

Here, sc denotes a class-specific local scale estimated from the median nearest-neighbor distance among samples of class c in the standardized training data. This perturbation shifts selected samples toward the opposite class while introducing a small random deviation, thereby increasing ambiguity in the vicinity of the class boundary.

For heavy-tailed contamination, a selected sample is perturbed according to

$${\widetilde{x}}_{i}={x}_{i}^{(c)}+\dfrac{0.25{s}_{c}}{\sqrt{d}}\,{t}_{i},$$
(99)

where d denotes the feature dimension and the components of ti independently follow a Student-t distribution with three degrees of freedom. Extreme component values are truncated for numerical stability. This perturbation introduces irregular feature deviations while preserving both the original sample labels and the class distribution.

The same repeated stratified outer cross-validation splits used in the clean benchmark experiments are retained. Feature standardization is determined from the clean training portion, and contamination is subsequently introduced only into the corresponding training data. During inner cross-validation, contamination is applied to the inner training subset according to the specified noise ratio, whereas the inner validation subset remains clean. Hyperparameters are selected according to inner-validation G-Mean. The selected model configuration is then trained on the contaminated outer training fold and evaluated on the corresponding clean outer test fold. Consequently, the outer test data are not involved in either contamination generation or hyperparameter selection.

The detailed dataset-wise results under the four contamination ratios are reported in Appendix Tables C1–C4. To keep the main text concise, Table 4 reports the mean performance over the four contamination ratios together with dataset-level average ranks. For each method, the four noise-specific results are first averaged within each dataset. The compared methods are then ranked separately on each of the 22 datasets, and the resulting dataset-level ranks are averaged across datasets and used in the Friedman–Nemenyi analysis.

Table 4 shows that VRIF-VPSVM achieves the highest mean G-Mean of 88.45% and the highest mean IBA of 79.22% under noisy imbalanced conditions. It also obtains the best dataset-level average ranks on these two metrics, with ranks of 3.52 and 3.48, respectively. These results indicate that the favorable balance-oriented performance of VRIF-VPSVM is maintained when feature contamination is introduced into the training data.

For AUPRC, CGFTSVM-ID achieves the highest mean value of 85.10% and the best average rank of 3.27. VRIF-VPSVM obtains a mean AUPRC of 82.17% with an average rank of 4.32. Thus, the advantage of VRIF-VPSVM under noisy imbalanced conditions is more clearly reflected in G-Mean and IBA, whereas CGFTSVM-ID exhibits stronger precision–recall performance among the compared methods.

To further compare the methods from a rank-based perspective, Fig. 9 presents the average-rank diagrams with the Nemenyi critical difference for G-Mean, AUPRC, and IBA. For each method, the performance values obtained under the four contamination ratios are first averaged within each dataset. The 11 methods are then ranked separately on each of the 22 datasets, and the final average ranks are obtained by averaging these dataset-level ranks. The Friedman tests indicate significant differences among the compared methods for all three evaluation metrics (p < 0.001). At the significance level α = 0.05, the Nemenyi critical difference for 11 methods over the 22 dataset-level statistical units is approximately 3.22.

As shown in Fig. 9, VRIF-VPSVM obtains the most favorable dataset-level average ranks for G-Mean and IBA, with average ranks of 3.52 and 3.48, respectively. For AUPRC, CGFTSVM-ID achieves the best average rank of 3.27, while VRIF-VPSVM obtains an average rank of 4.32. The rank differences among several leading methods are smaller than the Nemenyi critical difference. Therefore, the results indicate favorable rankings of VRIF-VPSVM on the balance-oriented G-Mean and IBA metrics, rather than statistically significant superiority over every competing method.

Table 5 further summarizes the performance at each contamination ratio. For each ratio and evaluation metric, the best-performing method, its corresponding mean value, and the mean performance of VRIF-VPSVM are reported. All values are averaged over the 22 benchmark datasets under the corresponding contamination ratio.

As shown in Table 5, VRIF-VPSVM achieves the highest G-Mean and IBA at all four contamination ratios. 5.2.4 Component ablation analysis

To examine the respective contributions of the VRIF robustness correction and the variance-penalization mechanism, an ablation study is conducted on ten representative imbalanced benchmark datasets, including Abalone, Ecoli, Glass, Haberman, Libra, Liver, Pima, Seed, Vehicle, and Wine. For each outer training fold, paired bidirectional label flipping is introduced while preserving the original class sizes. The noise ratios are set to 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%. Noise is introduced only into the training data, whereas the validation and test labels remain clean. All variants use the same data partitions, noise realizations, and inner cross-validation procedure. Within this ablation study, the same predefined candidate parameter sets are used across the examined noise levels, and the final parameter values are determined exclusively through inner cross-validation. The main benchmark comparison in Section 5.2.2 still reports results on the full benchmark set.

Four model variants are considered to examine the roles of the weighting and variance-control mechanisms. IRIFSVM uses the IRIF base membership without variance penalization. IRIF-VPSVM retains the same IRIF base membership and introduces variance penalization. VRIF-FSVM uses the proposed VRIF membership without variance penalization. VRIF-VPSVM evaluates the empirical mean and variance on the VRIF-weighted loss distribution. This design allows the effect of variance control to be examined under both IRIF and VRIF weighting, while the influence of the VRIF correction on the weighted loss distribution can be assessed separately.

Table 6 reports the average G-Mean, AUPRC, and IBA of the four ablation variants under label-flipping noise. The results show that variance penalization provides the clearest improvement in predictive performance. Compared with IRIFSVM, IRIF-VPSVM improves the average G-Mean, AUPRC, and IBA by 1.70, 1.52, and 2.89 percentage points, respectively. A similar pattern is observed under VRIF weighting, where VRIF-VPSVM improves these three metrics over VRIF-FSVM by 1.75, 1.57, and 2.98 percentage points, respectively. These consistent improvements indicate that controlling the dispersion of the reliability-weighted losses is beneficial under noisy and imbalanced training conditions.

The direct predictive contribution of the VRIF correction is limited in the present ablation results. Without variance penalization, IRIFSVM and VRIF-FSVM achieve nearly identical average performance. Within the variance-penalized framework, VRIF-VPSVM improves the average G-Mean and IBA over IRIF-VPSVM by 0.06 and 0.08 percentage points, respectively, while its AUPRC differs by only 0.02 percentage points in the opposite direction. These results indicate that the VRIF correction should not be interpreted as providing an independent statistically significant improvement in predictive performance. Rather, it serves as an additional reliability-aware adjustment of the loss distribution within the complete variance-penalized framework.

To assess whether the component effects are consistent across datasets, dataset-wise statistical tests are further conducted. The four noise-level results are first averaged within each dataset so that different noise conditions generated from the same dataset are not treated as independent observations. Friedman tests indicate significant differences among the four variants for G-Mean (p = 0.0061), AUPRC (p = 0.0005), and IBA (p = 0.0120). For the three prespecified component-wise comparisons, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction show statistically supported improvements when variance penalization is introduced. Specifically, IRIF-VPSVM versus IRIFSVM and VRIF-VPSVM versus VRIF-FSVM yield adjusted p-values of 0.041, 0.041, and 0.029 for G-Mean, AUPRC, and IBA, respectively. In contrast, VRIF-VPSVM versus IRIF-VPSVM does not show a statistically significant difference, with adjusted p-values of 0.641, 0.359, and 0.547, respectively. These results indicate that variance penalization accounts for the main predictive improvement observed in the component ablation.

The relatively small difference in predictive performance suggests that the effect of the VRIF correction should be examined more directly through its influence on the reliability-weighted loss distribution. To isolate this effect from changes in the learned classifier, a same-decision-function counterfactual analysis is conducted. For each trained IRIF-VPSVM reference model, the decision scores are fixed, while the sample losses are recomputed under different membership transformations. Hence, within this counterfactual comparison, changes in the resulting weighted-loss variance can be attributed to the weighting transformation rather than to differences between independently trained decision functions.

In addition to the proposed reciprocal attenuation, exponential and linear attenuation functions are introduced as alternative controls. To make the comparison depend primarily on functional form rather than endpoint strength, the three functions are endpoint-matched. Let q = 1 − rIRIF and ρ = (1+η)−1. The reciprocal, exponential, and linear attenuation functions are respectively defined as

$${\psi }^{R}(q)=\frac{1}{1+\eta q},\quad {\psi }^{E}(q)={\rho }^{q},\quad {\psi }^{L}(q)=1-(1-\rho )q,$$

so that all three satisfy ψ(0) = 1 and ψ(1) = ρ. The alternative attenuation variants use the same base model parameters, data partitions, and cross-validation protocol, while their attenuation parameter η is selected separately through the same inner-validation procedure.

Figure 10 shows that all three attenuation mechanisms produce positive average reductions in weighted-loss variance relative to the original IRIF weighting. The proposed reciprocal VRIF correction produces average reductions of approximately 4.10%, 5.62%, 6.16%, and 5.67% at noise ratios of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%, respectively. For comparison, the corresponding average reductions are approximately 2.44–3.29% for exponential attenuation and 1.61–1.94% for linear attenuation.

Among the three endpoint-matched attenuation mechanisms, the reciprocal correction provides the largest average reduction in weighted-loss variance at each examined noise level. This observation does not imply that the reciprocal form universally minimizes the loss variance for every dataset. Rather, under the present experimental settings, the proposed VRIF correction produces a more pronounced average stabilization of the reliability-weighted loss distribution than the two alternative attenuation controls. This finding supports its intended role as a reliability-aware loss-shaping mechanism before variance penalization is imposed.

Importantly, the observed reduction in weighted-loss variance should be distinguished from an improvement in predictive performance. The former provides mechanism-oriented evidence that the VRIF correction reshapes the reliability-weighted loss distribution in the intended direction, whereas the latter is not statistically supported for the VRIF correction itself in the component ablation. Therefore, the variance-reduction analysis and the predictive-performance analysis provide complementary but distinct evidence.

The ablation results, therefore, clarify the distinct but complementary roles of the two mechanisms. The VRIF correction acts at the sample level by attenuating low-reliability observations and reshaping the loss distribution before variance control is imposed. Variance penalization then acts at the distribution level by controlling the dispersion of the resulting reliability-weighted losses. Their sequential interaction through the weighted loss distribution defines the complete VRIF-VPSVM framework for learning under noisy and imbalanced conditions.

5.2.5 Parameter sensitivity analysis

To examine the influence of the main parameters in VRIF-VPSVM, we analyze both the interaction between the mean-loss and variance-penalty parameters and the sensitivity of the VRIF robustness-control parameter. Five representative datasets, namely Abalone, Ecoli, Libra, Seed, and Vehicle, are used for this analysis. These datasets cover different sample sizes, dimensionalities, and imbalance levels. The same repeated stratified cross-validation partitions and bidirectional label-flipping realizations as those used in the preceding ablation study are retained. The IRIF scale-correction parameter is fixed at τ = 0.75 throughout the analysis.

We first investigate the joint influence of the mean-loss parameter λ1 and the variance-penalty parameter λ2. According to the direct variance-penalized formulation, these two parameters control different aspects of the reliability-weighted empirical loss: λ1 determines the contribution of its empirical mean, whereas λ2 determines the strength of variance penalization. Therefore, examining them independently may overlook their interaction.

The candidate sets are

$${\lambda }_{1}\in \{1{0}^{-3},1{0}^{-2},1{0}^{-1},1,10,1{0}^{2},1{0}^{3},1{0}^{4}\},$$

and

$${\lambda }_{2}\in \{0,1{0}^{-3},1{0}^{-2},1{0}^{-1},1,1{0}^{2},1{0}^{3},1{0}^{4}\}.$$

Joint sensitivity is evaluated at representative noise ratios of 10% and 20%. For each outer fold, the kernel parameter h and the VRIF robustness parameter η are fixed at the values selected by inner cross-validation in the corresponding VRIF-VPSVM setting in the main experiment, while only λ1 and λ2 are varied. This conditional design isolates the mean–variance interaction without introducing additional variation from the kernel or membership parameters.

Figure 11 reveals a clear interaction between λ1 and λ2. In particular, when the mean-loss penalty is weak, excessively strong variance penalization can lead to a substantial deterioration in G-Mean. This behavior is consistent with the roles of the two terms in the VRIF-VPSVM objective: controlling the dispersion of the weighted losses alone is insufficient if their empirical mean is not adequately constrained.

In contrast, the heatmaps contain relatively broad regions of high G-Mean when the mean-loss and variance terms are assigned more balanced strengths. The favorable region is not restricted to a single isolated parameter pair, indicating that VRIF-VPSVM does not require an extremely precise choice of (λ1, λ2) within the examined range. At the same time, the change in performance across different combinations shows that the two parameters should be tuned jointly rather than treated as independent regularization constants. This observation supports the joint inner-cross-validation strategy used in the main experiments.

We next investigate the robustness-control parameter η, which determines the strength of the unreliability-aware attenuation in the VRIF membership. Recall that η = 0 deactivates the additional VRIF robustness correction, whereas increasing η strengthens the attenuation applied to samples with lower reliability. The following candidate set

$$\eta \in \{0,0.05,0.10,0.25,0.50,0.75,1.00,1.50,3.00\}$$

is examined at noise ratios of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%. For this analysis, h, λ1, and λ2 are fixed at their fold-specific values selected by inner cross-validation in the corresponding main experiment, while only η is varied. Hence, the resulting curves describe the conditional sensitivity to the strength of the VRIF correction.

As shown in Fig. 12, the influence of η is relatively moderate compared with the joint effect of λ1 and λ2. The G-Mean and IBA curves remain within relatively narrow ranges over a substantial portion of the candidate set, indicating that the proposed method is not excessively sensitive to small changes in the attenuation strength. Nevertheless, the preferred value of η varies across noise conditions, and the response is mildly non-monotonic rather than following a uniformly increasing or decreasing trend.

In particular, increasing η does not provide a consistent performance benefit across all noise levels. Although the performance may increase again at the upper end of the examined range for some low-noise settings, the largest tested value does not consistently yield the best result across metrics and noise conditions. Therefore, the local variations near the upper boundary should not be interpreted as evidence that increasingly strong attenuation is universally beneficial. Instead, the results indicate a data- and noise-dependent trade-off: insufficient attenuation may leave low-reliability samples relatively influential, whereas stronger attenuation can reduce the contribution of both unreliable and potentially informative observations.

The sensitivity analysis further clarifies the different roles of the parameters in VRIF-VPSVM. The pair (λ1, λ2) governs the balance between empirical mean-loss control and loss-variance control, while η regulates the strength of sample-level reliability attenuation before variance penalization is imposed. Neither mechanism is characterized by a single universally optimal fixed setting. Accordingly, λ1, λ2, and η are selected through inner cross-validation in the main experiments using the predefined candidate grids associated with the corresponding experimental configuration.

5.2.6 Runtime and scalability analysis

To further evaluate the computational behavior of VRIF-VPSVM, we examine the training time on three representative large-scale or high-dimensional benchmark datasets: HTRU2 (n = 17, 898, d = 8), Block (n = 5, 473, d = 10), and MUSK2 (n = 6, 598, d = 166). The reported training times exclude the time required for inner cross-validation-based hyperparameter selection. For each method, the results are averaged over the repeated outer folds and examined contamination settings. Accordingly, the reported values reflect the model-fitting cost under the selected hyperparameter configurations rather than the total end-to-end computational cost of hyperparameter selection and model training.

As shown in Fig. 13, VRIF-VPSVM is not uniformly faster than the comparison methods, since the reliability-membership construction and variance-aware optimization introduce additional computational cost. Nevertheless, its training time remains within a practical range on the examined datasets. On the high-dimensional MUSK2 dataset, the training time of VRIF-VPSVM is comparable to those of SVM, IRIFSVM, and RASVM-VP, whereas the oversampling-based methods require substantially longer training because of the enlarged effective training set.

For HTRU2, the reduced-kernel implementation uses 800 basis samples, corresponding to approximately 5.6% of each outer training fold. This reduces the dimension of the Newton system while retaining all training samples in the evaluation of the empirical mean and variance of the weighted losses. Together with the complexity analysis in Section 4, the observed runtime results support the practical role of the reduced-kernel formulation in controlling the optimization cost for relatively large training sets.

5.3 Discussion of experimental results

The experimental results are consistent with the design motivation of VRIF-VPSVM. The artificial experiments illustrate that adjusting the loss distribution according to sample reliability and class-imbalance information before variance control can lead to more favorable decision boundaries under imbalanced, noisy, and locally ambiguous data structures. The benchmark experiments further show favorable performance of VRIF-VPSVM on balance-oriented evaluation criteria. In particular, its advantage is mainly reflected in the balanced-class recognition characterized by G-Mean and IBA, indicating that the proposed framework can effectively account for both minority- and majority-class classification performance.

The noisy-data experiments further show that VRIF-VPSVM maintains stable balance-oriented performance across the examined contamination levels. The dataset-level rank analysis also places VRIF-VPSVM among the leading methods on G-Mean and IBA, while the Friedman tests indicate statistically significant overall differences among the compared methods. These observations suggest that incorporating sample reliability and class-imbalance information into the loss distribution before variance control helps preserve balanced classification performance when unreliable observations are introduced into the training data.

The component analysis clarifies the roles and interactions of the two main mechanisms in VRIF-VPSVM. The VRIF weighting mechanism adjusts the contribution of individual samples according to their reliability and class-imbalance information, thereby reshaping the loss distribution before its empirical mean and variance are evaluated. Variance penalization subsequently controls the dispersion of this reliability-adjusted loss distribution. The same-decision-function analysis supports the role of VRIF weighting in reducing weighted-loss variance, while the comparison with alternative attenuation functions illustrates the behavior of the proposed correction relative to the two alternative forms. These results indicate that the two mechanisms are coupled through the weighted loss distribution: VRIF weighting determines the sample-wise losses on which the empirical mean and variance are subsequently evaluated, while variance penalization controls the dispersion of the resulting reliability-adjusted loss distribution.

The parameter-sensitivity analysis provides further insight into this coupled mechanism. The mean-loss parameter λ1 and the variance-penalty parameter λ2 exhibit a clear interaction, indicating that the empirical mean and the dispersion of the weighted losses should be controlled jointly. The robustness-control parameter η provides an additional adjustment of the reliability-aware weighting effect according to the data characteristics and noise conditions. These observations further support the coordinated roles of sample-level reliability adjustment and distribution-level variance control in the proposed framework.

The runtime analysis further shows that the additional reliability-membership construction and variance-aware optimization introduce measurable computational overhead, while the reported model-fitting cost remains practical on the larger and higher-dimensional datasets examined in this study. In particular, the reduced-kernel implementation decreases the optimization dimension on HTRU2, demonstrating its practical role in controlling the computational cost for relatively large training sets.

The present VRIF-VPSVM is formulated for binary imbalanced classification, where the reliability weighting and class-imbalance adjustment are defined with respect to the minority–majority class structure. Extending this framework to multiclass problems would therefore require the reliability and imbalance adjustments to accommodate multiple classes with different class frequencies and reliability distributions. The variance-penalized formulation would also need to be defined for an appropriate multiclass loss or margin representation. From a computational perspective, depending on whether a one-vs-rest, one-vs-one, or direct multiclass formulation is adopted, the extension may involve multiple decision functions or coupled class margins, thereby increasing the optimization cost, particularly in the kernel setting.

6 Conclusions

This paper proposed VRIF-VPSVM, a robust intuitionistic fuzzy variance-penalized support vector machine for class-imbalance learning. The proposed VRIF membership incorporates local relative-density information, class-wise scale correction, class-imbalance adjustment, and an unreliability-degree-based correction to characterize sample reliability under imbalanced and noisy conditions. Theoretical analysis established the validity and class-wise aggregate control of the proposed membership, together with its monotonicity and scalar sensitivity. Sample-wise sensitivity bounds for the weighted objective and sufficient convexity conditions for the smoothed formulation were also derived.

The key idea of VRIF-VPSVM is to adjust the loss distribution before variance control is performed. Specifically, the VRIF membership reweights the sample-wise hinge losses according to reliability and class-imbalance information, and the empirical mean and variance are subsequently evaluated on the resulting reliability-adjusted loss distribution. In this way, sample-level reliability adjustment directly influences the statistical loss quantities on which variance penalization operates, providing a unified formulation for incorporating class imbalance, sample reliability, and loss-distribution variability into the learning process.

For computation, a direct variance-penalized formulation was derived through a constrained representation of the square-root variance objective. The nonsmooth hinge loss was approximated using the numerically stable softplus function, and the resulting smooth problem was solved by a stabilized Newton-type method with Armijo backtracking. A reduced-kernel extension was further developed to reduce the computational burden for relatively large training sets.

The experimental studies on artificial and benchmark imbalanced datasets support the proposed formulation from both predictive and mechanism-oriented perspectives. The artificial experiments illustrate favorable decision-boundary behavior under imbalanced, noisy, and locally ambiguous data structures. On the benchmark datasets, VRIF-VPSVM shows strong balance-oriented classification performance, particularly in terms of G-Mean and IBA, and maintains stable behavior across the examined contamination levels. The component and same-decision-function analyses further show that VRIF weighting reshapes the reliability-adjusted loss distribution before variance control, while variance penalization subsequently regulates the dispersion of this distribution. The parameter-sensitivity results also reveal the interaction between mean-loss control, variance penalization, and reliability-aware weighting. The runtime analysis further indicates that the reported model-fitting cost remains practical on the examined larger and higher-dimensional datasets, while the reduced-kernel implementation helps control the optimization cost on relatively large training sets.

Future work will develop a unified generalization analysis that jointly accounts for data-dependent VRIF weighting and classifier estimation, with particular attention to the conditions under which reliability-aware variance control can yield tighter generalization bounds. More efficient kernel approximations and basis-selection strategies will also be explored to improve scalability. In addition, a theoretically principled and computationally efficient multiclass extension of VRIF-VPSVM will be investigated, including multiclass reliability weighting, variance modeling, and optimization.

Appendix B Proofs of the properties of the VRIF-weighted objective

Proof.

Proof of Proposition 3 Recall that

$${\widetilde{\ell }}_{i}={s}_{i}^{{{\rm{VRIF}}}}{\ell }_{i},\qquad \overline{\widetilde{\ell }}=\dfrac{1}{n}{\sum }_{j = 1}^{n}{\widetilde{\ell }}_{j},$$
(B27)

and that the loss-dependent component of the direct variance-penalized objective is

(B29)

For the variance term, define

$$S(\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{\ell }}}})={\sum }_{j = 1}^{n}{\left({\widetilde{\ell }}_{j}-\overline{\widetilde{\ell }}\right)}^{2}.$$
(B30)

Using

$$S(\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{\ell }}}})={\sum }_{j = 1}^{n}{\widetilde{\ell }}_{j}^{2}-n{\overline{\widetilde{\ell }}}^{2},$$
(B31)

$$\dfrac{\partial \overline{\widetilde{\ell }}}{\partial {\widetilde{\ell }}_{i}}=\dfrac{1}{n},$$

2.3 B.3 Proof of lower boundedness and existence

Proof.

Proof of Proposition 4 Consider the direct variance-penalized objective

$$F({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})=\frac{1}{2}{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{T}K{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}+{\lambda }_{1}{\hat{{{ \mathcal R }}}}_{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})+\frac{{\lambda }_{2}}{n}{V}_{n}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}).$$
(B45)

The hinge loss is continuous with respect to α. Since the VRIF memberships are fixed, the weighted empirical risk and weighted empirical variance are also continuous. Therefore, F is continuous.

Because K is symmetric positive semidefinite,

$${{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{T}K{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\ge 0.$$
(B46)

Moreover,

$${\hat{{{ \mathcal R }}}}_{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})\ge 0,\qquad {V}_{n}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})\ge 0.$$
(B47)

Under λ1, λ2 ≥ 0, it follows that

$$F({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})\ge 0.$$
(B48)

Hence, the objective is bounded from below.

It remains to establish that its infimum is attained. Let

$${r}_{K}={{\mbox{rank}}}(K).$$
(B49)

If rK = 0, then K = 0. In this case, both the decision values Kα and the regularization term are independent of α. The objective is therefore constant with respect to α, and every coefficient vector is a global minimizer.

Suppose now that rK > 0. Let the positive-eigenvalue decomposition of K be

$$K={U}_{K}{{\rm\Lambda }}_{K}{U}_{K}^{T},$$
(B50)

where

$${U}_{K}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{n\times {r}_{K}},\qquad {U}_{K}^{T}{U}_{K}=I,$$
(B51)

and

$${{\rm\Lambda }}_{K}={\mathrm{Diag}}({\lambda }_{K,1},\ldots ,{\lambda }_{K,{r}_{K}})\succ 0.$$
(B52)

Define the effective kernel coordinate

$${{\boldsymbol{v}}}={{\rm\Lambda }}_{K}^{1/2}{U}_{K}^{T}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{{r}_{K}}.$$
(B53)

Then

$$\begin{array}{l}{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{T}K{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}={{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{T}{U}_{K}{\Lambda }_{K}{U}_{K}^{T}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\\ ={\left\Vert {\Lambda }_{K}^{1/2}{U}_{K}^{T}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\right\Vert }_{2}^{2}=\parallel {{\boldsymbol{v}}}{\parallel }_{2}^{2},\end{array}$$
(B54)

and

$$\begin{array}{c}K{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}={U}_{K}{{\rm\Lambda }}_{K}{U}_{K}^{T}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\\ ={U}_{K}{{\rm\Lambda }}_{K}^{1/2}{{\boldsymbol{v}}}.\end{array}$$
(B55)

The hinge losses depend on α only through the vector of training decision values Kα. Therefore, the objective can be expressed as a function of v:

$$\hat{F}({{\boldsymbol{v}}})=\frac{1}{2}\parallel {{\boldsymbol{v}}}{\parallel }_{2}^{2}+{\lambda }_{1}{\hat{{{ \mathcal R }}}}_{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{v}}})+\frac{{\lambda }_{2}}{n}{V}_{n}^{{\mathrm{VRIF}}}({{\boldsymbol{v}}}),$$
(B56)

where the losses are evaluated using the decision vector \({U}_{K}{\Lambda }_{K}^{1/2}{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\).

The function \(\hat{F}\) is continuous and satisfies

$$\widehat{F}({{\boldsymbol{v}}})\ge \dfrac{1}{2}\parallel {{\boldsymbol{v}}}{\parallel }_{2}^{2}.$$
(B57)

Consequently,

$$\widehat{F}({{\boldsymbol{v}}})\to +\infty \qquad \,{\mathrm{as}}\,\qquad \parallel {{\boldsymbol{v}}}{\parallel }_{2}\to +\infty .$$
(B58)

Thus, \(\widehat{F}\) is coercive on \({\mathbb{R}}^{{r}_{K}}\). A continuous coercive function on a finite-dimensional Euclidean space attains its global minimum. Therefore, there exists \({{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}^{* }\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{{r}_{K}}\) such that

$$\hat{F}({{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}^{* })={\min }_{{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{{r}_{K}}}\hat{F}({{\boldsymbol{v}}}).$$
(B59)

A corresponding coefficient vector is

$${{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{* }={U}_{K}{{\rm\Lambda }}_{K}^{-1/2}{{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}^{* }.$$
(B60)

Indeed,

$${\Lambda }_{K}^{1/2}{U}_{K}^{T}{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}}^{* }={{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}^{* },$$
(B61)

so α* attains the same objective value as v*. Hence, the original direct variance-penalized objective admits at least one global minimizer.

If K is singular, a vector in the null space of K can be added to α* without changing either Kα* or α*TKα*. Therefore, existence is guaranteed, but uniqueness is not implied. 7D2□

Remark 3

The preceding proof establishes the existence of a global minimizer for the direct variance-penalized objective. For the finite-p smoothed formulation, Proposition 5 provides sufficient conditions for convexity. Under these conditions, every stationary point is a global minimizer; if K ≻ 0, the minimizer is unique.

2.4 B.4 Proof of the conditional convexity result

Proof.

Proof of Proposition 5 From (68), we have

$${{\boldsymbol{\chi }}}=K{{\boldsymbol{a}}}.$$
(B62)

Therefore, the Hessian in (69) can be rewritten as

$$\begin{array}{c}{\nabla }^{2}{F}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})=K+K{{\mathrm{Diag}}}({{\boldsymbol{\omega }}})K-{c}_{\lambda }K{{\boldsymbol{a}}}{{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}^{T}K\\ =K+K[{{\mathrm{Diag}}}({{\boldsymbol{\omega }}})-{c}_{\lambda }{{\boldsymbol{a}}}{{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}^{T}]K.\end{array}$$
(B63)

For any \({{\boldsymbol{v}}}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}\),

$$v^T \nabla^2 F_p(\alpha) v = v^T K v + (Kv)^T [{Diag}(\omega) - c_\lambda aa^T](Kv).$$
(B64)

By assumption, both matrices appearing in the two quadratic terms are positive semidefinite. Hence,

$${{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}^{T}{\nabla }^{2}{F}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}){{\boldsymbol{v}}}\ge 0$$
(B65)

for every v and every α ∈ Ω. Thus,

$${\nabla }^{2}{F}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}})\succcurlyeq 0,\qquad {{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}\in {\rm\Omega },$$

and Fp is convex on Ω.

If K ≻ 0, then for every nonzero v,

$${{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}^{T}K{{\boldsymbol{v}}} > 0.$$

It follows from (B64) that

$${{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}^{T}{{\rm\nabla }}^{2}{F}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\alpha }}}){{\boldsymbol{v}}} > 0,$$

so ∇2Fp(α) ≻ 0 and Fp is strictly convex. □
