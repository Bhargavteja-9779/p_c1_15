Abstract

The combination of class imbalance and class overlap represents one of the most critical challenges in machine learning. While early studies identified class overlap as a key factor that aggravates classification challenges in imbalanced domains, subsequent research has largely examined class imbalance and class overlap separately. As a result, their combined effect in multi-class scenarios remains insufficiently explored. Moreover, the lack of a unified investigation into overlap-mitigating data-level approaches limits their systematic evaluation. To address this gap, this paper presents a unified and structured overview of imbalanced data classification from the perspective of class overlap, with a particular focus on multi-class scenarios. We begin by revisiting the fundamental definitions and implications of both phenomena and highlighting their joint impact on classifier performance. We then provide a structured synthesis of data-level preprocessing methods, particularly sampling strategies (oversampling, undersampling, and hybrid sampling), as well as feature selection approaches, to elucidate their respective roles in mitigating challenges caused by class overlap. Finally, we discuss open challenges and outline promising future directions for multi-class imbalanced learning under overlapping class distributions.

1 Introduction

The presence of imbalanced data has become a significant challenge in the field of machine learning and data mining, particularly in real-world applications such as medical diagnosis (Abdoh et al. 2018; Gupta et al. 2021; Suresh et al. 2023), fraud detection (Forough and Momtazi 2022; Huang et al. 2024; Singh et al. 2022), and anomaly detection (Gao et al. 2024; Jeffrey et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2023; Xue and Zhu 2024; Zubair and Yoon 2022). In these scenarios, the number of samples in one class (the majority or negative class) significantly outnumbers those in the other class or classes (the minority or positive class), resulting in the data imbalance. Such imbalance leads to biased learning, as most traditional classifiers favor the majority class and fail to correctly identify minority instances, which are often of greater practical importance (Krawczyk 2016). Consequently, addressing data imbalance has become an essential step toward improving classification reliability and decision-making accuracy.

While most early studies have focused on binary imbalanced classification, many real-world problems involve multi-class imbalanced data, where multiple minority and majority classes coexist simultaneously (Yang et al. 2024a). In such cases, the relationships between classes become more intricate, and imbalance between different class pairs may vary considerably. This complexity introduces additional challenges, as methods designed for binary imbalance often fail to generalize effectively to multi-class settings. As a result, research on multi-class imbalanced learning has gained increasing attention, aiming to design algorithms capable of handling imbalance among multiple classes.

Multi-class imbalanced classification presents greater challenges than binary-class classification. This is primarily due to the presence of severe class overlap problems, as noted by Krawczyk (2016); García et al. (2006); Santos et al. (2023), where instances from different classes exhibit remarkably similar characteristics in the feature space. Indeed, classifier performance degradation is often attributed more to class overlap than to class imbalance itself (Krawczyk 2016; Sleeman and Krawczyk 2021; Lango et al. 2025). When classes exhibit similar characteristics within the same region, distinguishing between them becomes more challenging. This issue is particularly pronounced in multi-class imbalance scenarios, where the intricate relationships between different classes lead to more complex class overlap.

Existing review papers have extensively examined the adverse effects of data imbalance and class overlap, as shown in Table 1. The studies in Santos et al. (2023) and Santos et al. (2022) treat class overlap as the overarching challenge in imbalanced learning and mainly focus on proposing or comparing overlap measurement criteria. Although these works highlight the importance of overlap, they offer no systematic synthesis of methods for mitigating overlap, nor do they extend their analyses to multi-class settings where overlap patterns become substantially more complex. Some reviews (Kumar et al. 2024; Pradipta et al. 2021b) discuss approaches aimed at mitigating class overlap in imbalanced classification; however, they do not differentiate between binary and multi-class formulations, thereby failing to establish a unified framework for addressing multi-class challenges from the perspective of overlap mitigation. In contrast, other reviews have primarily focused on summarizing methods developed to address the class imbalance problem itself (Rezvani and Wang 2023; Altalhan et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2024; Carvalho et al. 2025), with overlap typically treated only implicitly or not at all. These studies seldom consider class overlap as a critical factor contributing to classifier performance degradation and largely overlook how overlap-related data characteristics influence the effectiveness of imbalance-handling algorithms. Moreover, most of these reviews center their discussions on binary imbalanced classification, providing only limited attention to multi-class imbalance scenarios, where class overlap and distributional complexity further exacerbate the learning difficulty.

Given the complexity of imbalanced data classification, particularly in multi-class scenarios where class overlap interacts with skewed class distributions, this review introduces a unified perspective that has been largely absent from existing surveys. Rather than treating imbalance and overlap as independent issues, the review positions class overlap as a structural factor that fundamentally shapes classifier behavior and affects the effectiveness of imbalance-handling strategies. Building on this theoretical viewpoint, the article presents a comprehensive synthesis of methods developed to address imbalanced data classification from the perspective of class overlap. Unlike prior reviews that focus exclusively on overlap measurement, overlap mitigation in binary-class scenarios, or general imbalance-handling techniques, this work provides a structured examination of how class overlap can be addressed in both binary and multi-class imbalanced settings, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of imbalance-handling methods. Specifically, the review focuses on data-level preprocessing approaches, highlighting how feature selection and sampling techniques (oversampling, undersampling, and hybrid sampling) help mitigate challenges caused by class overlap. Data-level strategies are commonly used as they act independently from specific classifiers, providing flexible and effective methods for addressing class imbalance and overlap without altering the underlying learning algorithms. In contrast, algorithm-level techniques, like cost-sensitive learning and ensemble-based methods, either involve increased model complexity or require classifier-specific modifications, thereby excluding them from the scope of this review. The main contributions of this review are summarized as follows:

  1. (1)

    We systematically analyze the joint impact of class imbalance and class overlap, highlighting how overlap further complicates imbalanced classification.

  2. (2)

    We provide a comprehensive survey of feature selection and sampling methods (oversampling, undersampling, hybrid) and show how addressing class overlap helps mitigate imbalance.

  3. (3)

    We extend the discussion to multi-class imbalanced problems, providing insights into addressing multi-class imbalance and overlap from an overlap-aware perspective.

  4. (4)

    We outline novel theoretical insights and propose future research directions, underscoring the pivotal role of class overlap in imbalanced classification, especially in multi-class scenarios.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the fundamentals of imbalanced data classification, including its definition, challenges, basic methods and evaluation metrics. Section 3 discusses the specific challenge of class overlap, covering its definition, impact, and evaluation. Section 4 reviews feature selection methods for imbalanced data classification. Section 5 summarizes existing sampling approaches for mitigating class overlap in imbalanced learning. Section 6 presents a discussion and outlines future research directions, and Section 7 concludes the paper.

2 Imbalanced data classification

Imbalanced data classification has emerged as a critical issue in contemporary machine learning. In such scenarios, sample distributions across classes are often highly skewed, leading traditional classifiers to favor the majority class. This section provides an overview of imbalanced data classification, covering its definition, challenges in multi-class scenarios, commonly used methods, and evaluation metrics.

2.1 Definition

In imbalanced scenarios, one or more classes contain substantially fewer samples than others, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The classification of imbalanced data can be broadly categorized into binary and multi-class scenarios. In binary datasets, imbalance is typically measured using the imbalance ratio (IR), which is defined as the ratio of the majority class size to the minority class size and is calculated as \(IR = \frac{N_{\max }}{N_{\min }}\) (He and Garcia 2009). Here, \(N_{\max }\) and \(N_{\min }\) represent the number of instances in the majority and minority classes, respectively. A higher IR indicates greater imbalance, posing significant challenges for classification models.

In binary classification, imbalance is commonly quantified by the ratio between the majority and minority class sizes (He and Garcia 2009). In contrast, multi-class imbalanced data extends beyond two classes, where the imbalance may vary across multiple classes. Currently, various definitions of the imbalance ratio have been proposed for multi-class imbalanced data, as shown in Table 2. Although various definitions have been proposed, the Maximum/Minimum Class Imbalance Ratio (MIR) remains the most widely adopted metric (Krawczyk et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2022; Ma et al. 2023). This metric provides a straightforward and intuitive measure of the most extreme imbalance within the dataset. By focusing on the ratio between the largest and smallest class sizes, MIR highlights the most severe imbalance affecting classification performance. Additionally, this ratio is simple to compute and interpret, making it a widely adopted choice in the literature despite the availability of more complex definitions.

2.2 Challenges for multi-class imbalanced data

Most machine learning models, such as K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), and Naive Bayes (NB), are typically designed to maximize overall accuracy, which may not be ideal for imbalanced datasets (Krawczyk 2016; Guo et al. 2017; Johnson and Khoshgoftaar 2019). This occurs because, in imbalanced datasets, these models tend to prioritize the majority class, as correctly classifying its instances contributes more to the overall accuracy. During training, the model is exposed to significantly more instances of the majority class, causing it to overfit for this class while underfitting the minority class. This issue is further amplified in cases of extreme class imbalance, where the minority class becomes so underrepresented that the model struggles to effectively learn and generalize its underlying patterns. However, minority classes are often of primary interest in imbalanced datasets (Guo et al. 2017). When the classifier disproportionately favors the majority class, it overlooks patterns essential for identifying minority instances, resulting in poor generalization and reduced practical utility. Therefore, it is crucial to improve the classifier’s ability to recognize minority classes in imbalanced data classification and ensure the model can make accurate and meaningful predictions for all classes.

Multi-class imbalanced problems pose significantly greater challenges compared to binary cases due to the complex relationships among classes. Unlike binary classification scenarios, where the focus is typically on distinguishing between two classes (e.g., diseased vs. non-diseased), multi-class datasets often involve the classification of multiple conditions simultaneously (Yang et al. 2024a). An early taxonomy of multi-class imbalance defined “multi-minority” cases, which have one majority class and multiple minority classes, and “multi-majority” cases, which have multiple majority classes and a single minority class (Wang and Yao 2012). The experimental results indicate that both “multi-minority” and “multi-majority” cases negatively impact overall and minority class performance, with performance deteriorating as the number of imbalanced classes increases. Moreover, the “multi-majority” scenario is more harmful to generalization performance than the “multi-minority” case, due to a more severe imbalance rate.

Subsequent studies showed that a class may be majority relative to one class, minority relative to another, and balanced with a third, further complicating imbalanced classification (Krawczyk 2016), as shown in Fig. 2. This exacerbates the challenge of achieving a balanced performance across all classes, as improving performance in some classes can lead to a decline in others (Krawczyk et al. 2019). Consequently, misclassification errors may occur more frequently in underrepresented classes, potentially leading to critical instances being overlooked or incorrectly handled. Therefore, analyzing inter-class relationships is essential for developing effective strategies to address multi-class imbalance (Krawczyk et al. 2019; Ma et al. 2023).

In recent years, researchers have recognized that the imbalance ratio is not the sole factor contributing to learning difficulties. Beyond the class imbalance distribution, the intrinsic characteristics of data can significantly impact classification performance. These data difficulty factors, including overlapping, noise, outliers, and small disjuncts, interact with class imbalance, further exacerbating the challenges faced by classification models (Koziarski et al. 2020; Stefanowski 2015). These difficult types of samples are shown in Fig. 3. Class overlap occurs in regions of the feature space that contain instances from multiple classes, where class boundaries are poorly separated and overlap. Noise may arise from errors in data collection or labeling processes, while outliers represent extreme samples within the data distribution. Small disjuncts refers to the presence of small, isolated subclusters of minority class samples in the feature space, which are difficult for classifiers to effectively learn due to their limited sample size (Pradipta et al. 2021b).

Among these factors, class overlap is one of the most critical challenges, as overlapping regions make minority samples difficult to distinguish. A study highlighted that class overlap significantly degrades classifier performance, particularly in imbalanced datasets, where minority class samples are more likely to be overshadowed by the majority class (Vuttipittayamongkol et al. 2021). Subsequent studies (Dai et al. 2024; Lu et al. 2024; Mayabadi and Saadatfar 2022) further demonstrated that the interaction between class overlap and class imbalance exacerbates misclassification of the minority class, making it a critical factor influencing the classification of imbalanced data.

While class overlap exists in both binary and multi-class imbalanced scenarios, they are significantly amplified in multi-class settings. In multi-class imbalanced scenarios, interactions among multiple classes introduce a more complex dynamic. Class overlap becomes multidimensional, involving multiple classes rather than a single majority-minority pair, thereby complicating boundary learning (Ma et al. 2023; Dai et al. 2024). Consequently, classifiers struggle to distinguish between multiple classes, leading to increased misclassification rates and overall degraded performance. Hence, characterizing inter-class relationships is crucial for addressing class overlap in multi-class imbalanced classification.

2.3 Basic methods for binary-class imbalanced learning

Over the past decade, numerous methods have been proposed to address binary-class imbalanced data classification. These methods can be broadly classified into two main groups: data-level methods and algorithm-level methods. The former focuses on balancing the class distribution, while the latter adjusts the learning algorithm or classifier without altering the training set to enhance learning toward the minority class (Thabtah et al. 2020). Data-level methods typically employ sampling strategies such as undersampling, oversampling, or a hybrid of both to mitigate class imbalance, while algorithm-level methods primarily include cost-sensitive learning and ensemble-based methods (Fernández et al. 2013; Thabtah et al. 2020).

2.3.1 Data-level methods

  • (1) Undersampling Undersampling is a preprocessing method that creates a subset of the original dataset to balance the dataset by eliminating a portion of the majority class samples (Kaur et al. 2019), as shown in Fig. 4. An early study proposed a method called Random Undersampling (RUS) (Kotsiantis et al. 2006), which balances the dataset by arbitrarily eliminating majority class samples. In a study (Gupta et al. 2021), RUS was employed to alleviate class imbalance in a medical dataset for diagnosing breast cancer. The results revealed significant enhancements in classification accuracy and other performance metrics, attesting to the utility of undersampling methods in handling imbalanced data. However, a key limitation of this method is the potential loss of informative samples, which may negatively impact the classifier’s performance in distinguishing between classes (Guo et al. 2008). Subsequent studies (Fan et al. 2016; Devi et al. 2017; Vuttipittayamongkol and Elyan 2020a, c) have proposed various improved undersampling methods to address imbalanced data classification. A study explored the application of the overlap-based undersampling method to address class imbalance in medical datasets for predicting diagnostics of diseases (Vuttipittayamongkol and Elyan 2020c). Experimental results show the achievement of high accuracy in the positive class, which is highly preferable in the medical domain. A comprehensive comparison was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of various undersampling methods in predicting the presence of lung cancer (Khushi et al. 2021). Although these undersampling methods exhibit less stability when contrasted with oversampling methods, it nonetheless contributes to enhancing the AUC value of classification to a certain degree. In addition, these approaches must carefully balance the trade-off between reducing class imbalance and minimizing information loss, as excessive sample removal may degrade classifier performance. However, undersampling inevitably risks information loss, as removing majority class instances may discard critical samples that encode essential distribution patterns or decision boundaries. To mitigate information loss, researchers have proposed hybrid frameworks such as ensemble undersampling (Liu et al. 2009; Krawczyk et al. 2016) and dynamic resampling (Liu et al. 2023). These methods generate multiple balanced subsets or adaptively adjust sampling rates based on local data complexity, thereby preserving global distributional properties while addressing class imbalance. Nevertheless, a residual risk of information loss persists. In fields like medical data classification, undersampling is often inadvisable due to the increased risk of discarding critical cases (e.g., rare diseases or atypical symptoms), which can undermine diagnostic accuracy and negatively impact patient outcomes (Guo et al. 2017).

  • (2) Oversampling Oversampling aims to balance datasets by replicating minority samples or generating synthetic instances based on minority class data characteristics (Kaur et al. 2019). Figure 5 illustrates a balanced dataset with a 1:1 ratio of majority to minority class samples; in practice, the distribution may not always conform to this idealized ratio. For instance, an oversampling method (Zhu et al. 2023) was applied only to classes with an imbalance ratio greater than 1.2 and continued until the ratio was reduced to 1.2 or below, resulting in a dataset that remained partially imbalanced rather than achieving a strict 1:1 class ratio. In random oversampling, minority class instances are randomly duplicated until they achieve equal representation with the majority class. However, this method may lead to overfitting, as the replicated samples do not introduce new information but instead reinforce existing patterns, potentially reducing the model’s generalization ability (Thabtah et al. 2020). To mitigate this issue, researchers have introduced more advanced methods that generate synthetic samples rather than simply duplicating existing ones. One of the most influential methods is the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) (Chawla et al. 2002). Unlike random oversampling, which merely duplicates minority class instances and is prone to overfitting, SMOTE generates synthetic samples to better represent the minority class. These synthetic samples are generated through interpolation between selected minority class instances within a specified neighborhood. A synthetic sample syn can be generated as follows:

    $$\begin{aligned} \text {syn} = x_{\min } + \gamma \cdot (x_{\min }' - x_{\min }) \end{aligned}$$
    (1)

    where \(x_{\min }\) is the feature vector of the minority instance selected for oversampling. \(x_{\min }'\) is a Candidate Assistant Reference (CAR) sample, indicating that the sample \(\text {syn}\) is created between \(x_{\min }\) and \(x_{\min }'\). \(\gamma \) is a constant vector where each value is a random number between zero and one. For this reason, this method operates within the “feature space” rather than the “data space,” meaning the algorithm relies on feature values and their interrelations rather than treating data points as independent entities. This has also prompted an in-depth analysis of the theoretical relationship between original and synthetic instances, considering factors such as data dimensionality (Fernández et al. 2018b). However, by interpolating between existing minority class samples, SMOTE may generate synthetic instances within class overlap regions, potentially increasing overlap and further complicating the decision boundary regions. This limitation is particularly pronounced in multi-class scenarios due to the intricate relationships among classes. In such contexts, decision boundary regions often consist of samples from multiple classes interwoven together, rather than a simple separation between a single majority and a single minority class. As a result, interpolating minority class samples near these complex boundaries increases the risk of generating synthetic instances that extend into the feature space of other classes, thereby misleading the classifier during training and ultimately degrading classification performance (Chen et al. 2024). Building upon SMOTE, several variations have been developed to further enhance oversampling effectiveness, with representative methods including Adaptive Synthetic Sampling (ADASYN) (He et al. 2008), Borderline Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (Borderline-SMOTE) (Han et al. 2005), Safe-Level Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (Safe-Level-SMOTE) (Bunkhumpornpat et al. 2009), Density-Based Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (DBSMOTE) (Bunkhumpornpat et al. 2012), and Modified Weighted Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (MWMOTE) (Barua et al. 2014). However, due to the limitations of these oversampling methods, they may also introduce new problems, such as exacerbating class overlap and noise issues (Shao and Yan 2023; Wang et al. 2024b). While SMOTE and its variants are widely used oversampling approaches, oversampling is not limited to these methods. Alternative techniques, such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) (Ding et al. 2024; Dong et al. 2021; Guan et al. 2024; Su et al. 2024), have also been explored to generate synthetic samples to improve classification performance. Unlike undersampling, which reduces the dataset size by removing majority class samples, oversampling preserves the completeness of the original dataset, ensuring that no critical patterns are lost. By increasing the representation of the minority class, oversampling enhances the classifier’s ability to identify rare but meaningful patterns. Furthermore, many oversampling techniques, such as SMOTE and its variants, offer fine-grained control over the generation of synthetic samples, allowing for domain-specific adaptations to better address class imbalance.

  • (3) Hybrid Sampling Hybrid sampling methods combine undersampling and oversampling to achieve balanced class distributions (Kaur et al. 2019). As shown in Fig. 6, this method enhances class balance by applying undersampling to remove samples and oversampling to generate synthetic samples, thereby reducing the number of majority class samples and increasing the minority class samples to a predefined cardinality. Hybrid sampling methods were introduced to overcome the limitations of both undersampling and oversampling (Qian et al. 2014). These methods were designed based on two key considerations: (1) Undersampling, while effective in reducing class imbalance, risks discarding informative instances, potentially leading to a loss of critical data patterns. (2) Oversampling, particularly when generating a large number of synthetic samples, increases the risk of overfitting by reinforcing existing patterns rather than introducing genuinely novel information. By combining both techniques, hybrid methods aim to mitigate these drawbacks, preserving valuable instances while preventing excessive model bias towards synthetic data. Subsequent research has explored various hybrid sampling strategies to enhance classification performance, such as SMOTE + OSS (Pristyanto et al. 2018), SMOTE + Tomek links (Swana et al. 2022), and SMOTE + ENN (Krawczyk 2016). Several studies have demonstrated that hybrid sampling methods can effectively address imbalanced data classification tasks. For instance, a hybrid sampling approach combining oversampling and undersampling methods was employed to address class imbalance in a medical dataset (Bach et al. 2017). Experiments have shown that 300% oversampling using the synthetic minority oversampling method, combined with an undersampling method, gives the required classification accuracy. A hybrid sampling strategy has been investigated to mitigate class imbalance in medical datasets, particularly for predicting breast cancer risk (Junsomboon and Phienthrakul 2017). The experimental results indicate that the combined undersampling and oversampling methods led to an enhanced recall value, implying improved accuracy in predicting minority class classifications. Additionally, the utilization of a hybrid sampling method has been applied to address class imbalance in eight medical datasets (Fujiwara et al. 2020). The research revealed significant enhancements in model performance, underscoring its effectiveness in enhancing predictive capabilities. Although hybrid sampling methods have received considerable academic attention, their practical adoption remains limited compared to standalone methods (Guo et al. 2017). In many empirical studies, traditional methods, such as SMOTE for oversampling and random undersampling, are favored for their simplicity, lower compute cost, and ease of parameter tuning (Maldonado et al. 2019; Bach et al. 2019). In contrast, the integration of both methods in hybrid sampling introduces additional complexity, which may inadvertently compound the inherent drawbacks of each technique (e.g., overfitting from synthetic data generation in oversampling and potential loss of informative instances in undersampling). An experimental comparison of 85 SMOTE variants was conducted, including hybrid sampling methods such as SMOTE-ENN and SMOTE-TomekLinks (Kovács 2019). The results demonstrated that, across different classifiers and evaluation metrics, the top-performing oversampling methods were typically standalone oversampling methods rather than hybrid sampling approaches. Consequently, while hybrid strategies can offer performance gains in specific contexts, these gains are not universally observed, thereby limiting their widespread application in classification tasks.

2.3.2 Algorithm-level methods

Algorithm-level methods mainly include cost-sensitive learning and ensemble methods, which aim to improve model performance by modifying the learning algorithm to account for class imbalance.

  • (1) Cost-sensitive learning Traditional classification algorithms typically assume that the misclassification cost is the same for all response variable values. However, the misclassification cost may differ across target class values in real-world applications (Thabtah et al. 2020; Chawla 2010). For instance, in the field of medical diagnosis, incorrectly predicting that a patient has a serious condition when they are actually healthy may result in unnecessary medical procedures and psychological distress. On the other hand, misclassifying a patient as disease-free when they actually have a life-threatening illness can delay critical treatment, potentially leading to severe health complications or mortality. In such cases, the high cost of misclassification necessitates that the classifier be tailored to the specific application and account for the associated misclassification costs (Thabtah et al. 2020; Araf et al. 2024). To deal with this problem, cost-sensitive learning was proposed. Cost-sensitive learning employs a cost matrix to incorporate varying misclassification costs for each available class. These misclassification cost values may be provided by domain experts or estimated through alternative methods (López et al. 2013). The cost matrix is shown in Table 3, where \(\lambda _{ij}\) denotes the misclassification cost of assigning a sample from true class j to class i. The objective of this method is to reduce misclassification costs by selecting the class with the lowest conditional risk (Thabtah et al. 2020). Cost-sensitive learning can be generally categorized into direct methods and meta-learning methods (Johnson and Khoshgoftaar 2019; Araf et al. 2024). The direct methods integrate misclassification costs directly into the learning algorithm during model training (Araf et al. 2024; Feng et al. 2020). In contrast, the meta-learning methods do not modify the learning algorithm itself but instead enhances cost sensitivity by adjusting the training data (preprocessing) or modifying the model’s outputs (postprocessing) (Liu et al. 2021). Popular preprocessing methods include instance weighting using a cost matrix and MetaCost, which adjusts training labels based on misclassification costs. Postprocessing techniques, meanwhile, typically involve modifying the decision thresholds according to the predefined misclassification costs (Araf et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2021). However, compared to sampling methods, cost-sensitive approaches are less commonly used due to the challenge of defining misclassification costs, which are not directly available from the data and can be difficult to set accurately (Kaur et al. 2019; Krawczyk et al. 2014).

  • (2) Ensemble methods In recent years, ensemble-based classifiers have emerged as a potential solution to the class imbalance issue (Dai et al. 2024; Krawczyk et al. 2014; Rodriguez et al. 2020; Edward and Rosli 2021). It combines multiple classifiers to obtain a stronger classifier that outperforms each individual classifier (Thabtah et al. 2020). Each individual classifier is based on specific assumptions and has distinct characteristics. By combining these classifiers, the individual variances are averaged, resulting in a reduction in the overall variance of the ensemble, thereby enhancing its generalization capability and performance (López et al. 2013). Subsequently, the outputs of these classifiers are aggregated to determine the final classification decision. For instance, Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) (Freund and Schapire 1997) is an ensemble learning algorithm that combines multiple weak classifiers to form a strong classifier. The key idea behind AdaBoost is to iteratively train weak classifiers, with each new classifier focusing on the misclassified instances from the previous one. During each iteration, the algorithm adjusts the weights of incorrectly classified instances, giving more importance to those that are hard to classify. The final prediction is then made by combining the weighted predictions of all individual classifiers. Ensemble-based methods integrate ensemble learning algorithms with one of the methods previously discussed, such as data-level methods or cost-sensitive learning solutions. When integrating a data-level method, the resulting hybrid method typically involves preprocessing the data prior to training individual classifiers within the ensemble. For example, SMOTEBoost (Chawla et al. 2003) integrates SMOTE with AdaBoost to address class imbalance by generating synthetic minority samples during each boosting iteration. It aims to enhance the classification accuracy of the minority class while preserving the overall accuracy of the dataset. In contrast, cost-sensitive ensemble methods focus on optimizing the cost-minimization process through the ensemble algorithm itself, rather than adapting the base classifiers to directly incorporate cost considerations during their learning phase (Araf et al. 2024; López et al. 2013). Following the AdaBoost framework, several cost-sensitive ensemble variations have been developed, such as AdaC1, AdaC2, and AdaC3 (Sun et al. 2007), which integrate misclassification costs directly into the learning process. For instance, AdaC2 (Sun et al. 2007) modifies the weight update rule by multiplying misclassification costs with error rates, prioritizing samples with higher misclassification penalties. Ensemble methods leverage the diversity of base learners to reduce variance and bias, often achieving superior performance in complex classification tasks. However, their practical application in imbalanced classification is limited due to several challenges. First, they often require substantial computational resources and training time due to the need to build and combine multiple models, which can be prohibitive in time-sensitive applications (Naderalvojoud and Hernandez-Boussard 2023; Rane et al. 2024). Additionally, many ensemble approaches, especially those incorporating cost-sensitive learning, rely on predefined misclassification costs, which are often difficult to quantify accurately in imbalanced classification settings (Kachuee et al. 2019; Karapanagiotis et al. 2021). Hence, despite their potential benefits, ensemble methods remain limited in practical imbalanced classification settings.

2.4 Basic methods for multi-class imbalanced learning

Multi-class learning is considered a challenging task due to the complex relationships among classes, where improving the recognition of one class may degrade the performance of others Santos et al. (2023). These challenges are further intensified by data difficulty factors such as class overlap, noise, and outliers (Koziarski et al. 2020; Stefanowski 2015). It has received increasing attention in recent years due to its practical significance in real-world applications. Existing methods for multi-class imbalance can be broadly categorized into two groups: binarization methods and ad hoc methods (Krawczyk et al. 2019; Guo et al. 2017).

2.4.1 Binarization methods

Traditional binary-class imbalance methods fail to directly address multi-class imbalance due to the complex relationships between classes. To adapt these methods to multi-class scenarios while maintaining their flexibility and widespread applicability, researchers have developed binarization solutions that decompose the original multi-class problem into multiple binary classification tasks, allowing imbalance-handling strategies to be applied individually to each subproblem (Krawczyk et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2022; Koziarski et al. 2020). One-versus-One (OvO) and One-versus-All (OvA) are the two most commonly used binarization strategies. OvO decomposes a multi-class problem with N classes into \(N(N-1)/2\) binary classification tasks, whereas OvA transforms it into N independent binary classification tasks (Krawczyk et al. 2019; Guo et al. 2017). Each decomposed task is then addressed using an appropriate binary imbalanced learning method. The OvO and OvA binarization strategies are shown in Figs. 7 and 8, respectively.

Although OvA and OvO allow binary solutions to be applied to multi-class problems, they disrupt the original structure of multi-class datasets. By decomposing the problem into multiple independent binary tasks, they ignore the inherent relationships among classes, potentially leading to inconsistencies in decision boundaries and a loss of inter-class information.

In recent years, researchers have proposed novel binarization strategies to mitigate the loss of multi-class data information. A binarization method called One-versus-Some (OvS) has been introduced in recent studies to address multi-class imbalance (Krawczyk et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2022; Koziarski et al. 2020). Unlike traditional binarization approaches such as OvA and OvO, OvS constructs binary classification tasks by dynamically defining majority and minority classes based on the class distribution. Specifically, OvS first sorts the classes in descending order based on the number of associated instances. The class with the highest number of instances is designated as the majority class. For each of the remaining classes, a composite majority class is constructed by sampling a fraction of instances from the already considered classes. In this way, instead of decomposing the problem into independent binary tasks, the majority class is constructed dynamically by combining a fraction of instances from multiple classes. This strategy avoids fully separating the original class distributions and preserves part of the inherent inter-class relationships, thereby maintaining the structural coherence of the data. A binarization strategy called One-versus-Ensemble (OvE) has been introduced to address class imbalance by iteratively treating each class, excluding the one with the largest number of instances, as the minority class (Koziarski et al. 2020). Specifically, OvE binarizes the multi-class problem by forming binary tasks where the selected minority class is oversampled while all other classes collectively serve as the majority class. By maintaining the largest class as a stable reference while sequentially balancing the others, OvE preserves the hierarchical relationships among classes and prevents the dominance of any single class from distorting the overall structure.

The OvS and OvE mitigate class structure disruption to some extent, thereby reducing the loss of inter-class information. By incorporating oversampling within the binarization process, OvS and OvE aim to preserve the relative distribution of classes while addressing multi-class imbalance. However, binarization inevitably fragments the multi-class structure and cannot fully preserve complex inter-class relationships. Consequently, despite their improvements over traditional OvA and OvO methods, OvS and OvE still face limitations in maintaining the integrity of multi-class information (Yang et al. 2024b; Abokadr et al. 2023). In fact, class decomposition is not always necessary for addressing multi-class imbalance. Related studies (Guo et al. 2017; Wang and Yao 2012) have demonstrated that integrating class decomposition with class imbalance methods without a comprehensive consideration of the global class distribution is unwise. In contrast, directly learning from the entire multi-class dataset without relying on decomposition strategies is sufficient for multi-class imbalance classification.

2.4.2 Ad hoc methods

Multi-class ad hoc methods are specifically designed to address multi-class imbalanced learning problems without decomposing the original multi-class structure into binary subproblems. Unlike binarization methods, which rely on transforming the multi-class problem into multiple binary tasks, ad hoc methods directly operate on the multi-class dataset (Sleeman and Krawczyk 2021; Ma et al. 2023). These methods modify either the learning algorithm or the data distribution to enhance minority class performance. Similar to binary-class methods, multi-class ad hoc methods can also be classified into two categories: algorithm-level methods and data-level methods. Among data-level methods, the most widely used strategies include extending SMOTE to handle multiple classes (Sáez et al. 2016; Zhu et al. 2017), integrating feature selection techniques (Cao et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2021), and employing alternative instance generation methods based on the Mahalanobis distance (Abdi and Hashemi 2016; Yang et al. 2018). On the other hand, algorithm-level solutions include cost-sensitive learning (Kim et al. 2016), ensemble solutions employing bagging (Sun et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024a), and boosting (Krawczyk et al. 2019).

In recent study (Wang et al. 2024c) has conducted a systematic mapping of techniques, challenges, and dataset types in multi-class imbalanced data classification. The findings indicate that among the challenges in multi-class imbalanced classification, the class overlap problem has received the most attention and has been extensively investigated. Furthermore, oversampling is the most commonly employed strategy to address data difficulty factors that exacerbate multi-class imbalance. In contrast, algorithm-level methods primarily focus on improving classifier accuracy and mitigating training biases. This reveals that, since data difficulty factors contribute to the severity of multi-class imbalance, oversampling remains a promising method for addressing such challenges. Moreover, several studies (Liu et al. 2009; Zhu et al. 2023; Kovács 2019) have revealed that data-level methods are widely applied in multi-class imbalanced classification due to several advantages over algorithm-level methods: 1) These methods are generally easy to implement and apply, as they operate directly on the data without modifying the underlying classification algorithm; 2) by enhancing the quality of imbalanced data, these methods can improve the performance of any classification model. Extensive empirical studies have demonstrated the broad effectiveness of oversampling and undersampling techniques (Sleeman and Krawczyk 2021; Ma et al. 2023; Koziarski et al. 2020; Zheng et al. 2022); and 3) they are independent of specific classifiers and can be seamlessly integrated with algorithm-level methods to develop sophisticated and competitive hybrid methods.

2.5 Evaluation metrics for imbalanced data

In imbalanced data classification, accuracy is not suitable as an evaluation metric due to its bias toward majority classes (Chen et al. 2024). To address this issue, various performance metrics have been proposed, focusing on different aspects of classifier performance. These metrics can be broadly categorized into those designed for binary classification and those adapted for multi-class imbalanced problems.

2.5.1 Binary-class evaluation metrics

In binary classification, evaluation metrics are commonly derived from the confusion matrix, which offers a detailed analysis of the model’s predictions in relation to the actual class labels. The confusion matrix provides a summary of the counts for True Positives (TP), False Positives (FP), False Negatives (FN), and True Negatives (TN), with each sample being classified into one of these four possible outcomes (Kaur et al. 2019), as shown in Table 4. Each possible outcome is defined as follows:

  1. (1)

    True Positives (TP): The model correctly classifies a positive (minority) sample as positive.

  2. (2)

    False Positives (FP): The model incorrectly classifies a negative (majority) sample as positive.

  3. (3)

    False Negatives (FN): The model incorrectly classifies a positive sample as negative.

  4. (4)

    True Negatives (TN): The model correctly classifies a negative sample as negative.

Following the confusion matrix, the most commonly used evaluation metrics have been developed to assess classification performance (Chen et al. 2024; Kaur et al. 2019; Araf et al. 2024), as summarized in Table 5. The section below describes each evaluation metric.

  • (1) Sensitivity / Recall: Sensitivity, also referred to as Recall or the True Positive Rate (TPR), measures the proportion of actual positive instances correctly identified by the model. In medical diagnosis, a high sensitivity value is particularly important as it reduces the likelihood of false negatives (FN), ensuring that individuals with the condition are accurately identified.

  • (2) Specificity: Specificity, also known as the True Negative Rate (TNR), measures a model’s ability to accurately classify negative instances. Along with sensitivity, specificity plays a crucial role in evaluating the overall performance of a classification model. In some applications, these two metrics are considered together to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of predictive accuracy.

  • (3) Precision: Precision measures the proportion of correctly predicted positive instances among all predicted positives. It reflects the reliability of a model’s positive classifications by assessing how many of the identified positive instances are actually true positives (TP). Precision and sensitivity often exhibit a trade-off, where an increase in one may lead to a reduction in the other. Precision is particularly important in minimizing false positives, which is essential for ensuring the efficiency and reliability of the classification model.

  • (4) AUC: The Area Under the Curve (AUC) is a crucial metric for evaluating a model’s ability to differentiate between positive and negative instances. It ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating stronger discriminative power. Due to its ability to measure a model’s robustness independent of a specific threshold, AUC is widely employed in medical diagnostics and other critical applications where reliable classification is essential.

  • (5) G-mean: The Geometric Mean (G-Mean) is a widely used evaluation metric that assesses a model’s overall performance by integrating both sensitivity and specificity. It is particularly suitable for imbalanced classification tasks, as it ensures a balanced evaluation across both majority and minority classes. By capturing the trade-off between correctly identifying positive instances and minimizing false negatives, G-Mean provides a more holistic measure of a classifier’s effectiveness.

  • (6) F-measure: The F-measure, also known as the F1-score, is a widely used metric that integrates precision and sensitivity to provide a comprehensive assessment of a classifier’s performance. By harmonizing these two metrics, the F-measure ensures a balanced evaluation of the model’s ability to correctly identify positive instances while minimizing false negatives.

2.5.2 Multi-class evaluation metrics

While the evaluation metrics for binary classification effectively assess model performance in binary-class problems, they are not directly applicable to multi-class classification due to the increased complexity of class relationships. In binary classification, these metrics primarily rely on pairwise class distinctions, where samples are categorized as either positive or negative. However, in multi-class scenarios, the presence of more than two classes necessitates evaluation metrics that extend beyond simple pairwise comparisons, as traditional binary metrics fail to comprehensively capture performance across all classes (Branco et al. 2017). To address these limitations, several metrics have been proposed specifically for multi-class imbalanced scenarios (Branco et al. 2017; Grandini et al. 2020). Table 6 presents these widely used multi-class evaluation metrics. Each metric is described in detail as follows.

  • (1) Class Average Accuracy (AvAcc) AvAcc (Branco et al. 2017) is a metric specifically designed to evaluate the overall classification performance in scenarios where the dataset contains multiple classes, especially with imbalanced class distributions. As shown in Table 6, where C is the number of classes in the dataset, \(TP_i\) is the number of true positive instances for class i, and \(FN_i\) is the number of false negative instances for class i. For each class i, the accuracy is calculated by dividing the number of true positives \(TP_i\) (correctly classified instances for that class) by the total number of actual instances for that class (the sum of true positives and false negatives). The final average accuracy (AvAcc) is computed as the mean of these per-class accuracies, ensuring that each class contributes equally to the overall performance metric. Unlike traditional accuracy, which aggregates all correctly classified instances without considering class proportions, AvAcc calculates the classification accuracy for each class separately and then averages these values. In multi-class imbalanced datasets, where certain classes may be significantly underrepresented, relying solely on overall accuracy can lead to misleading conclusions. A model with high accuracy may primarily succeed in predicting majority class instances while failing to classify minority classes correctly. AvAcc addresses this limitation by assigning equal importance to each class, ensuring that minority class performance is not overshadowed by the majority class and thereby providing a more representative measure of overall classification performance. However, AvAcc focuses solely on how well the model classifies each class, without directly accounting for the trade-off between false positives and false negatives.

  • (2) Class Balance Accuracy (CBA) CBA (Mosley 2025) is designed to provide a more equitable assessment of classification performance in multi-class imbalanced scenarios. As shown in Table 6, where C is the number of classes in the dataset, \(mat_{(i,i)}\) represents the number of correctly classified samples for class i, \(\sum _{j=1}^{C} mat_{(i,j)}\) is the total number of samples predicted as class i (i.e., the sum of the i-th column in the confusion matrix), and \(\sum _{j=1}^{C} mat_{(j,i)}\) is the total number of actual samples belonging to class i (i.e., the sum of the i-th row in the confusion matrix). For minority classes, where models often under-predict due to limited training data, CBA normalizes using the actual number of samples in the denominator, ensuring that the model’s ability to correctly classify these classes is adequately assessed. Conversely, for majority classes, where over-prediction may occur due to model bias or overfitting, CBA normalizes using the predicted number of samples, emphasizing the model’s precision. This dual consideration allows CBA to adapt to the specific characteristics of each class, providing a more balanced and fair evaluation of model performance in multi-class imbalanced classification.

  • (3) Macro-averaged F1-score (macro-F1) The macro-F1 (Grandini et al. 2020) is an extension of the F1-score designed to evaluate the performance of classifiers in multi-class settings, especially when dealing with imbalanced datasets. It calculates the F1-score for each class independently and then averages these scores equally, as shown in Table 6, where C is the total number of classes and \(F1_i\) denotes the F1-score of class i. By averaging the F1-score across all classes, macro-F1 effectively captures both false positive and false negative rates across the entire dataset, ensuring that the misclassification of minority classes is properly reflected in the evaluation. While AvAcc effectively provides a balanced assessment across all classes, it does not account for the trade-off between false positives and false negatives. In contrast, macro-F1, by incorporating both precision and recall, offers a more nuanced evaluation of model performance, making it more suitable for applications where minimizing false negatives is critical.

  • (4) Arithmetic Macro-average of Recall in Each Class (\(Rec_M\)) \(Rec_M\) (Branco et al. 2017) is a metric designed for evaluating multi-class imbalanced classification performance. It focuses on ensuring fair representation of all classes by considering the recall (true positive rate) for each class individually and averaging these values. This metric prevents the dominance of majority classes in the evaluation and provides a more balanced assessment of model performance across all classes. While macro-F1 also incorporates recall through the F1-score, it balances recall with precision, making it sensitive to both false positives and false negatives. In contrast, CBA prioritizes recall exclusively, making it a more appropriate metric in scenarios where failing to detect minority class instances (false negatives) is a primary concern.

  • (5) Geometric Average of Recall in Each Class (MAvG) MAvG (Sun et al. 2006) serves as a robust evaluation metric for multi-class imbalanced classification. Computed using the geometric mean of recall values across all classes, MAvG is particularly sensitive to poor recall in any individual class. Consequently, if a classifier performs poorly on even a single class, the overall MAvG score decreases significantly. This property ensures that classifiers achieving high MAvG scores must maintain consistently strong performance across all classes, rather than excelling in only a few.

  • (6) Mean \(F_{\beta }\) Measure (\(F_{\beta M}\)) \(F_{\beta M}\) (Branco et al. 2017) is an evaluation metric designed for multi-class imbalanced classification that generalizes the F1-score by introducing a weighting factor, \(\beta \), to control the trade-off between precision and recall. The key distinction between \(F_{\beta M}\) and macro-F1 lies in its ability to emphasize recall when \(\beta > 1\) or precision when \(\beta < 1\). By adjusting \(\beta \), researchers can tailor the evaluation metric to align with the specific priorities of a given classification task.

Additionally, some studies have employed additional metrics, such as Macro-average of Precision (\(Prec_M\)), Micro-average of Recall (\(Rec_{\mu }\)), and Micro-average of Precision (\(Prec_{\mu }\)) (Branco et al. 2017), to provide further insights into model performance under imbalanced conditions. However, these metrics are less frequently used in multi-class classification tasks due to their inherent limitations in handling class imbalance and their tendency to mask the performance disparities between minority and majority classes (Sokolova and Lapalme 2009). Consequently, careful selection of evaluation metrics becomes essential, as it determines the accuracy and fairness of model assessment, comparison, and optimization.

3 Class overlap in imbalanced data classification

Although class imbalance has long been recognized as a major challenge in machine learning, increasing evidence suggests that its most severe effects are primarily driven by class overlap. When these two factors interact, especially in multi-class settings, the learning process becomes more complex and often undermines traditional imbalance-handling techniques. Consequently, a systematic analysis centered on class overlap is essential. This section adopts such a perspective by formally defining class overlap, examining its impact on classification performance, and reviewing commonly used metrics for its quantification.

3.1 Definition of class overlap

Different researchers define class overlap using terms such as “class overlap”, “overlap regions”, and “overlap areas” (Santos et al. 2023). These definitions are closely related because class overlap inherently involves ambiguous regions within the data space. Class overlap is commonly defined as: (1) regions within the data space where class representations exhibit similarity (Denil and Trappenberg 2010; 2) regions in the data space where training samples from different classes are present in comparable proportions (López et al. 2013; 3) regions in the data space where class priors are approximately equal (Fernández et al. 2018b); or (4) regions in the data space containing instances from multiple classes, where class boundaries are not well-separated and exhibit overlap (Fernández et al. 2018a). These definitions collectively convey the idea that certain regions of the data space may be shared by multiple classes.

However, definitions (1) to (3) describe class overlap under the assumption of a balanced scenario, where all classes have comparable sample distributions and class imbalance is not considered. In an imbalanced scenario, these definitions may not fully apply, as the distribution of classes in overlap regions is often skewed rather than similar. Thus, a global definition of class overlap refers to the existence of regions in the data space where samples from multiple classes are intertwined, leading to ambiguous class boundaries (Santos et al. 2023). As shown in Fig. 3, samples located in these overlap regions typically lie near the decision boundary regions between classes, where class separation is weak. Despite belonging to different classes, these samples exhibit similar feature representations, which exacerbate the challenges of class imbalance as overlap regions disproportionately affect classifier performance (Lango and Stefanowski 2022).

In fact, the global definition of class overlap does not imply that samples within overlap regions lack distinct characteristics. Accordingly, class overlap can be quantified in different ways, each emphasizing different properties of the overlap regions and thereby offering distinct insights into the problem (Ho and Basu 2002; Sotoca et al. 2006). The quantification of the class overlap problem is typically conducted using class overlap measurement metrics, which often refer to the concepts of “Overlap Degree” or “Overlap Ratio”. “Overlap Degree” is perhaps the most widely used term to characterize the extent to which class overlap impacts certain domains in imbalanced data (Santos et al. 2023). Some foundational studies quantify the overlap degree as the proportion of the domain area occupied by overlap regions (García et al. 2006, 2008), while others measure it based on the proportion of samples located near the decision boundaries (Napierała et al. 2025; Wojciechowski and Wilk 2017). Therefore, selecting an appropriate metric to quantify class overlap is crucial, as it directly impacts the assessment and interpretation of class overlap in different scenarios.

3.2 Impact of class overlap on classification performance

Addressing class overlap is essential for improving classifier performance in imbalanced classification, especially in multi-class scenarios. Existing research has highlighted the impact of class overlap from three main perspectives: (1) Class overlap significantly impacts the decision-making of classifiers; (2) the challenge of class imbalance primarily arises from class overlap rather than the imbalance itself; and (3) class overlap is a key factor that makes multi-class imbalanced classification more challenging. The following discussion provides a detailed analysis and supporting evidence for these three perspectives.

  • (1) Class overlap significantly impacts the decision-making of classifiers. The decision boundary between classes is more likely to be located in a region with substantial class overlap, making it difficult for the classifier to distinguish between the majority and minority classes. In an imbalanced dataset, the decision boundary is often surrounded by a large number of majority class samples, resulting in the majority class being presented to the learner more frequently than the minority class. As class overlap increases boundary ambiguity, hence altering the structure of the learning problem and exacerbating the intrinsic bias of conventional classifiers towards the majority class. This explains the persistent performance decrease observed across diverse classifiers and datasets, even with small imbalance ratios. In this regard, class overlap is a structural source of difficulty rather than a dataset-specific feature. Traditional classifiers favor the majority class when learning from samples located in overlap regions near decision boundary due to their inherent bias towards the majority class. This bias leads to a distorted decision boundary, which makes it difficult for the classifier to accurately classify minority class instances in overlap regions. As a consequence, minority instances close to the boundary may be misclassified (Vuttipittayamongkol et al. 2021). This is undesirable in real-world applications because boundary instances, often referred to as hard-to-learn samples, play a crucial role in classification. Accurately identifying these challenging instances can enhance overall classification performance (Wei et al. 2020). Moreover, precise recognition of such samples is essential in practical scenarios, such as medical diagnosis, where conditions like benign and malignant lung nodules share similar feature attributes. These instances may lie in overlapping regions, and misclassifying a malignant nodule as benign could lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment, significantly impacting patient outcomes (Mishra et al. 2024).

  • (2) The challenge of class imbalance primarily arises from class overlap rather than the imbalance itself. In recent years, researchers have shown that the deterioration in classifier performance is primarily attributed to class overlap rather than the class imbalance itself when addressing class imbalance issues (Krawczyk 2016; Vuttipittayamongkol et al. 2021; Lango and Stefanowski 2022). For instance, to investigate the interrelationship between imbalance and class overlap, a series of experiments were carried out using various datasets in which the imbalance rate was systematically increased from 1 to 20, and the overlap rate was increased from 1 to 10 (Lango and Stefanowski 2022). The results revealed two key findings: 1) An increase in class overlap significantly impairs the recognition of minority classes, particularly in datasets with higher imbalance ratios; and 2) the effect of class overlap is more detrimental than that of class imbalance, leading to a more pronounced decline in classifier performance. This result demonstrates a significant influence of class overlap, with the extent of its impact varying based on the types of overlapped classes. Class imbalance influences the frequency of class observations, but class overlap dictates the informativeness of these observations. When minority samples are clustered in overlap regions, increasing their quantity via rebalancing strategies does not inherently enhance class separability. Consequently, numerous data-level techniques that exclusively aim to modify class distributions often demonstrate diminishing benefits in instances with significant overlap. The common difficulty recognized in various studies is not merely inadequate minority representation, but rather the existence of overlapping samples that skew decision boundary learning. Notably, class overlap between minority and majority classes was found to be more challenging compared to other types of overlap. An experimental investigation into the impact of class imbalance and class overlap on sensitivity degradation, the results indicated that class overlap noticeably influenced sensitivity even in the absence of class imbalance (Vuttipittayamongkol et al. 2021). Conversely, when class overlap was absent, optimal sensitivity values were achievable regardless of imbalance degrees. In this case, sensitivity refers to the accuracy of detecting minority class instances, which is crucial in imbalanced data classification. Furthermore, the authors highlight the significant challenge that class overlap poses in imbalanced data classification. While rebalancing class distribution is a widely used strategy to mitigate class imbalance, they argue that it may be insufficient when class overlap is present. They emphasize that effectively handling class overlap should be a primary focus when developing new methods for imbalanced data classification. These findings demonstrate that class imbalance and class overlap have interdependent effects, and it becomes crucial to prioritize the mitigation of the class overlap during the development of dedicated methods for imbalanced data classification.

  • (3) Class overlap is a key factor that makes multi-class imbalanced classification more challenging. Multi-class imbalanced classification is more challenging than binary classification due to class overlap. In binary-class imbalanced scenarios, class overlap typically occurs when synthetic samples mistakenly fall within the regions of the majority class. However, in multi-class imbalance problems, the synthetic samples of a minority class have the potential to overlap not only with the majority samples but also with samples from other classes (Zhu et al. 2023), as shown in Fig. 9. Class overlap creates a greater complexity that cannot be adequately represented by pairwise class interactions alone.

Different from binary-class imbalanced scenarios, where there is a clear distinction between a majority and minority class, the relationships among multiple classes in multi-class imbalanced scenarios are less apparent. Early studies on multi-class imbalanced data introduced the concept of class size configuration, distinguishing between multi-minority (where one class is considered as the majority and the rest as the minority) and multi-majority (where one class is treated as the minority and the rest as the majority) scenarios (Wang and Yao 2012). An investigation into the impact of class size configuration revealed that it significantly affects the recognition of minority classes (Lango and Stefanowski 2022). Notably, they observed that a multi-majority class configuration poses greater challenges than a multi-minority one, as it increases the classifier’s bias toward the majority classes.

Recent research has identified limitations in such a taxonomy. A particular class can simultaneously be considered a majority when compared to some classes, a minority to others, and even exhibit relative balance with the remaining classes (Sleeman and Krawczyk 2021; Krawczyk et al. 2019; Zheng et al. 2022). Such complex class relationships more accurately reflect real-world applications (Krawczyk et al. 2019). This complex multi-class relationship more accurately reflects real-world applications. To validate the complexity of such class relationships, the impact of increasing the number of classes on classifier performance was analyzed (Lango and Stefanowski 2022). Experimental results demonstrated that datasets with a larger number of classes pose greater challenges than those with fewer classes. Moreover, the increasing extent of class overlap significantly affects classifier performance.

Additionally, popular methods designed specifically for binary-class problems fail to be directly applied to their multi-class counterparts (Krawczyk et al. 2019). This is because these methods are unable to effectively model the complex relationships among multiple classes and address the inherent learning difficulties associated with multiclass imbalanced classification. As illustrated in Fig. 10, class 2 is considered a minority class compared to classes 0 and 1, but a majority class compared to class 3. Meanwhile, classes 0 and 1 remain relatively balanced. Overlap patterns might differ among class pairings, resulting in conflicting optimization goals when establishing global decision boundaries. Some papers (Chen et al. 2021; Chujai et al. 2019) employ binarization techniques to decompose the M-class problem into either M(M-1)/2 binary subproblems using the OvO approach or M binary subproblems using the OvA approach. However, binarization techniques break down the inherent relationships among multiple classes, reducing them to pairwise relationships (Krawczyk et al. 2019), leading to a substantial amount of valuable information regarding the characteristics and interactions among different classes being lost. This offers a cohesive rationale for the frequent ineffectiveness of binarization approaches failing expanding to multi-class scenarios: they inherently assume independent or symmetric class relationships, which are disrupted by varied overlap structures. As a result, complex inter-class relationships intensify class overlap, making its mitigation crucial in multi-class imbalanced classification.

3.3 Evaluation metrics for class overlap

The quantification of class overlap, often referred to as the degree of class overlap, plays a pivotal role in addressing the challenges arising from class distributions with overlap in imbalanced data classification. As class overlap directly increases the complexity of multi-class imbalanced data classification, researchers have introduced various metrics to systematically quantify its extent, providing insights into its effect on model performance and guiding the development of more effective classification strategies. Broadly, existing class overlap quantification metrics can be categorized into two main types: (1) decomposition-based metrics, which partition the data space into subregions to assess pairwise class overlap, and (2) non-decomposition metrics, which directly quantify overlap through statistical or geometric properties of the feature space (Santos et al. 2023).

Decomposition-based metrics rely on the decomposition of the data space to analyze overlap between class pairs. By breaking down the feature space, they estimate localized class overlap, typically by measuring pairwise class separability. While effective for binary subproblems, decomposition-based class overlap metrics may not be adequate for precisely assessing classification complexity in multi-class scenarios (Santos et al. 2023). A study demonstrated that metrics used to quantify class overlap complexity do not always accurately reflect a model’s classification performance on the corresponding class (Oh 2011). In other words, a class considered highly complex may still be well-classified, while a seemingly simple class may suffer from significant classification errors. This inconsistency suggests that existing class overlap metrics have limitations and may not fully capture a model’s performance across different classes. The major question is that this form of decomposition distorts the original decision boundaries by ignoring the individual characteristics of each class and mutual relationships between classes (Santos et al. 2023).

Unlike decomposition-based metrics, non-decomposition metrics directly quantify the overall degree of class overlap without explicitly isolating individual class pairs. These methods often leverage geometric properties to assess how intertwined different classes are within the feature space (Napierała et al. 2025; Greene 2025; Mercier et al. 2018). Among these metrics, degOver and SI provide a global assessment of the overall degree of class overlap within the dataset, whereas BE, kDN, and IPoints focus on evaluating the complexity of individual data samples (Santos et al. 2022). Incorporating both global overlap and sample-level complexity enables a more comprehensive evaluation of class overlap.

4 Feature selection in class imbalance

Building on the discussion of class overlap and its impact on classifier performance, this section examines feature selection techniques as a strategy to mitigate overlap in imbalanced datasets. By identifying and retaining the most informative features, these methods aim to reduce ambiguity in overlap regions and enhance the effectiveness of imbalance-handling approaches.

4.1 Introduction to feature selection

Feature selection is a critical preprocessing step in imbalanced datasets, as it enhances the identification of discriminative features (Liu et al. 2019). The primary objective of feature selection is to identify a subset of k features from the full feature space that enables a classifier to achieve optimal performance, where k is either predefined by the user or adaptively determined (Guo et al. 2017). When machine learning algorithms are applied to imbalanced datasets, they often exhibit a bias toward the majority class. However, removing classification-irrelevant features can reduce majority class bias and improve minority class recognition (Chawla et al. 2004). This is particularly important because minority class samples are frequently misidentified as noise and discarded in imbalanced scenarios. By eliminating irrelevant features from the feature space, the risk of misclassification can be reduced, thereby improving the overall classification performance (Yijinga et al. 2016).

This role becomes more critical in multi-class imbalanced scenarios, where class overlap further complicates the learning process. Overlap regions in the feature space, where instances from multiple classes share similar characteristics, amplify ambiguity in decision boundaries, particularly for minority classes (Wei et al. 2020). Feature selection mitigates this challenge by prioritizing features that maximize inter-class separability while minimizing intra-class variance (Song et al. 2024). In other words, a well-designed feature selection strategy can more clearly distinguish the characteristics of different classes, making inter-class differences more pronounced while reducing irrelevant variations within the same class, thereby ensuring that samples of the same class are more compact in the feature space. This strategy is particularly crucial in imbalanced data classification, as it helps mitigate class overlap and enhances the classifier’s ability to recognize the minority class.

Feature selection methods can be categorized into filter, wrapper, and embedded approaches (Guo et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2019). Filter methods evaluate features based on various performance metrics and rank them accordingly, selecting the top-N features with the highest scores. These methods rely entirely on the intrinsic properties of the data and operate independently of any specific classifier. The selection of features in filter methods remains unaffected by classification outcomes. Among the three feature selection approaches, filter methods are regarded as the most computationally efficient. A study (Bolón-Canedo et al. 2014) points out that a major drawback of filter methods is their lack of interaction with the classifier that will ultimately be used for classification. As a result, the selected features may not necessarily contribute to optimal classifier performance. Another limitation is that most filter methods operate independently of other features, evaluating each feature in isolation without considering its relationships with others Liu et al. (2019). This can be particularly problematic in multi-class imbalanced classification, where class overlap is already a significant challenge. Ignoring feature dependencies may prevent filter methods from capturing complex inter-class relationships, resulting in suboptimal discrimination of minority classes.

Wrapper methods select optimal feature subsets by iteratively training a model and evaluating its performance using a predefined metric. These methods iteratively search through possible feature combinations, selecting the subset that maximizes classifier performance (Fu et al. 2020). Although wrapper approaches are computationally intensive, they are particularly valuable in imbalanced scenarios. This is because they can incorporate resampling or cost-sensitive methods to ensure that the impact of minority classes is adequately considered (Ramos-Lopez and Maldonado 2021; Khaldy and Kambhampati 2018). Embedded methods share similarities with wrapper methods. These methods perform feature selection during model training, jointly optimizing the classifier and feature subset (Liu et al. 2019). One approach to embedded feature selection leverages the inherent properties of decision tree algorithms, such as Iterative Dichotomiser 3 (ID3), Random Forest, and Classification and Regression Tree (CART) (Guyon and Elisseeff 2003). These methods balance model accuracy and feature relevance by design, making them inherently adaptable to imbalanced data.

4.2 Feature selection methods for imbalanced classification

A standard feature selection method includes four components: subset generation, evaluation, stopping criteria, and validation (Tsai et al. 2024). Selecting or designing an effective feature search strategy is essential to ensuring that the most informative features are retained while minimizing redundancy. Currently, researchers have employed a range of search strategies in feature selection, including greedy algorithms, heuristic approaches, and random search methods (Fu et al. 2020). However, many existing feature selection methods suffer from limited search efficiency and premature convergence (Song et al. 2024).

To overcome this limitation, population-based metaheuristic algorithms, including those inspired by evolutionary computation (e.g., Genetic Algorithm, GA) and swarm intelligence (e.g., Particle Swarm Optimization, PSO; Whale Optimization Algorithm, WOA), have been widely applied in feature selection (Hancer et al. 2025). A study have reported that over 290 papers indexed in the Web of Science between January 2013 and May 2024 have employed PSO as a search strategy for feature selection (Song et al. 2024). Unlike traditional optimization methods, these algorithms do not require gradient information, enabling it to perform effective global searches. Figure 11 illustrates the overall process of population-based feature selection methods. Compared to traditional feature selection methods, these feature selection methods offer several advantages (Song et al. 2024): 1) These methods possess robust global optimization abilities, enabling an efficient exploration of the entire feature space through population-based strategies inspired by biological evolution and swarm intelligence. This allows for a more comprehensive search process, leading to the identification of high-quality feature subsets. 2) They exhibit strong adaptability, as they can dynamically adjust search strategies based on different scenarios and data characteristics. By defining appropriate fitness functions tailored to specific problem requirements, these methods effectively address a wide range of complex feature selection challenges. Consequently, integrating population-based algorithms enhances global exploration and reduces the risk of local optima in feature selection.

Related studies have explored population-based metaheuristic algorithms, including evolutionary and swarm intelligence methods, for feature selection in imbalanced classification to enhance classification performance. A GA-based feature selection method tailored for binary imbalanced data was introduced, where the fitness function is enhanced using the G-mean evaluation criterion instead of overall classification accuracy (Du et al. 2014). An improved version of this GA-based method later incorporated an extended G-mean evaluation criterion to determine the optimal feature subset (Du et al. 2015). A GA method was also employed to identify representative shape-based features in leaf images, demonstrating superior performance compared to filter-based feature selection techniques (Sainin et al. 2017). To address class overlap in multi-class logging data, an ensemble classification algorithm integrating PSO for feature selection was proposed (Yijinga et al. 2016). Additionally, a novel feature selection method based on PSO was developed to tackle the complex interplay between missing data and class imbalance (Zhang et al. 2021), while a federated feature selection method based on PSO was introduced to identify optimal feature subsets while preserving data privacy (Hu et al. 2022). These PSO-based approaches have shown effectiveness in improving the processing of imbalanced datasets and enhancing classification accuracy. More recently, the WOA (Mirjalili and Lewis 2016) has emerged as a competitive method in class imbalance learning (Sun et al. 2022, 2023; Zhou and Zeeshan 2023; Tyagi et al. 2024). WOA has demonstrated strong performance in optimizing parameters to identify relevant features for both binary and multi-class imbalanced data, significantly improving overall classification performance (Sun et al. 2022, 2023).

5 Existing sampling approaches for addressing class overlap in imbalance tasks

In addition to feature selection techniques aimed at reducing class overlap, another major line of research in imbalanced classification involves sampling-based preprocessing strategies designed to mitigate overlap. Over the last decade, extensive research has been devoted to addressing class imbalance, leading to the development of a diverse range of methodological solutions. These solutions are typically categorized into three groups: data-level approaches (Krawczyk et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2022), algorithm-level methods (Yu et al. 2018), and ensemble-based techniques (Fernandes et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2022). Among these solutions, data-level approaches have shown great potential by trying to solve the class overlap problem in imbalanced classification tasks (Vuttipittayamongkol et al. 2021; Vuttipittayamongkol and Elyan 2020a). These approaches seek to address the issue of imbalanced class distribution by either duplicating or creating synthetic samples of the minority class (oversampling) (Krawczyk et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2022), reducing samples of the majority class (undersampling) (Vuttipittayamongkol and Elyan 2020b; Vuttipittayamongkol et al. 2018), or using a combination of both methods (hybrid sampling) (Palli et al. 2022).

5.1 Undersampling methods

One of the earliest and most often used strategies for alleviating class overlap in imbalanced learning is undersampling. Existing research present diverse algorithmic designs, although their fundamental concepts can be categorized according to the definition of class overlap and the removal of majority class samples. Generally, most methodologies assume that overlap occurs either near to the decision boundary or within obviously recognizable overlapping regions, resulting in two primary undersampling paradigms.

5.1.1 Undersampling methods in binary-class classification

A major line of research concentrates on minimizing class overlap by selectively removing majority class instances situated near the decision boundary, based on the assumption that these samples predominantly contribute to confusing class separation. In this context, a decision boundary refers to the hypersurface in a feature space that separates different classes (Ma et al. 2023), and overlap is regarded as a local phenomenon concentrated around this boundary.

Early efforts in this category employed structural or graph-based techniques to refine boundary regions. For example, a two-stage undersampling method based on a minimum spanning tree was proposed to identify and clean boundary samples, thereby mitigate class overlap in imbalanced classification tasks (Guzman-Ponce et al. 2020). Based on this idea, the concept of overlapping degree was introduced to explicitly measure the extent of class overlap. An undersampling technique was subsequently proposed to remove majority class instances overlapping a margin-based classification boundary (Zhang et al. 2020). These methods emphasize a common mechanism: overlap can be determined directly by boundaries closeness, and samples that predominantly contribute to boundary uncertainty are chosen eliminated. Furthermore, evolutionary and population-based techniques further expanded this design. A GA-based undersampling technique for binary credit classification has shown that removing majority samples close to the decision boundary can effectively mitigate class imbalance and overlap, particularly when integrated with feature selection to eliminate duplicate or misleading features (Gong et al. 2022). This integration reveals a significant insight: class overlap is not merely a sampling concern but is also affected by the relevance and representation of features.

Recent research has attempted to enhance boundary identification by integrating multi-scale or adaptive neighborhood data. The multi-granularity relabeled undersampling (MGRU) method (Dai et al. 2022), for instance, improves overlap detection by moving beyond global nearest-neighbor criteria, such as those used in Tomek Links. By utilizing local granularity subspaces and a globally relabeled index, MGRU attains enhanced boundary refinement and reduces the likelihood of misidentifying non-overlapping instances. An improved equalization undersampling approach (Bai et al. 2024) was introduced to assess sample contributions in relation to the decision boundaries and to eliminate noisy or excessively overlapping samples by weighted boundary sampling. These approaches emphasize a fundamental design principle: accurate border characterization is essential for efficient overlap reduction, but too rigorous boundary cleaning could risk the preservation of useful majority samples.

Unlike boundary-focused methods, an alternative research approach considers class overlap as a regional phenomenon and directly addresses samples located within overlapped regions of the feature space. Current methods primarily depend on similarity metrics, clustering, or matrix-based representations to explicitly identify overlapping regions before executing undersampling. A representative method (Vuttipittayamongkol et al. 2018) use fuzzy clustering algorithms, such as Fuzzy C-Means (FCM) (Bezdek et al. 1984), to assign a membership degree to each instance. Samples from the majority class that exhibit ambiguous memberships, indicating a high similarity to minority class characteristics, are classified as overlapping instances and are subsequently eliminated. While this strategy offers a more explicit characterization of overlap regions than boundary-based methods, it introduces new challenges. Elimination thresholds are frequently established empirically, and global clustering assumptions might result in the disproportionate removal of majority samples within heterogeneous data distributions. To overcome these limitations, subsequent studies introduced adaptive or data-driven threshold mechanisms. A novel undersampling method incorporating an automatic exclusion threshold was introduced to more accurately detect and eliminate overlapping majority cases based on the assessed degree of class overlap (Vuttipittayamongkol and Elyan 2020a). This strategy, in contrast to previous approaches that concentrated mostly on boundary samples, directly addresses the whole overlap region, leading to enhanced stability and consistency in overlap mitigation (Vuttipittayamongkol and Elyan 2020b). This shift signifies a significant conceptual advancement: overlap is not limited to a restricted boundary area but can extend across a wider area of the feature space.

The characterization of global overlap has also been investigated using matrix decomposition techniques. The Schur Decomposition Class-overlap Undersampling (SDCU) (Dai et al. 2023) method mitigates a common constraint of nearest-neighbor-based strategies by integrating global similarity data. By employing matrix decomposition, SDCU recognizes overlapping instances on a global scale, facilitating comprehensive overlap detection in complex data distributions. In addition, a metaheuristic optimization-based undersampling algorithm has been suggested to balance the reduction of overlap with the preservation of information (Soltanzadeh et al. 2023). This approach seeks to mitigate excessive undersampling while efficiently diminishing overlap, especially in areas with significant overlap, by assessing classifier performance during the selection of majority samples.

Despite differences in implementation, the mentioned methods share a core objective: enhancing class separability by selectively eliminating majority class samples that predominantly contribute to overlap. Decision-boundary-oriented methods focus on local ambiguity and perform when overlap occurs at class boundaries, while overlap-region-oriented approaches offer a broader perspective and are more appropriate for datasets with diffuse or intricate overlap patterns. Both paradigms have a shared challenge: balancing overlap reduction with the potential for information loss caused by excessive undersampling.

5.1.2 Undersampling methods in multi-class classification

In contrast to binary imbalanced learning, undersampling methods have been far less investigated in multi-class classification contexts. This disparity arises not solely from an absence in methodological interest, but rather reflects the inherent complexity of class relationships in multi-class data, where overlap may simultaneously exist across multiple class pairs and cannot be clearly defined by a singular decision boundary or overlap region.

Current multi-class undersampling research mainly employs boundary-oriented strategies and depends on support vectors to identify interesting instances. For example, a support vector-based undersampling strategy (Arafat et al. 2019) was presented to address multi-class imbalanced classification by preserving majority class instances that significantly define inter-class decision boundaries. Experimental findings indicated improved classification accuracy for both minority and majority classes, indicating that boundary-focused undersampling may partially mitigate the negative effects of imbalance and overlap, even in the absence of explicit modeling of class overlap. A two-step undersampling method (Krawczyk et al. 2021) was similarly established for multi-class imbalanced data. At first, a one-class classifier identifies support vectors as representative samples for each class, markedly reducing the data size while maintaining border information. In the next step, an undersampling method is employed to achieve further balance in the training set. In this way, the approach may indirectly alleviate class overlap by enhancing inter-class separation.

Despite these efforts, a significant limitation of existing multi-class undersampling approaches is that class overlap is not accurately defined, quantified, or localized. In multi-class scenarios, overlap frequently demonstrates more complex interactions, where a sample may simultaneously exist in overlapping regions associated with numerous classes. Eliminating instances of the majority class related to a certain class pair may inadvertently alter the distribution or decision boundaries of other classes, resulting in diminished performance. This difficulty clarifies why undersampling is particularly overlooked in multi-class imbalanced learning. In contrast to binary classification, where the removal of majority samples primarily impacts a singular boundary, multi-class undersampling necessitates careful coordination across various class boundaries to prevent significant information loss. These facts suggest that effective multi-class undersampling requires overlap-aware methods that simultaneously address inter-class interactions rather than treating each class independently. 5.2 Oversampling methods

5.2.1 Oversampling methods in binary-class classification

Existing binary-class oversampling techniques can be generally classified based on where overlap is expected to occur and how synthetic samples are generated to handle it. Specifically, most methods implicitly assume that class overlap occurs either close to the decision boundary or within observable overlapping regions, and so implement various mechanisms to guide sample generation.

A major line of research emphasizes strengthening the decision boundary by creating synthetic minority samples from informative instances situated near the majority class. These methods are driven by the recognition that misclassification in imbalanced datasets frequently arises from ambiguous areas around class boundaries, when minority samples are limited and readily overshadowed by majority instances. For example, an oversampling strategy was presented that finds difficult-to-learn minority instances and provides them adaptive weights depending on their euclidean distance from the nearest majority samples. Synthetic samples are generated from these weighted cases using a clustering-based approach, thereby selectively reinforcing the decision boundary in susceptible areas (Barua et al. 2014). Similarly, the approach in Nekooeimehr and Lai-Yuen (2016) clusters minority samples to identify hard-to-learn instances and allocates greater weights to those nearer to the majority class. This strategy decreases the possibility of producing synthetic samples in highly overlapped areas by including majority class information during clustering. In both cases, the underlying premise is that minority samples along the boundary are more valuable for classification, but also more sensitive to overlap, necessitating carefully controlled sample selection.

Beyond weighting and clustering strategies, several studies have attempted to reshape the decision boundary by jointly adjusting minority and majority samples. An energy-based oversampling method was implemented to shift majority samples that are overly proximate to minority samples away from the boundary, therefore reducing local overlap and enhancing class separability (Koziarski and Woźniak 2017). This idea was further extended through the concept of class potential, which incorporates both minority and majority distributions to guide energy-based resampling in a more global manner (Koziarski et al. 2021). Unlike traditional oversampling methods, which merely synthesis minority samples, these methods produce minority samples while translating majority instances, resulting in a more explicit boundary adjustment process. Radius-SMOTE (Pradipta et al. 2021a) employs a similar idea, defining a “safe radius” for each selected minority sample depending on its nearest majority neighbor. Synthetic samples are created exclusively inside this radius, so effectively preventing newly generated instances from intruding into majority-dominated areas. In addition, a support vector data description (SVDD) model was used to define class boundaries and detect overlapping samples (Tao et al. 2022). Eliminating these overlapping instances before oversampling mitigates their adverse impact on the creation of subsequent synthetic samples.

Unlike boundary-oriented methods, another category of oversampling strategies seeks to represent and address class overlap at a regional level, based on the assumption that overlap may extend across wider areas rather than being restricted to a limited decision boundary. These approaches typically categorize minority samples into different structural types and apply region-specific sampling strategies. A representative method (Zhu et al. 2020) categorizes minority samples into inland, borderline, and trapped categories, while establishing specific interpolation criteria for each category. A similar method (Wang et al. 2024d) employes an improved density-based clustering algorithm to identify inland, borderline, and trapped regions, and then applies distinct oversampling strategies according to local data characteristics. These findings emphasize that uniform interpolation among all minority samples can be harmful, especially in intricate overlapping situations, and that region-aware oversampling is crucial for maintaining class structure.

Several methods further enhance regional modeling by introducing explicit overlap quantification mechanisms. A novel oversampling method (Zhang et al. 2023) combining kernel density estimation with neighbor density selection was introduced to estimate density ratios that reflect the degree of class overlap. This information is utilized to guide SMOTE, promoting the creation of synthetic samples far from overlapping areas. Similarly, a grouping-based approach (Ren et al. 2023) identifies minority samples located within overlapping regions and generates synthetic instances in safer areas to rectify distorted decision boundaries. A cluster-based oversampling method restricts sample generation to predefined safe regions, thereby minimizing overlap between the newly generated samples and the original minority instances (Lu et al. 2024). Furthermore, an oversampling method was established the concept of “overlapping degree” by simultaneously considering local and global information from both classes (Jubair et al. 2025). This statistic measure quantifies a minority sample’s contribution to the overlap, facilitating targeted transformation-based sample generation

Additionally, recent studies have shown that combining oversampling with feature selection offers an alternate method for reducing class overlap, in addition to direct resampling procedures. Rather than exclusively altering sample distributions, these strategies seek to minimize overlap by identifying discriminative feature subsets before or during the oversampling process. For instance, feature selection techniques have been incorporated into the oversampling process in metabolomics to reduce inter-class overlap in the chosen feature space (Fu et al. 2020). A further study (Omar et al. 2021) utilized sparse feature selection to minimize overlap and subsequently implemented SMOTE and ADASYN on several credit card datasets. The findings indicate that oversampling performance is significantly affected by the underlying feature representation, and that the joint optimization of feature selection and resampling is a potential approach for addressing overlap in imbalanced classification.

Overall, current oversampling techniques for binary classification demonstrate a distinct theoretical development: from boundary-focused reinforcement strategies to region-sensitive and overlap-assessed sampling schemes, and further toward hybrid frameworks that integrate feature selection. This development indicates a growing acknowledgment that class overlap is a diverse occurrence, necessitating flexible, data-driven strategies instead of standardized resampling rules.

5.2.2 Oversampling methods in multi-class classification

Due to the increased complexity of inter-class relationships and a higher likelihood of simultaneous overlap within multiple classes, multi-class imbalanced classification presents far more challenges than binary classification. In multi-class scenarios, class overlap extends beyond a singular decision boundary, frequently exhibiting as intertwined overlapping regions common to multiple classes. Consequently, oversampling techniques intended for binary classification cannot be directly applied without alteration. Current oversampling methods for multi-class imbalanced data typically adhere to two principal paradigms: (1) enhanced decomposition-based oversampling methods, which transform the multi-class issue into a series of binary subproblems, and (2) direct multi-class oversampling methods, which explicitly address multi-class overlap by recognizing overlapping regions or establishing safe zones for synthetic sample creation within the original feature space.

Building upon traditional decomposition-based strategies, several improved methods have been proposed to better capture class overlap in multi-class scenarios. Representative methods include Radial-based Oversampling (MC-RBO) (Krawczyk et al. 2019), Combined Cleaning and Resampling method (MC-CCR) (Koziarski et al. 2020), Multiclass Neighborhood Repartition-based Oversampling (MC-NRO) (Shen et al. 2024), and Noise-robust Oversampling (NROMM) (Liu et al. 2022). These methods aim to overcome the limitations of classical One-vs-All (OvA) and One-vs-One (OvO) strategies, which often distort inter-class relationships and exacerbate overlap during independent binary resampling. MC-RBO presents the notion of mutual class potential, which integrates distributional data from all classes instead of addressing each binary subproblem alone. Synthetic samples are produced in areas where the mutual class potential approaches zero, signifying a balanced effect from competing classes and a reduced probability of overlap. MC-CCR employs an energy-based strategy to define spherical regions around minority instances and executes a preliminary cleansing phase by eliminating most samples within these areas. Synthetic samples are subsequently produced inside the cleaned areas to enhance class boundaries and minimize sample overlap. Similarly, MC-NRO calculates mutual potentials among classes to partition the data space into optimal subregions for sample creation and subsequently eliminates majority samples situated near decision boundaries to further reduce overlap. NROMM employs a density-based clustering approach to prevent the merging of dense clusters from distinct classes, hence restricting oversampling to more secure areas. Furthermore, it defines overlapping regions and classifies samples as inland, borderline, or trapped, eliminating inland samples with the highest degree of overlap. Although MC-RBO, MC-CCR, and MC-NRO adopt the OVS binarization strategy and NROMM relies on OVE, both approaches still suffer from inherent limitations associated with decomposition-based learning. These strategies enhance overlap handling relative to traditional OvA and OvO methods; however, they may still undermine the overall integrity of multi-class information and create inconsistencies among subproblems, especially in datasets characterized by significant imbalance and intricate overlap patterns (Yang et al. 2024b; Abokadr et al. 2023).

Unlike decomposition-based methods, an increasing amount of research emphasizes direct handling of multi-class imbalanced data without explicit binarization, driven by the recognition that decision boundaries in multi-class settings are sometimes unclear and challenging to define. These methods typically explore the entire data space to identify overlapping regions or safe areas for synthetic sample generation. A Mahalanobis Distance-based Oversampling (MDO) method was proposed to generate synthetic samples that preserve the Mahalanobis distance to their respective class means (Abdi and Hashemi 2016). Building on this approach, later work (Yang et al. 2018) improved MDO to allow mixed-type data, making it more applicable to real-world datasets with diverse feature representations. Additionally, a clustering-based oversampling method has been introduced, which partitions data into k clusters, effectively transforming the original problem into k subproblems. Unlike conventional decomposition strategies, this method maintains inter-class relationships by grouping similar samples together. Within each cluster, class overlap is mitigated by reducing majority instances in overlap regions while applying targeted oversampling to the minority class (Chen et al. 2021). An alternative strategy identifies clean subregions within the minority class, ensuring that no majority class samples are present. Synthetic samples are generated exclusively within these clean subregions, effectively reducing class overlap not only between synthetic and original minority samples but also among synthetic samples from different minority classes (Zhu et al. 2023).

Recent research has emphasized that not all samples contribute equally to classification tasks, leading to the estimation of instance complexity and regional categorization of samples, each receiving a tailored oversampling strategy based on their characteristics (Santos et al. 2023). In line with this idea, a global-local-based oversampling method (GLOS) has been introduced, which identifies hard-to-learn instances and categorizes them into three groups: safe, borderline, and small disjunct. Different sample synthesis strategies were then applied based on the intrinsic characteristics of each instance type (Han et al. 2023). To directly address class overlap in multi-class settings, a membership-based multi-class resampling and cleaning method (MC-MBRC) was proposed. MC-MBRC computes membership values for all instances and assigns them into safe, overlap, and noisy regions according to their respective degrees, thereby enabling more informed resampling (Ma et al. 2023). Unlike other methods that treat all samples uniformly, GLOS and MC-MBRC consider the heterogeneous relationships between classes. By employing complexity estimation, they refine the classification of instances into different types and strictly constrain synthetic sample generation within appropriate class regions. This approach mitigates class overlap and preserves the structural integrity of the data. In particular, MC-MBRC has demonstrated the effectiveness of using membership degrees to represent inter-class relationships, as this approach captures the varying degrees of association between samples and different classes, enabling a more refined understanding of class distributions in multi-class imbalanced classification. 5.2.3 Integrating oversampling and feature selection for imbalanced classification

The combination of feature selection methods and oversampling has demonstrated remarkable effectiveness in addressing class overlap, which is crucial for handling multi-class imbalanced data. For instance, two feature selection algorithms combined with SMOTE have been proposed to minimize the degree of class overlap (Fu et al. 2020). The findings demonstrate that the proposed algorithms exhibit strong competitiveness and can serve as effective alternative feature selection strategies in class imbalance learning. The findings also highlight their applicability to multi-class imbalanced data classification. A GA was utilized to select an optimal combination of heterogeneous clustering techniques and to identify overlapping instances near decision boundaries, thereby helping to mitigate class overlap in multi-class imbalanced problems (Dai et al. 2024). Furthermore, relevant studies have integrated population-based feature selection with oversampling methods, demonstrating its superior ability to handle class overlap in multi-class imbalanced data. For instance, the integration of various feature selection methods with the SMOTE oversampling method has been explored to identify the optimal combination procedure for multi-class imbalanced data (Tsai et al. 2024). The experimental results indicate that: 1) ensemble feature selection combined with SMOTE provides very similar classification performance compared with single feature selection algorithms, such as WOA and GA. However, it introduces higher complexity and computational cost; 2) applying feature selection prior to oversampling yields superior performance compared to the reverse order; and 3) among the different feature selection combined with SMOTE, WOA-based feature selection demonstrates outstanding performance, ranking in the top three across more than ten feature selection methods, including filter-based, wrapper-based, and embedded methods. These findings suggest that researchers can explore the integration of WOA-based feature selection with other oversampling methods to further enhance the handling of multi-class imbalanced data.

5.3 Hybrid sampling methods

5.3.1 Hybrid sampling methods in binary-class classification

Hybrid sampling methods use a combination of oversampling and undersampling techniques to handle both class imbalance and class overlap. Unlike pure oversampling or undersampling approaches, hybrid methods are driven by the finding that class overlap frequently results from both insufficient minority representation near decision boundaries and overwhelming majority dominance in overlapping regions. As a result, hybrid techniques seek to alter the data distribution from both sides by selecting deleting overlapping majority samples while generating informative minority samples in strategically constrained regions.

A density-based hybrid approach (Mayabadi and Saadatfar 2022) balances and normalizes class distributions by first detecting majority class samples situated within a predetermined radius of minority instances and removing them to reduce local overlap. This method implicitly assumes that class overlap is most common in areas with significant inter-class proximity, and that removing most samples from these regions may create a clearer separation. By then applying oversampling, the technique enhances minority representation without generating synthetic samples in ambiguous areas, emphasizing the need of coordinated sample removal and generation. More recent works have combined hybrid sampling and generative models to enhance sample synthesis flexibility. A hybrid sampling method integrating an optimized generative adversarial network (GAN)-based oversampling with local density-based undersampling has been developed to selectively remove majority instances that significantly contribute to class overlap, thereby refining the classification boundary (Liu et al. 2024). This method reflects a growing trend in hybrid sampling: using powerful generative models to enrich minority classes while preserving cautious undersampling procedures to keep synthetic samples from intruding on overlapping regions. However, its success is highly dependent on correctly identifying overlap-prone majority samples, emphasizing the common difficulty of reliable overlap detection. Another hybrid sampling strategy (Li and Liu 2025) based on natural neighbors and density estimation highlights the relevance of local data topology in overlap reduction. This method removes most samples from overlapping regions during the undersampling stage by specifically considering the structural properties of overlapping instances. The oversampling stage then enriches information not only in minority-dominated regions, but also in carefully selected overlap-prone areas, with the goal of correcting inaccurate decision boundaries. This design emphasizes a crucial idea shared by hybrid methods: overlap mitigation is more than just reducing ambiguity; it is also about selectively reinforcing informative regions that contribute to boundary refinement.

Despite differences in implementation, these hybrid sampling methods share several fundamental mechanisms and challenges. First, they rely primarily on local density or neighborhood-based criteria to detect overlapping locations, making their performance susceptible to parameter selection and data scarcity. Second, good coordination between undersampling and oversampling is crucial; excessive removal of majority samples may result in information loss, whereas unrestrained oversampling increases overlap. Finally, most existing hybrid techniques are based on the assumption of relatively simple overlap structures, which may restrict their effectiveness in datasets with complicated class boundaries.

5.3.2 Hybrid sampling methods in multi-class classification

Hybrid sampling methods explicitly designed to address class overlap in multi-class imbalanced learning remain relatively scarce. This scarcity is partly due to the inherent complexity of multi-class overlap, in which multiple classes may interact concurrently within the same region of the feature space, making basic boundary-based or pairwise techniques ineffective. Existing hybrid approaches in this field frequently attempt to detect unsafe or overlap-prone instances across multiple classes before choosing applying oversampling or undersampling strategies based on their estimated contribution to class overlap.

An early attempt in this direction proposed identifying different types of minority examples in multi-class imbalanced datasets to guide the application of hybrid sampling strategies (Lango et al. 2025). The primary contribution of this work is its emphasis on modeling mutual inter-class relationships, which are critical for understanding the causes of data difficulties in multi-class scenarios. Instead of relying exclusively on intra-class characteristics to determine sample safety, the method considers information about the relationships between a given sample and its nearby classes. These relationships are quantified using a similarity factor, which measures the degree of similarity between classes. Based on this factor, each sample is assigned a safety score and classified as safe or unsafe. Unsafe samples are more likely to be in overlapping regions, increasing the likelihood of misclassification. This approach emphasizes an important conceptual insight: class overlap in multi-class data is fundamentally relational, resulting from interactions between multiple classes rather than separate class pairs. However, one significant shortcoming of this strategy is its reliance on user-defined similarity information, which demands strong domain knowledge and restricts its application in data-driven or large-scale settings.

Motivated by this, a solution known as Similarity Oversampling and Undersampling Preprocessing (SOUP) was introduced, which identifies different types of minority samples by creating a safe level coefficient based on class similarity for each sample (Janicka et al. 2019). The safety level coefficient for a sample is determined based on the imbalance ratio between classes rather than being manually specified by the user. The lower the safe level coefficient of a sample, the more unsafe that sample is and the more prone it is to class overlap. The method balances class distribution by undersampling the majority classes, removing the most unsafe examples, while oversampling the minority classes by duplicating the safest examples in the opposite direction. This design illustrates the common hybrid sampling principle in multi-class settings: Hybrid samples contribute disproportionately to class overlap, and resampling choices should be informed by their assessed risk level. However, the method defines class similarity solely based on the imbalance ratio between classes, which limits its ability to capture more complex inter-class relationships. By neglecting the positional relationships and proximity between classes in the feature space, it may fail to characterize class overlap accurately. A related work investigated the application of similarity and dissimilarity estimation to define inter-class relationships and differentiate between safe and unsafe samples in multi-class imbalanced data (Zheng et al. 2022). Unsafe samples, which are more likely to contribute to class overlap, were identified and subsequently removed during the undersampling process.

Taken together, existing hybrid sampling methods for multi-class classification share several fundamental characteristics and challenges. First, they highlight the necessity of sample-level safety evaluation as an essential requirement for effective overlap mitigation. Second, they attempt to incorporate inter-class similarity information to model the relational nature of multi-class overlap, although current implementations often rely on simplified or indirect similarity measures. Overall, hybrid sampling methods in multi-class classification represent an important but underdeveloped research direction. Progressing this study necessitates richer representations of inter-class relationships and adaptive methods capable of capturing the various and interwoven characteristics of class overlap in multi-class imbalanced learning. 6 Discussion and future directions for multi-class imbalanced learning

After reviewing major data-level strategies for mitigating class imbalance and class overlap, it is necessary to situate these approaches within the broader context of multi-class imbalanced learning. Multi-class scenarios typically exhibit more complex overlap patterns than binary cases, exposing limitations of existing techniques and motivating further investigation. In what follows, this chapter outlines future research directions in multi-class imbalanced learning, covering membership degree–based oversampling strategies, feature selection and oversampling integration, sample partitioning, and evaluation metrics for overlap assessment.

6.1 Membership degree–based adaptive oversampling strategies

Traditional methods developed for binary imbalanced classification fail to be directly applicable to multi-class scenarios. One common strategy is to decompose a multi-class problem into multiple binary classification tasks, enabling the use of binary-class methods. However, this transformation disrupts the inherent relationships among classes, potentially leading to suboptimal performance. Thus, it is more advantageous to develop and apply ad hoc methods specifically tailored for multi-class imbalanced data. Among existing ad hoc strategies, data-level ad hoc methods have demonstrated certain advantages in mitigating intrinsic data challenges such as class overlap. This highlights the need for ad hoc methods specifically designed for multi-class imbalanced data, particularly those capable of jointly modeling imbalance and overlap.

Data-level ad hoc methods can be broadly classified into two categories: undersampling methods and oversampling methods. Undersampling methods mitigate class overlap by handling samples in overlap regions or removing majority instances near decision boundaries to improve class separability. However, undersampling for multi-class imbalanced problems remains limited. This is mainly due to the intricate relationships between classes, where the removal of majority class instances risks losing critical information, potentially distorting class distributions and compromising decision boundaries. For binary oversampling methods, one strategy involves carefully generating synthetic minority samples near the decision boundary to enhance their recognition. Another alternative is to identify overlap regions within the entire data space to prevent synthetic samples from being generated in these areas. These approaches are not directly applicable to multi-class settings because they fail to model complex inter-class relationships. In addition, the application of hybrid methods in both binary and multi-class scenarios remains relatively limited, as they may introduce the inherent drawbacks of each technique, including the risk of overfitting from synthetic data generation in oversampling and the potential loss of informative instances in undersampling.

In multi-class oversampling, restricting synthetic sample generation in globally identified overlap regions is often preferred, as the intricate relationships among multiple classes obscure clear decision boundaries, making it more effective to manage overlap at a global level. Recent multi-class oversampling studies such as MC-MBRC employ membership degree–based modeling to characterize inter-class relationships. Instead of assigning samples to a single class in a crisp manner, membership degrees quantify the extent to which an instance is associated with multiple classes. By leveraging these degrees, samples can be more precisely partitioned according to their overlap severity, allowing resampling strategies to adaptively constrain sample generation in regions with high membership ambiguity. However, such membership-based formulations are inherently dependent on distance representations in the feature space. In high-dimensional data, irrelevant features may bias distance measurements and class center estimation, which in turn compromises the robustness of membership assignment for overlap-aware resampling. Future research should focus on robust membership modeling that reduces sensitivity to high-dimensional distance distortions.

6.2 Population-based feature selection and its integration with oversampling

In imbalanced data classification, feature selection enhances performance by reducing irrelevant variations and mitigating bias toward the majority class. This effect is especially important in multi-class problems with severe class overlap. By improving inter-class separability and ensuring a more compact representation of minority class samples, feature selection can directly alleviate overlap-related challenges. However, existing feature selection methods often suffer from low search efficiency and a tendency to converge to local optima. To address these challenges, population-based feature selection methods leverage robust global optimization capabilities to explore the feature space effectively and adapt search strategies based on data characteristics, enabling the identification of high-quality feature subsets.

Currently, population-based feature selection methods for addressing class imbalance classification problems are primarily developed based on GA, PSO, and WOA. Among these, WOA-based approaches have demonstrated strong competitiveness in both binary-class and multi-class imbalanced data classification. Beyond standalone feature selection, recent research has highlighted the effectiveness of integrating population-based feature selection with oversampling methods, particularly in mitigating class overlap in multi-class settings. For example, integrating GA or WOA with SMOTE has been used to identify instances located in overlap regions near decision boundaries (Dai et al. 2024; Fu et al. 2020), thereby improving minority class representation and enhancing classifier performance.

However, most integrated methods rely on oversampling techniques designed for binary problems and fail to capture complex multi-class relationships. Despite the strong performance of the WOA, research on integrating WOA with oversampling strategies to address class overlap in multi-class imbalanced classification remains unexplored. Moreover, the standard WOA suffers from inherent limitations. In particular, its control factor is defined as a linearly decreasing function of the iteration number, which may result in premature convergence in complex search spaces.

To alleviate this issue, an improved WOA variant (Sun et al. 2023) introduces a logarithmically decaying control factor, enabling more aggressive exploration in the early stages and more stable, fine-grained exploitation in later iterations. Integrating such improved WOA-based feature selection with oversampling strategies designed for multi-class data, such as membership-aware oversampling, offers a clear methodological direction. Specifically, the adaptive search behavior of improved WOA supports the selection of discriminative features, while membership-based oversampling enables precise identification of overlap regions.

6.3 Exploration of sample partitioning strategies

Although the SOUP method has certain limitations, it shows potential for improving multi-class imbalanced data classification, particularly in addressing class overlap. Since not all minority class samples contribute equally to classification tasks, it is meaningful to assess their importance and categorize them into different data types for targeted processing. This allows for more effective handling of different types of samples, thereby improving the classifier’s ability to discriminate between minority and majority classes. Related studies have also explored the development of models aimed at identifying different sample types. For instance, a study has been proposed that categorizes class samples into safe and unsafe instances by analyzing the neighborhood structure of minority class samples to assess their safety levels (Janicka et al. 2019). A clustering algorithm based on Fast Search and Find of Density Peaks (CFSFDP) (Rodriguez and Laio 2014) was employed to categorize data samples into borderline, trapped, and inland types, with tailored oversampling strategies applied to each type to enhance classification performance (Liu et al. 2022). Additionally, a novel method partitioned samples into safe, overlapping, and noisy regions based on their membership degrees. Subsequently, different operations were applied to each region, considering the impact of the samples on classification performance (Ma et al. 2023).

As shown in Table 12, different studies have proposed varying strategies for sample partitioning, such as dividing samples into safe and unsafe categories (Lango et al. 2025), or further refining them into safe, borderline, rare, and outlier types based on their distribution characteristics (Napierala and Stefanowski 2016). Given the complexity of multi-class imbalanced data, models designed for sample partitioning should account for the interactions between different samples and classes. However, the exploration of relationships between classes in multi-class scenarios remains insufficient. A promising future direction lies in fuzzy membership-based approaches. As evidenced by existing studies, fuzzy membership functions offer a flexible mechanism to for modeling uncertainty and ambiguous in data, leading to more interpretable representations of complex class structures (Wang et al. 2022; Charizanos et al. 2024). In contrast to traditional hard classification methods, which assign each sample to a single class, fuzzy membership modeling allows instances to be associated with multiple classes to varying degrees. This soft assignment enables a more nuanced characterization of overlap regions, making it suitable for imbalanced multi-class classification (Ma et al. 2023; Dorzhigulov and James 2019).

In MC-MBRC, membership degrees are further exploited to partition samples into safe, overlap, and noisy regions, enabling region-aware resampling and more targeted handling of class overlap. Recent advances in fuzzy clustering indicate that enhanced fuzzy membership formulations, such as Possibilistic Fuzzy C-Means (PFCM) and Kernelized Fuzzy C-Means (KFCM) (Tiwari et al. 2024; Yu et al. 2024), can provide more expressive representations of uncertainty and complex data structures. Such properties are particularly beneficial for imbalanced datasets with ambiguous class boundaries and severe class overlap. Accordingly, future research could explore incorporating these advanced FCM-based membership formulations into oversampling methods to refine sample region categorization and guide the generation of more informative synthetic instances.

6.4 Selection of multi-class evaluation metrics and overlap quantification

The evaluation of multi-class imbalanced classification introduces challenges that are not adequately addressed by evaluation metrics originally designed for binary classification. Unlike binary classification, where performance assessment involves only one positive class and one negative class, multi-class scenarios involve multiple classes with more complex relationships. As a result, conventional metrics such as AUC and G-mean often fail to capture class-specific performance across multiple classes and may obscure severe misclassification behaviors. To address these limitations, various multi-class evaluation metrics have been developed, each offering distinct insights into classifier performance. From a future research perspective, a key open problem lies in establishing principled guidelines for metric selection and combination in multi-class imbalanced learning. Future work could develop adaptive metric selection frameworks based on class distribution, misclassification costs, and application requirements. In high-stakes domains such as medical diagnosis, such frameworks could further incorporate domain priorities, for example emphasizing recall-oriented metrics when false negatives carry critical consequences. Additionally, determining the optimal number of evaluation metrics to use is a crucial consideration, as relying on a single metric may provide an incomplete assessment, while an excessive number of metrics could introduce redundancy or misinterpretation. This is particularly important in high-stakes applications such as medical diagnosis, where reliable and clinically relevant evaluation is essential.

Beyond performance evaluation, quantifying class overlap is crucial for understanding the intrinsic difficulty of multi-class imbalanced classification. The degree of class overlap provides a measurable characterization of how strongly different classes are intertwined in the feature space. Existing overlap metrics can be broadly categorized into decomposition-based and non-decomposition approaches. Decomposition-based metrics may be insufficient for accurately evaluating classification complexity in multi-class scenarios, as they overlook the mutual relationships between classes. In contrast, non-decomposition metrics mitigate these limitations by preserving the inherent structure of the data, making them more suitable for measuring class overlap in multi-class imbalanced classification. However, current research on multi-class imbalanced classification primarily relies on multi-class performance metrics to evaluate classifier effectiveness. The use of non-decomposition metrics to directly quantify and exploit the structure of overlap regions remains limited. A promising research direction is therefore the joint utilization of performance metrics and overlap information, enabling a multi-resolution understanding of overlap complexity. This approach could guide the development of multi-class imbalance solutions by explicitly addressing class overlap, which is a critical factor influencing classifier performance and overall model effectiveness.

7 Conclusion

In this review, we presented a comprehensive analysis of the intertwined challenges posed by class imbalance and class overlap, with a particular focus on multi-class imbalanced learning. By systematically summarizing existing studies from a data-level perspective, this paper highlighted that class overlap, rather than imbalance alone, often constitutes the primary source of classification performance degradation. We provided an organized overview of various data-level techniques, including undersampling, oversampling, hybrid sampling, and feature selection, and described the specific strategies adopted by these methods to mitigate overlap-induced difficulties in both binary and multi-class settings. By doing so, this review not only consolidates existing research but also provides a structured foundation for researchers to better understand the interaction between imbalance and overlap, offering practical insights for selecting or developing effective preprocessing strategies tailored to complex multi-class scenarios.
