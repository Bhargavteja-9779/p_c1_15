Abstract

Fairness in machine learning (ML) is a software quality requirement in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, hiring, and criminal justice, where biased predictions based on protected attributes can harm individuals. Individual fairness testing (IFT) searches for pairs of inputs, known as individual discriminatory instances (IDIs), that differ only in a protected attribute yet receive different classifier predictions, indicating discrimination. Existing search-based IFT methods suffer from three limitations: (1) random feature perturbation that ignores the varying discriminatory influence of features, (2) reliance on computationally expensive per-instance local explanations, and (3) high test redundancy that wastes computational resources. This study introduces FIFT (Feature Importance-Guided Fairness Testing), an evolutionary approach that computes global feature importance once via permutation feature importance and uses the normalised scores to guide the search for IDIs. The approach is motivated by the observation that features with greater predictive influence often provide effective search guidance toward classifier decision boundaries where fairness violations may occur. Rather than treating feature importance as a direct indicator of fairness, FIFT employs it as a computationally efficient heuristic for guiding evolutionary search. FIFT introduces Importance-Guided Hybrid Mutation (IHM), which scales perturbation magnitude inversely to feature importance for influential features while applying random perturbations to less influential ones, thus balancing exploitation and exploration of the input space. Experiments on five benchmark datasets and four ML classifiers show that FIFT detects 20.8%–190.4% more IDIs than the strongest baseline, achieves 2.37\(\times \)–3.1\(\times \) higher throughput than local-explanation-based methods, and reduces test redundancy by 84.0%–98.0% relative to other search-based approaches. Retraining with discovered IDIs improves fairness by 28.6%–88.0% with negligible accuracy loss. These results indicate that global feature importance, used as an active search signal rather than a post-hoc diagnostic, improves both the effectiveness and the efficiency of individual fairness testing.

1 Introduction

Machine learning (ML)-based software (or ML Software) is increasingly employed in high-stakes decisions such as healthcare diagnostics, recruitment, lending, and criminal sentencing (Yu et al. 2018; Uma et al. 2023; Counts 2018; Berk 2021), raising serious concerns about the fairness of these systems. Empirical studies reveal that ML software frequently exhibits biased behaviour, leading to discriminatory outcomes against marginalised individuals and groups (Mehrabi et al. 2021). For example, automated resume screening software was shown to discriminate by gender, downgrading female candidates who were equally qualified to their male counterparts (Dastin 2018). Such discriminatory behaviour compromises the trustworthiness, safety, and societal acceptance of these systems.

From a software engineering (SE) perspective, these biased behaviours are classified as fairness bugs, which are systematic disparities between the intended equitable behaviour and the actual observed software outputs (Brun and Meliou 2018). Regulatory frameworks enacted by bodies such as the European Union (Smuha 2019) and IEEE (Chatila and Havens 2019) include fairness as one of the key requirements of ML software, making automated fairness testing a software quality assurance activity for detecting and mitigating these defects prior to deployment (Chen et al. 2024).

Various definitions have been proposed for software fairness, generally categorised into individual fairness and group fairness. Individual fairness requires that similar individuals receive comparable outcomes from ML software (Galhotra et al. 2017), while group fairness emphasises equitable treatment across privileged and unprivileged groups within a protected category (Verma and Rubin 2018). Recent studies report that individual fairness detects subtler forms of discrimination than group-level metrics (Chen et al. 2024; Mamman et al. 2023). This has motivated increased research interest in individual fairness testing (IFT), which aims to identify individual discriminatory instances (IDIs) produced by a classifier under test (CuT) through systematic examination of input pairs that differ only in protected attributes yet receive different predictions (Galhotra et al. 2017).

Themis (Galhotra et al. 2017; Angell et al. 2018), the first automated fairness testing framework, pioneered IFT by randomly generating test inputs, perturbing their protected attribute values, and checking whether the classifier output changes. IDIs are detected when the output is different between the original and the counterfactual inputs.

Subsequent IFT methods frequently employ Search-Based Software Testing (SBST) techniques to detect IDIs (Mamman et al. 2023). For example, AEQUITAS (Udeshi et al. 2018) discovers IDIs through random search and then applies local perturbations to non-protected features to generate more IDIs in their neighbourhoods. ExpGA (Fan et al. 2022) uses local, model-agnostic explanation techniques to select high-quality seeds and then uses a genetic algorithm to search for IDIs. GRFT (Quan et al. 2025) uses a genetic algorithm to search for IDIs and then follows up with a lightweight random local search to discover more IDIs. Other approaches such as ADF (Zhang et al. 2021a) and EIDIG (Zhang et al. 2021b) exploit gradient information from deep neural networks to direct the search for IDIs.

These IFT methods are subject to three limitations:

  1. 1.

    Uniform Random Perturbation: Methods such as GRFT (Quan et al. 2025) and AEQUITAS (Udeshi et al. 2018) apply perturbations uniformly across all non-protected attributes without prioritising those most influential to classifier predictions. While computationally efficient, such approaches do not direct testing effort toward the features that provide informative guidance for exploring classifier decision boundaries where discriminatory behaviour is more likely to be observed. Although ExpGA (Fan et al. 2022) employs local explanations to identify high-quality seed instances, the insights derived from these explanations are not used to guide the test generation directly. An IFT framework is therefore needed that integrates an importance-aware perturbation strategy throughout the search process.

  2. 2.

    Limitations of Local Feature Importance for Bias Detection: Explanation-guided perturbation methods for IFT, such as ADF (Zhang et al. 2021a), EIDIG (Zhang et al. 2021b), and MAFT (Wang et al. 2024), rely on input-specific gradient signals to guide the search for discriminatory instances. Because these gradients, typically derived via backpropagation, are instance-specific, they must be recomputed for every candidate input evaluated, imposing high computational overhead, particularly during exhaustive searches (Zhang et al. 2021b). Gradient computation also requires access to hidden layer activations, rendering gradient-based methods intractable in model-agnostic or black-box settings where internal representations are unavailable (Wang et al. 2024; Fan et al. 2022). Beyond these efficiency concerns, local explanations cannot reliably capture the global structure of feature importance across the input space (Little et al. 2023). Since bias is a global statistical property of a classifier (Ghosh et al. 2023), local explanations may overlook discrimination patterns arising from higher-order feature interactions across the broader data distribution. This calls for IFT approaches that adopt a global view of feature influence to better inform the test generation process.

  3. 3.

    Test Redundancy: Existing IFT methods, such as GRFT (Quan et al. 2025), MAFT (Wang et al. 2024), ExpGA (Fan et al. 2022), and AEQUITAS (Udeshi et al. 2018), frequently generate redundant test cases by repeatedly sampling previously evaluated inputs. In the absence of explicit mechanisms to track search history or penalise re-evaluation, these approaches tend to converge on narrow regions of the input space, consuming computational resources without broadening the discovery of new IDIs. Search strategies are therefore needed that reduce redundancy while promoting the discovery of novel IDIs (Soremekun et al. 2025).

This study proposes FIFT (Feature Importance-Guided Fairness Testing), an evolutionary IFT framework that integrates global feature importance (GFI) to guide the search for IDIs. FIFT computes GFI once using permutation feature importance (PFI) and incorporates the normalised scores into the test generation process via a genetic algorithm. At the core of this framework is the Importance-Guided Hybrid Mutation (IHM) operator, which applies differential perturbation pressure based on a threshold of GFI scores: high-importance features above the threshold undergo conservative mutations scaled inversely to their importance scores, keeping candidates near critical decision boundaries, while low-importance features below the threshold receive broader random perturbations to promote exploration of the input space. This differential pressure induces an exploit–explore balance that reduces test redundancy while exposing a wider range of discriminatory behaviours. To reduce redundancy further, FIFT incorporates a duplicate-penalisation scheme that assigns minimum fitness to previously encountered instances, biasing the evolutionary search toward novel regions of the input space.

Although feature importance has been widely applied in feature selection, adversarial testing, and search-based optimization, existing individual fairness testing approaches have not incorporated reusable global feature importance as a continuous search signal throughout evolutionary fairness testing. Existing methods either rely on random perturbations, computationally expensive local explanations, or gradient information. FIFT fills this gap by integrating global feature importance directly into the mutation operator of a black-box evolutionary fairness testing framework.

We evaluated FIFT across five benchmark datasets and four ML classifiers. On average, FIFT detects 20.8%–190.4% more IDIs than state-of-the-art baseline methods, while achieving 2.37\(\times \)–3.1\(\times \) higher IDI throughput compared to methods that rely on per-instance local explanations. FIFT also reduces test redundancy by up to 91% relative to search-based baselines. Ablation studies confirm that the combination of PFI as the GFI estimator with a 25th percentile threshold, the IHM operator, and the duplicate-penalisation scheme together produce the most effective and efficient configuration for IFT.

The main contributions of this study are as follows:

  1. 1.

    We propose FIFT, an evolutionary-based IFT framework that integrates GFI with an evolutionary search operator to target and expose individual fairness violations in ML systems.

  2. 2.

    We design the IHM operator, which calibrates perturbation intensity according to feature importance, applying conservative mutations to high-importance features inversely proportional to their importance scores, while applying random mutations to low-importance ones. Unlike standard adversarial generation that alters influential features to flip predictions directly, IHM conditions non-protected features selectively to expose the model’s reliance on the protected attribute alone. We also apply a duplicate penalisation scheme that assigns minimum fitness to previously encountered instances, biasing the search toward novel regions of the input space. Together, these mechanisms produce an exploit–explore balance that improves IDI detection effectiveness and reduces test redundancy.

  3. 3.

    We conduct an empirical study across five benchmark datasets and four ML classifiers to evaluate the proposed FIFT framework. FIFT detects 20.8%–190.4% more IDIs on average than the baseline methods, reduces test redundancy by 91% relative to search-based methods, and achieves 2.37\(\times \)–3.1\(\times \) higher IDI throughput than methods relying on per-instance local explanations. Retraining classifiers on the discovered IDIs improves fairness by 28.6%–88.0% with negligible impact on predictive accuracy. Ablation studies confirm the contribution of each framework component and the stability of results across varying configurations.

  4. 4.

    We provide an open-source implementation of FIFT, processed datasets, and raw result logs as a complete experimental replication package at https://github.com/hmamman/FIFT.

2 Preliminaries

This section establishes the formal foundations for FIFT, covering the problem formulation, the role of feature importance in fairness testing, the exploit–explore balance that motivates the proposed mutation operator, and the IFT objective.

2.1 Problem formulation

We consider binary classification problems where an ML classifier \(\mathcal {M}\) maps input instances \(\textbf{x}\) to binary outcomes \(y \in \{0,1\}\). Following established practices in fairness testing literature (Chen et al. 2024), we adopt a black-box assumption where the internal architecture and parameterisation of \(\mathcal {M}\) remain inaccessible. This assumption ensures broad applicability across diverse ML classifier architectures while restricting attention to observable input-output relationships rather than implementation-specific details.

Each input instance is characterised by a set of attributes \(A = \{a_1, a_2, \ldots , a_n\}\), where each attribute \(a_i\) has an associated domain \(\text {Dom}(a_i) = \{v_{i1}, v_{i2}, \ldots , v_{im_i}\}\) defining its possible values. An individual I represents a specific instantiation of these attributes as an ordered tuple \(I = (I[a_1], I[a_2], \ldots , I[a_n])\), where \(I[a_i] \in \text {Dom}(a_i)\). The complete input space \(\mathcal {I}\) forms the Cartesian product \(\mathcal {I} = \text {Dom}(a_1) \times \text {Dom}(a_2) \times \cdots \times \text {Dom}(a_n)\), encompassing all possible attribute combinations.

2.2 Feature importance as a signal for discrimination

Feature importance quantifies the contribution of individual attributes to classifier predictions through scoring functions \(\phi (a_i)\) that assign numerical values \(FI_{a_i}\) reflecting each feature’s influence on classifier behaviour. Several studies have shown that influential features frequently correlate with protected attributes or act as proxies for discriminatory behaviour. For example, geographic location may perpetuate societal biases through indirect demographic associations (Kim et al. 2023; Adebayo and Kagal 2016; Ghosh et al. 2023). Disproportionate feature influence has also been associated with biased model outcomes (Khodadadian et al. 2021), while feature importance has been successfully used to audit and interpret fairness-related behaviour in machine learning models (Giannopoulos et al. 2024; Openja et al. 2023). These findings suggest that feature importance provides useful information about the model’s decision-making process and therefore offers a promising heuristic for guiding the search toward influential regions of the decision space where discriminatory behaviour is more likely to be uncovered.

Despite this potential, existing IFT methods primarily rely on random perturbations, local explanations, or gradient information to guide test generation, while feature importance is typically used only for post-hoc analysis or model auditing. The search process does not exploit global knowledge about which input features exert the greatest influence on classifier predictions. FIFT addresses this gap by integrating global feature importance directly into the mutation operator, using it as a reusable search heuristic to prioritise perturbations during evolutionary fairness testing.

2.3 Exploit–explore balance via feature importance

Search-based optimisation must balance exploitation (refining promising solutions near known optima) against exploration (sampling broadly to discover new promising regions) (Katoch et al. 2021). In fairness testing, this trade-off manifests directly: perturbations that are too aggressive may displace test inputs away from known discriminatory decision boundaries, while perturbations that are too conservative trap the search in narrow, local regions of the input space. A mutation operator must therefore calibrate perturbation intensity to balance exploration and exploitation.

We address this by introducing a mutation bias guided by global feature importance. Rather than applying a uniform mutation rate or random perturbation across all input dimensions, we exploit the varying influence that different features exert on the classifier’s predictions. As established in Section 2.2, features with the greatest influence on model outcomes frequently correlate with discriminatory behaviour. Therefore, functionally critical features (those with high global importance scores) require a conservative perturbation strategy. By restricting mutations to these highly influential dimensions, the search algorithm exploits the known discriminatory boundaries, ensuring that candidate instances do not randomly drift into purely non-discriminatory regions.

Features with low predictive influence, by contrast, admit aggressive exploration. Because sweeping changes to these less-important dimensions are unlikely to alter the classifier’s core decision logic, the search can freely sample across the entire domain without degrading the objective. By calibrating the perturbation intensity inversely to feature importance, the search allocates its mutation budget, exploiting sensitive decision boundaries by preserving high-importance values, and exploring diverse combinations of low-importance attributes to broaden input-space coverage. This feature-importance-driven exploit–explore principle motivates the design of our dual-mode mutation operator, detailed in Section 3.

2.4 Individual fairness testing

Individual fairness testing aims to identify discriminatory instances through systematic examination of input pairs that differ only in protected attributes yet receive different classifier predictions (Galhotra et al. 2017). Let \(f: X \rightarrow Y\) denote the CuT, where \(X \subseteq \mathbb {R}^d\) represents the input feature space of dimension d, and Y denotes the output space (classification labels). Each input instance \(\textbf{x} \in X\) can be partitioned into two subsets: the protected attributes \(\textbf{x}_p \in X_p\) (e.g., gender, race, or age) and the non-protected attributes \(\textbf{x}_{np} \in X_{np}\). An IDI is defined as a pair of inputs \((\textbf{x}, \textbf{x}')\) such that:

$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{x}_{np} = \textbf{x}'_{np}, \quad \textbf{x}_p \ne \textbf{x}'_p, \quad \text {and} \quad f(\textbf{x}) \ne f(\textbf{x}') \end{aligned}$$
(1)

Under this formalisation, two individuals who are identical on all non-protected attributes but differ in protected attributes should not receive different predictions. If \(f(\textbf{x}) \ne f(\textbf{x}')\), the classifier violates individual fairness with respect to \(\textbf{x}\). The systematic identification of such instances requires efficient exploration strategies that can navigate the exponentially large input space while prioritising regions most likely to exhibit discriminatory behaviour.

The goal of IFT is to efficiently generate candidate instances \(\textbf{x} \in X\) that are most likely to uncover IDIs. Formally, let \(\mathcal {T}\) denote the set of all unique candidate instances generated during the search process, and let \(\mathcal {T}_{disc} \subseteq \mathcal {T}\) denote the subset of discriminatory instances. The objective is to maximise \(|\mathcal {T}_{disc}|\) with respect to \(|\mathcal {T}|\).

3 Methodology

3.1 Overview of the FIFT framework

FIFT integrates GFI analysis with a genetic algorithm to efficiently uncover IDIs. Unlike prior methods that rely on random perturbations or local explanation signals, FIFT employs a one-time computation of GFI scores to guide the mutation operations throughout the search. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the framework operates in two main phases: feature-importance preprocessing and a modified genetic algorithm.

In the first phase, GFI scores are computed using the dataset and the CuT through PFI. The computed scores are normalised to produce a global importance distribution, from which a threshold \(\theta \) is derived to partition features into high-importance (\(F_i > \theta \)) and low-importance (\(F_i \le \theta \)) subsets. This partition directly guides how the mutation operation behaves during the second phase.

The second phase begins with the initialisation of a population of candidate test inputs within the valid feature domain. Each candidate is then evaluated for individual fairness violations by comparing its prediction against those of its protected-attribute counterfactuals; candidates that reveal discriminatory behaviour receive higher fitness scores. Duplicate candidates are penalised by reducing their fitness scores. During the selection operations, candidates with higher fitness scores (those that exhibit discriminatory behaviour) are preferentially selected for reproduction. A standard uniform crossover operation is applied to the selected candidates to facilitate the exchange of genetic material between parents and produce offspring. The IHM applies differentiated mutation pressures based on feature importance, which strikes a deliberate balance between exploitation and exploration to detect more IDIs while reducing redundancy.

3.2 Global feature importance computation

FIFT computes GFI once and reuses it throughout the evolutionary search. This avoids per-candidate explanation overhead and, more importantly, allows FIFT to target perturbations to the features most likely to induce discriminatory behaviour. Prior work provides both empirical and theoretical support for this relationship: a classifier relies most heavily on high-importance features when constructing its primary decision boundaries. Conservative test case perturbations that remain near these highly weighted dimensions are therefore more likely to traverse a decision boundary and induce a flipped prediction (fairness violation) than unguided, multidimensional exploration (Adebayo and Kagal 2016; Ewald et al. 2024; Ribeiro et al. 2016).

FIFT adopts PFI (Altmann et al. 2010; Breiman 2001) as the default GFI estimator. PFI is a widely used model-agnostic technique (Molnar 2025) that estimates the contribution of individual features directly by evaluating the error increase caused by permuting a feature’s values across the validation set. If a feature is important, shuffling its values noticeably degrades the model’s performance because the model loses access to the true information (Scikit-Learn 2024). While PFI can be sensitive to strongly correlated features, it was selected because it is strictly model-agnostic, easily scalable, and provides a sufficiently accurate global heuristic to guide the genetic algorithm.

Formally, the algorithm begins by calculating the original model error \(\epsilon _{orig} = L(Y, \mathcal {M}(X_{val}))\) using the trained classifier \(\mathcal {M}\) on validation data \(X_{val}\). For each feature \(j \in \{1,\ldots ,d\}\), its values are randomly permuted to break the relationship between the feature and the target variable Y, yielding a permuted dataset \(X_{val, perm}^{(j)}\). The error is recomputed as \(\epsilon _{perm} = L(Y, \mathcal {M}(X_{val, perm}^{(j)}))\). The unnormalised importance score for feature j is then determined as:

$$\begin{aligned} FI_j = \epsilon _{perm} - \epsilon _{orig} \end{aligned}$$
(2)

As outlined in Algorithm 1, the initial baseline error is calculated on the validation data (Line 1). For each feature, the data is permuted and the new error is computed (Lines 2-4) to determine the raw importance score (Line 5). These scores are collected and centralised into a normalised distribution (Line 7), before the appropriate 25th percentile threshold is formulated (Line 8).

This approach evaluates feature contributions systematically across the entire dataset rather than on a per-instance basis, ensuring that the GFI is global by construction. This overcomes the limitation of existing methods that rely on local, instance-specific gradient or explanation heuristics. Finally, we normalise the vector such that it sums to one:

$$\begin{aligned} \bar{F}_j = \frac{FI_j}{\sum _{k=1}^{d} FI_k} \end{aligned}$$
(3)

This produces the global feature importance distribution \(\textbf{F}\) used to guide mutation operations. FIFT also supports alternative estimators including SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) (Lundberg and Lee 2017) or Gini importance (Breiman 2001).

3.2.1 Thresholding strategy

To separate high-importance features from low-importance features, FIFT applies a threshold \(\theta \) to the normalised distribution \(\textbf{F}\). Features with \(\bar{F}_j > \theta \) are treated as critical and undergo the conservative, proximity-preserving mutation operator, whereas those with \(\bar{F}_j \le \theta \) undergo aggressive exploration via reset mutation.

Several thresholding strategies have been proposed in the literature. A fixed cutoff of 0.05 has been used as a simple rule for feature selection (Prasetiyowati et al. 2021; Altmann et al. 2010). Other studies have applied the mean of feature weights as the cutoff (Pedregosa et al. 2011), while some have relied on the standard deviation of feature weights to capture attributes that deviate significantly from the mean (Prasetiyowati et al. 2021). More recently, percentile-based thresholds have been explored, with related works examining quartile cutoffs such as the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles (Altmann et al. 2010; Chandrashekar and Sahin 2014; Van Hulse et al. 2012).

FIFT adopts a percentile-based threshold. Specifically, we use the 25th percentile of the normalised distribution as the cutoff:

$$\begin{aligned} \theta = Q_{25}(\textbf{F}) \end{aligned}$$
(4)

This choice ensures that the bottom quartile of features is marked for exploration, while the remaining 75% are treated conservatively. Compared to mean or fixed cutoffs, which often converge to similar values after normalisation (Pedregosa et al. 2011; Prasetiyowati et al. 2021), the 25th percentile provides a distribution-aware and adaptive cutoff, particularly suitable for skewed or heavy-tailed feature-importance profiles, where mean- and standard-deviation-based thresholds are known to be sensitive to extreme values (Tukey 1977; Leys et al. 2013). This aligns with standard percentile-based filtering methodologies, which routinely discard bottom-quartile features as a noise-reduction strategy (Van Hulse et al. 2012; Chandrashekar and Sahin 2014). We note that this threshold is an empirical design choice validated through ablation (Section 5.5.1), not a theoretically derived optimum; alternative percentile thresholds (e.g., the 50th percentile) give comparable performance. Empirically, the importance profiles of the evaluated dataset–classifier pairs are strongly concentrated: the features at or below the 25th percentile threshold hold only 1.2% of total normalised importance mass on average (Section 5.8), so the explore set is drawn from dimensions with negligible influence on the classifier’s predictions.

3.3 Importance-Guided Hybrid Mutation (IHM)

Traditional genetic algorithms apply uniform mutation operators that treat all solution dimensions equally, distributing identical mutation probabilities and magnitudes across the entire solution space. Drawing on the exploit–explore principle established in Section 2.3, we design an adaptive mutation operator that calibrates perturbation intensity based on feature importance. Features most influential to classifier predictions should receive conservative perturbations to preserve proximity to discriminatory decision boundaries, while less influential features warrant aggressive exploration to broaden input-space coverage. This reasoning leads to the proposed IHM operator.

IHM implements a dual-mode mutation strategy based on a feature importance threshold \(\theta \). For each feature j of candidate \(\textbf{x}_i\), mutation is applied with probability \(p_m\). If selected, the mutation proceeds according to the feature’s global importance score \(\textbf{F}_j\).

Inverse Importance Mutation (IIM)

For high-importance features (\(\textbf{F}_j > \theta \)), we apply Inverse Importance Mutation (IIM), where the perturbation magnitude is inversely proportional to the feature importance:

$$\begin{aligned} x'_{i,j} = x_{i,j} + \alpha \cdot (1 - \textbf{F}_j) \cdot \Delta _j \cdot \epsilon , \quad \epsilon \sim U(-1,1) \end{aligned}$$
(5)

where \(\Delta _j = (b_{u,j} - b_{l,j})\) denotes the maximum allowable perturbation range (defined by the upper and lower bounded domain limits) for feature j, and \(\alpha \in (0,1)\) is a user-defined scaling factor controlling the baseline mutation intensity. This formulation ensures that highly important features (\(\textbf{F}_j \rightarrow 1\)) undergo proportionally minimal perturbation, thereby preserving the candidate’s proximity to sensitive discriminatory decision boundaries.

Random mutation

For low-importance features (\(\textbf{F}_j \le \theta \)), we apply a random reset mutation to promote exploration:

$$\begin{aligned} x'_{i,j} = U(b_{l,j}, b_{u,j}) \end{aligned}$$
(6)

where \(b_{l,j}\) and \(b_{u,j}\) denote the lower and upper bounds of feature j, respectively. The IHM operator can be expressed as:

$$\begin{aligned} x'_{i,j} = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} x_{i,j} + \alpha (1 - \textbf{F}_j)\Delta _j \epsilon , & \text {if } \textbf{F}_j > \theta \\ U(b_{l,j}, b_{u,j}), & \text {otherwise} \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$
(7)

By combining IIM with random mutation, FIFT achieves a deliberate balance between exploitation (refining candidates near discriminatory regions) and exploration (covering underexplored regions of the input space).

Algorithm 2 formalises this process. For each feature (Line 2), mutation is applied probabilistically (Line 3). High-importance features activate the IIM branch (Line 5), while low-importance features trigger random reset mutation (Line 7). The resulting value is clipped to ensure validity within the input domain (Line 9). This design mitigates premature convergence while improving search diversity, thereby increasing the likelihood of discovering novel IDIs.

Our ablation study (Section 5) confirms the effectiveness of IHM compared to either using random mutation or inverse importance mutation alone.

3.4 Duplicate penalisation

Fitness evaluation determines whether a test input generated by the GA uncovers discrimination in the CuT. FIFT’s fitness function follows the individual fairness criterion standard in IFT, which requires that individuals differing only in protected attributes receive the same prediction outcomes (Galhotra et al. 2017). Thus, IDIs are detected when:

$$\begin{aligned} \text {IDI}(\textbf{x}) \iff \exists p \in P: f(\textbf{x}) \ne f(\textbf{x}^{(p)}) \end{aligned}$$
(8)

where \(\textbf{x}\) is a test input, p is a protected attribute, and \(\textbf{x}^{(p)}\) is a counterfactual input obtained by altering only p while holding other features fixed.

To perform discrimination checks on the candidates, FIFT follows a similar approach as in Quan et al. (2025). We construct the full set of protected-attribute counterfactuals for each candidate \(\textbf{x}\) by replacing the protected coordinate with every integer value in its permitted domain. Then we compare their predictions to detect IDIs. Let s be the index of the protected attribute and let its valid domain be \([b_l, b_u] \cap \mathbb {Z}\). For a candidate \(\textbf{x}\), define the similar set:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {S}(\textbf{x}) = \{\textbf{x}' : x'_s \in [b_l, b_u], \forall j \ne s: x'_j = x_j\} \end{aligned}$$
(9)

On every generation, FIFT performs predictions on base candidates (test inputs) and their counterfactual variants to detect IDIs. For each candidate \(\textbf{x}_i\), the fitness is the number of distinct predictions across its base and all protected variants:

$$\begin{aligned} \text {fit}(\textbf{x}_i) = |\{f(\textbf{x}') : \textbf{x}' \in \{\textbf{x}_i\} \cup \mathcal {S}(\textbf{x}_i)\}| \end{aligned}$$
(10)

Hence, \(\text {fit}(\textbf{x}_i) > 1\) iff \(\textbf{x}_i\) produces distinct predictions across at least one pair of its counterfactuals, indicating that an IDI has been discovered. Candidates that do not trigger a fairness violation receive a fitness of 1.

To discourage redundant exploration, FIFT adopts a duplicate-penalisation mechanism during fitness evaluation, drawing on standard practice in genetic algorithms where fitness shaping is used to control population diversity (Katoch et al. 2021). While the primary fitness function is defined strictly by discrimination detection (Equation 10), we implement duplicate tracking as a supplementary redundancy control step. Before computing fitness, each candidate is checked against a global set of all previously evaluated inputs. Any candidate whose feature vector has already been observed is immediately assigned the minimum fitness score of 1, regardless of whether it actually triggers a fairness violation. By artificially equalising the fitness of identical candidates with those of non-discriminatory instances, this penalisation logic ensures they are not preferentially selected for reproduction, freeing the evolutionary search to explore novel regions of the input space.

Following fitness evaluation, FIFT employs tournament selection to form the next generation. Tournament selection is widely adopted in evolutionary computation for its controllable selective pressure and computational simplicity: unlike rank- or roulette-wheel-based schemes, it does not require global sorting, making it more efficient (Katoch et al. 2021). Let N be the population size, \(k = \max (5,\, \lfloor 0.05N \rfloor )\) the tournament size, and \(\text {fit}(\textbf{x}_i)\) the fitness of the i-th candidate. The algorithm first computes normalised sampling weights:

$$\begin{aligned} T_i = \frac{\text {fit}(\textbf{x}_i)}{\sum _{j=1}^{N} \text {fit}(\textbf{x}_j)} \end{aligned}$$
(11)

For each of N offspring draws, a tournament set of size k is sampled without replacement from \(\{1, \ldots , N\}\) using T as sampling probabilities, and the individual with the highest fitness within that set is promoted to the next population. By biasing participant selection toward higher-fitness individuals and then deterministically choosing the best within each tournament, this design produces a stable selective pressure that favours novel, discriminatory candidates.

4 Experimental setup

4.1 Research Questions

FIFT is evaluated using the following research questions (RQ):

  • RQ1: How effective is the proposed FIFT in detecting individual discriminatory instances? The primary objective of any IFT method is to detect as many IDIs as possible within a given testing budget. This question evaluates whether FIFT can detect a higher number of IDIs than existing fairness testing approaches across diverse datasets and classifier architectures. We adopt two evaluation metrics to quantify effectiveness. The total number of discriminatory instances (#IDI) counts the unique test inputs that violate individual fairness. The generation success rate (GSR) measures the density of discovered fairness violations, calculated as the percentage of IDIs found relative to the total number of unique samples generated (\(GSR = \frac{\#IDI}{TNS} \times 100\%\)), where TNS denotes the total number of non-duplicate samples generated by an approach.

  • RQ2: To what extent does the proposed framework avoid generating redundant test cases? A prevalent limitation of existing IFT methods is the generation of duplicate test instances, which consume computational resources without contributing novel fairness insights. High redundancy rates reduce the effective coverage of the input space and artificially increase the apparent testing effort. We quantify redundancy using the duplicate generation rate (\(DGR = \frac{\beta -TNS}{\beta } \times 100\%\)), where \(\beta \) denotes the total number of test inputs generated by an approach.

  • RQ3: How efficient is the proposed FIFT in generating individual discriminatory instances? Beyond absolute effectiveness, the rate at which an IFT method can discover discriminatory instances matters in practice, particularly when testing large-scale or computationally expensive classifiers under constrained time budgets. This question compares the computational efficiency of FIFT against the baselines. We measured efficiency using the effective generation speed (EGS), defined as the number of discriminatory instances detected per second. A higher EGS indicates that the method can produce actionable fairness evidence more rapidly, making it better suited for integration into other systems such as continuous testing pipelines.

  • RQ4: How useful are the generated individual discriminatory instances in improving the fairness of the model? This question evaluates the practical utility of the discovered IDIs by using them as an augmented training set for model repair through fairness retraining. We assess the impact of this retraining on both model performance and fairness robustness. Model performance is measured using accuracy to check that fairness improvements do not come at the cost of classification accuracy. To evaluate global fairness robustness beyond the testing set, we adopt the individual fairness on random data (\(IF_r\)), which measures the proportion (between 0.0 and 1.0) of discriminatory instances discovered within a large set of random samples (\(N = 10,000\)) drawn uniformly from the input space. A lower \(IF_r\) after retraining indicates that the model has developed broader resistance to discriminatory behaviour.

  • RQ5: How does each core design choice in FIFT contribute to its effectiveness and efficiency? The performance of FIFT is driven by several key design decisions. This question validates those decisions through a systematic ablation study, isolating the effect of each component and assessing the sensitivity of the framework to key parameter choices, including the choice of the GFI estimator, the thresholding strategy, the duplicate-penalisation mechanism, and the mutation operator.

To address these research questions, experiments were conducted across five benchmark datasets using four different ML classifiers.

These datasets are commonly referenced in the fairness testing literature (Chen et al. 2024) and include Bank Marketing (Moro et al. 2014), Census Income (Becker and Kohavi 1996), COMPAS (Angwin et al. 2016), German Credit (Hofmann 1994), and Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (Agency and Research 2018) datasets.

  2. 2.

    German Credit (Hofmann 1994): With 600 samples and 20 features, this dataset supports the prediction of credit risk levels (classified as “good” or “bad”). The protected attributes in this dataset are gender and age.

  3. 3.

    Adult Census Income (Becker and Kohavi 1996): This dataset, containing 48,842 records, is used for predicting whether an individual’s annual income exceeds $50,000. The dataset includes 13 attributes, among which the protected attributes are gender, race, and age.

  4. 4.

    COMPAS (Angwin et al. 2016): This dataset includes 7,214 samples and 12 features. It is used to evaluate recidivism risk among individuals charged with crimes. The protected attributes include age, race, and gender.

  5. 5.

    Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (Agency and Research 2018): Containing 15,675 records and 40 features, this dataset is designed to predict healthcare needs. The protected attribute in this dataset is gender.

4.3 Classifiers

Four ML classifiers widely used for fairness evaluation (Galhotra et al. 2017; Aggarwal et al. 2019; Mamman et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2021b; Udeshi et al. 2018) were employed to evaluate the FIFT framework: Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), Logistic Regression (LR), and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). To train the models and establish their baseline performance, each dataset was partitioned into an 80% training set and a 20% test set. The parameter settings used for each classifier are presented in Table 2.

4.4 Baseline approaches

Five state-of-the-art (SOTA) fairness testing approaches were selected as baselines to compare FIFT against the diverse search guidance strategies in the IFT literature, as summarised in Table 3.

The selected baselines span a range of methodologies: GRFT (Quan et al. 2025) uses a two-phase Genetic-Random approach; ExpGA (Fan et al. 2022) integrates local LIME explainability with a genetic algorithm; EIDIG (Zhang et al. 2021b) and MAFT (Wang et al. 2024) represent gradient-driven searches in white-box and black-box settings, respectively; and AFT (Zhao et al. 2024) employs verification via decision-tree subspace approximations. These baseline methods are briefly described as follows:

GRFT (Quan et al. 2025) operates in two distinct phases to boost testing efficiency. In its global phase, GRFT employs a genetic algorithm to generate a large volume of global IDIs from an initial seed. Subsequently, its local phase uses a lightweight random search strategy to explore the neighbourhoods of the global instances, discovering additional IDIs without heavy computational overhead.

ExpGA (Fan et al. 2022) integrates local explainability with a genetic algorithm to detect discriminatory instances in ML classifiers. ExpGA uses LIME (Ribeiro et al. 2016) to extract explanation results that identify high-quality initial seed instances exhibiting potential discrimination. It then employs standard genetic operators (mutation, crossover) to systematically generate a large volume of IDIs from these seeds.

EIDIG (Zhang et al. 2021b) is a white-box gradient-based testing framework designed for deep learning systems. EIDIG operates in two phases: a global generation phase that produces diverse discriminatory seeds guided by the gradient of the classifier’s output, and a local generation phase that expands on these seeds to identify greater volumes of fairness violations. The technique exploits prior gradient information from successive iterations to accelerate convergence and reduce edge-case gradient recalculations.

MAFT (Wang et al. 2024) is a black-box fairness testing approach for deep neural networks. Unlike white-box gradient methods, MAFT achieves classifier independence by using zero-order gradient search (gradient estimation via asymmetric difference quotients) instead of true backpropagation gradients. This allows it to iteratively perturb input attributes to expose fairness defects without requiring access to the classifier’s internal architecture.

AFT (Zhao et al. 2024) is a verification-based fairness testing methodology that uses a systematic subspace sampling approach. Rather than relying on random search, AFT trains a decision tree surrogate on the classifier’s predictions to partition the input space into non-overlapping deterministic subspaces, confining the generation of test cases within these partitions to uncover more IDIs.

We note that MAFT and EIDIG are evaluated only on the MLP classifier, as both require gradient information not available for non-differentiable classifiers such as DT, RF, and LR. All five SOTA approaches are implemented using the optimal hyperparameter settings specified in their original papers.

4.5 Settings

All methods are evaluated under identical constraints: a maximum budget of \(S_{\max } = 1{,}000{,}000\) generated samples or a maximum wall-clock time of one hour, whichever occurs first (Wang et al. 2024; Quan et al. 2025; Fan et al. 2022). This universal budget is allocated according to each method’s operational paradigm and verified at the conclusion of each algorithmic cycle, so that no method receives disproportionate computational resources. We did not impose any additional constraints on the methods, such as limiting the number of iterations or generations. Each configuration was executed 10 independent times so that reported results reflect the distributional behaviour of each approach under stochastic variation. FIFT’s parameter settings are detailed in Table 4.

4.6 Implementation details

To compute global feature importance scores, PFI (Altmann et al. 2010; Breiman 2001) was used as the default estimator. We implemented PFI using the permutation_importance module from the Scikit-Learn library (Scikit-Learn 2024). To compute stable and reliable importance estimations, a 20% validation split of the complete dataset was used for PFI computation, with the performance drop averaged across 5 permutations per feature (\(n\_repeats=5\)). Although GFI computation is a one-time preprocessing step, its cost is included in the total elapsed time used to compute EGS (Section 5.3), ensuring a fair efficiency comparison. For the alternative SHAP estimator evaluated in the ablation study (Section 5), appropriate explainers were selected based on classifier type (TreeExplainer (Lundberg et al. 2020), DeepExplainer (Lundberg and Lee 2017), etc.), and a subset of up to 100 representative background samples was drawn uniformly to offset the severe computational overhead inherent to local SHAP attribution logic. MLP classifiers were implemented using the TensorFlow library (Abadi et al. 2015), while all other ML classifiers were implemented using the Scikit-Learn library (Pedregosa et al. 2011).

Experiments were performed on a Dell PowerEdge T40 desktop computer equipped with Ubuntu 22.04 as the operating system. The system has 8GB of installed RAM and an Intel® Xeon® E-2224G CPU with 3.50GHz and four cores. It also used a Mesa Intel® UHD Graphics P630 for graphics processing.

5 Results and discussion

This section presents the results of our empirical evaluation of the FIFT framework across five benchmark datasets and four ML classifiers to answer the research questions on IDI detection effectiveness, redundancy reduction, operational efficiency, fairness improvement, and the robustness of our design choices.

5.1 RQ1: effectiveness of FIFT

Tables 5 and 6 present the effectiveness of each approach as measured by #IDI and GSR, respectively.

Number of Discriminatory Instances (#IDI)

On the LR classifier, FIFT achieves the highest IDI discovery volume, generating 651,134 IDIs on average. This result is 1.47 times that of AFT (442,320), 2.15 times that of GRFT (302,615), and 2.96 times that of ExpGA (220,083). This indicates that the global heuristic provides better search guidance than local approximations or pure random sampling. For tree-based classifiers (DT and RF), the margins are larger. For DT, FIFT discovers 963,138 IDIs, 1.21 times that of AFT (797,153) and 3.91 times that of GRFT (246,561). Similarly, on RF, FIFT maintains high IDI generation effectiveness with 887,517 IDIs, which is 1.50 times that of AFT (590,070), 3.14 times that of GRFT (283,083), and 8.86 times that of ExpGA (100,194). Finally, on MLP, FIFT outperforms approximation-guided and gradient-parameterised baselines. FIFT generates 717,824 IDIs on average: 2.90 times that of AFT (247,195), 2.47 times that of GRFT (290,665), 6.90 times that of MAFT (104,058), and 6.91 times that of EIDIG (103,828). This margin suggests that avoiding gradient and decision-tree approximations allows FIFT to explore the input space of neural networks more broadly.

Generation Success Rate (GSR)

In addition to absolute IDI discovery volume, we analyse the precision of each approach via the GSR, which measures the percentage of unique test inputs that successfully reveal discrimination. Across non-MLP classifiers, FIFT records the highest GSR. On the LR classifier, FIFT records a GSR of 69.89%, indicating that 69.89% of the generated unique test inputs are IDIs. This exceeds the success rate of all the remaining baselines: GRFT (62.15%), AFT (46.84%), and ExpGA (40.49%). The same pattern holds on tree-based classifiers: FIFT attains a 97.64% GSR on DT and a 91.82% GSR on RF, compared with the best baselines, ExpGA (79.50% on DT) and AFT (62.03% on RF). On MLP, FIFT maintains a GSR of 75.43%, exceeding AFT (34.11%), ExpGA (49.74%), and GRFT (56.05%). Gradient-based methods such as MAFT (78.39%) and EIDIG (78.51%) achieve a marginally higher GSR on MLP due to their local gradient-based guidance, but they performed worse on all the other metrics. Thus, FIFT achieves the best balance of absolute discrimination discovery (#IDI) and generation precision (GSR) among the evaluated methods.

We attribute the performance advantage of FIFT across both #IDI and GSR metrics to its IHM strategy, which uses global feature importance to guide the test generation. IHM partitions the feature space into high- and low-importance groups to bias mutations. High-importance features are perturbed inversely proportionate to their importance scores, while low-importance features receive random perturbations. As high-importance features are primary drivers of model decision boundaries, this targeted perturbation strategy increases the likelihood of generating inputs that expose discriminatory behaviour. Although gradient-based methods such as MAFT and EIDIG record a marginally higher GSR on MLP (78.39% and 78.51% respectively, against FIFT’s 75.43%), their dependence on local gradient information severely constrains absolute discrimination discovery, producing #IDI counts approximately 6.9 times lower than FIFT on the same classifier. These results indicate that generation precision alone can be insufficient as a measure of IFT effectiveness; an effective approach must sustain both precision and discovery volume across diverse classifier architectures (Fig. 2).

5.2 RQ2: Redundancy avoidance

Table 7 presents the average DGR recorded by each approach across all datasets and sensitive attributes, per classifier. DGR measures the percentage of redundant instances generated by each approach. Figure 3 visualises the results. As seen, FIFT records the lowest DGR among search-based methods (GRFT, ExpGA, MAFT, and EIDIG) across all classifier architectures. On LR classifier for example, FIFT achieves an average DGR of 7.48%, which is 6.7 times lower than GRFT (49.86%) and 6.3 times lower than ExpGA (46.73%). On DT, FIFT reduces redundancy further to an average DGR of 1.38%, compared to 67.36% for GRFT (48.8 times lower) and 43.79% for ExpGA (31.7 times lower). On RF, FIFT records an average DGR of 3.44%, which is 14.4 times lower than GRFT (49.70%) and 12.9 times lower than ExpGA (44.43%). On MLP, where gradient-based methods are additionally evaluated, FIFT records an average DGR of 5.59%, 8.4 times lower than GRFT (46.96%), 14.5 times lower than ExpGA (80.89%), 9.9 times lower than MAFT (55.07%), and 9.97 times lower than EIDIG (55.76%).

We attribute the low DGR recorded by FIFT to two complementary mechanisms. First, the duplicate-penalisation mechanism described in Section 3.4 checks every candidate on each generation against a global registry of all previously evaluated inputs. Any candidate whose feature vector has already been observed is immediately assigned a minimum fitness of 1, regardless of its discriminatory status. This directly discourages the search from revisiting previously explored regions and redirects evolutionary pressure toward novel areas of the input space. Second, our IHM operator (Section 3.3) contributes to population diversity through its dual-mode mutation strategy. Because high-importance features correlate with discriminatory classifier behaviour, conservative perturbations on these dimensions keep candidates within regions most likely to expose fairness violations. Low-importance features, by contrast, contribute less to classifier decisions and can tolerate aggressive random resets without displacing candidates from discriminatory regions, thereby continuously reintroducing structural diversity across the population. These two mechanisms prevent the population from converging on a narrow region of the input space, which is the primary cause of the high redundancy rates observed in most of the baseline methods, i.e., GRFT, ExpGA, MAFT, and EIDIG.

On the other hand, AFT, a verification-based method, records lower DGR than FIFT across all classifier architectures (3.53%, 0.01%, 0.20%, and 0.16% on LR, DT, RF, and MLP respectively). However, this advantage is a structural consequence of AFT’s deterministic subspace sampling strategy, which partitions the input space into non-overlapping decision tree subspaces and confines the search within each partition. This design eliminates inter-region revisits by construction, but it is not directly comparable to search-based methods that traverse the input space freely and non-deterministically. In search-based IFT, the probability of revisiting previously evaluated inputs increases naturally as the population converges, since the search is not structurally constrained to disjoint regions. The contribution of FIFT is that it achieves near-AFT levels of redundancy control within this unconstrained search space, while detecting 1.21–2.90 times as many IDIs as AFT (as reported in RQ1). This shows that the duplicate-penalisation and IHM mechanisms reduce redundancy in search-based IFT without sacrificing discrimination discovery.

5.3 RQ3: Efficiency of FIFT

The EGS metric measures the number of IDIs detected per second and is used here to evaluate the operational efficiency of each approach. For FIFT, the reported EGS includes the one-time PFI preprocessing cost in the total elapsed time, ensuring that all computational overhead is reflected in the efficiency comparison. Table 8 presents the average EGS recorded by each method across all datasets and sensitive attributes. FIFT outperformed all baselines that rely on local explanations (ExpGA), gradient-guided search (MAFT and EIDIG), or verification-based sampling (AFT). On the LR classifier, FIFT recorded 7,297 EGS, which is 3.2 times higher than AFT (2,312 EGS) and 10.2 times higher than ExpGA (717 EGS). Similarly, on DT, FIFT recorded 11,135 EGS, which is 1.4 times higher than AFT (7,726 EGS) and 9.0 times higher than ExpGA (1,236 EGS). The gap is wider on the RF classifier, where FIFT recorded 6,960 EGS, which is 23.4 times higher than AFT (297 EGS) and 232.0 times higher than ExpGA (30 EGS). On MLP, FIFT recorded 6,787 EGS, which is 76.3 times higher than AFT (89 EGS), 135.7 times higher than ExpGA (50 EGS), and over 193 times higher than both MAFT (34 EGS) and EIDIG (35 EGS). These results indicate that the one-time global heuristic scales better than local explanation queries (ExpGA), per-instance gradient computation (MAFT and EIDIG), or continuous subspace mapping (AFT).

GRFT, however, outperformed FIFT in EGS on simpler classifiers such as LR and DT, recording 22,894 EGS and 13,918 EGS respectively, compared to FIFT’s 7,297 EGS and 11,135 EGS. Its speed advantage on these architectures stems primarily from its lightweight unit-step local search, which incurs minimal per-candidate inference cost. On more complex classifiers such as RF and MLP, where inference overhead is higher, this advantage diminishes and FIFT matches or exceeds GRFT in EGS. This suggests that FIFT strikes a more consistent balance between generation speed and IDI detection volume across classifier architectures, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

5.4 RQ4: Fairness improvement

The usefulness of the generated IDIs can be measured by their ability to repair the underlying biased models. Similar to the way adversarial training improves model robustness by incorporating adversarial examples (Madry et al. 2017), we can retrain target models on a mixture of original data and newly discovered IDIs to mitigate fairness violations (Zhang et al. 2021b; Fan et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2024).

To utilise the discovered IDIs for model repair, they must first be assigned appropriate labels to guide the classifier toward equitable decision boundaries. Following the methodology of EIDIG (Zhang et al. 2021b), we constructed a surrogate ensemble classifier composed of k-Nearest Neighbors, Multi-Layer Perceptron, Support Vector Machine, Random Forest, and Gaussian Naive Bayes models. From the total pool of IDIs generated during the testing phase, we randomly sampled 5% and determined their ground-truth labels based on the ensemble’s majority vote. These labelled instances were then appended to the original training set, allowing the target classifier to be retrained on this augmented dataset.

To quantify the success of this bias mitigation strategy, we measured the Individual Fairness Ratio (\(IF_r\)) before and after retraining. Following EIDIG (Zhang et al. 2021b), we randomly sampled 10,000 instances from the input domain to compute the \(IF_r\). Table 9 details the impact of this retraining process. On the LR classifier, FIFT and AFT jointly achieve the greatest fairness improvement, dropping the average \(IF_r\) from 0.037 to 0.007 (an 81.1% reduction). For the DT classifier, FIFT achieves the largest improvement across all methods, reducing the initial \(IF_r\) from 0.183 to 0.022 (an 88.0% reduction). On the RF classifier, AFT achieves the lowest post-retraining \(IF_r\) (0.022, down from 0.105), with FIFT second at 0.027. For the MLP classifier, FIFT achieves the lowest average post-retraining \(IF_r\) of 0.020 (down from 0.028).

This improvement in fairness is achieved without compromising the predictive performance of the models. As shown in Table 10, the average classification accuracy across all four architectures marginally increased from 0.880 to 0.890 after retraining. While the LR classifier experienced a minor accuracy decrease (0.818 to 0.793), the DT and RF classifiers gained accuracy, rising from 0.928 and 0.942 respectively to 0.966. This suggests that the high-volume IDIs generated by FIFT, when accurately relabeled via the ensemble majority vote, act as an effective data augmentation strategy that helps models better resolve decision boundaries in previously sparse or ambiguous regions of the feature space.

5.5 RQ5: Ablation study

To understand the core components contributing to FIFT’s detection capabilities, an ablation and robustness study was conducted (answering RQ5). This analysis isolates the impact of four design choices: the strategy employed during the mutation phase (IHM), the choice of global feature importance estimator alongside its corresponding thresholding method, the effectiveness of the duplicate-penalisation mechanism, and the robustness of the search guidance to SHAP background sample sizes. By evaluating these configurations independently, we assess which design choices are most consequential for FIFT’s performance and stability across diverse benchmark classifiers.

5.5.1 Feature importance thresholding strategies and GFI estimators

Table 11 compares the performance of three GFI estimators (PFI, surrogate classifiers, and SHAP) across various thresholding strategies.

PFI combined with the 25th percentile threshold detected an average of 198,542 IDIs across the classifiers while operating globally without the overhead of local heuristics.

We note, however, that the performance difference between the 25th and 50th percentile thresholds is modest: 198,610 vs. 177,666 average IDIs for the alternative SHAP estimator (an 11.8% margin). This indicates that FIFT’s performance is not sharply sensitive to the exact percentile choice within this range, and the 50th percentile remains a competitive alternative. The margin widens at more extreme thresholds: the 75th percentile (150,056 IDIs) restricts too many features to conservative mutation, reducing exploration, while the fixed 0.05 cutoff (167,716 IDIs) lacks distribution awareness. The 25th percentile is therefore selected as the default based on its empirical advantage, not as a theoretically unique optimum.

A practical limitation of percentile-based thresholding arises for datasets with few features. For COMPAS (12 features), the 25th percentile marks only \(\sim \)3 features for aggressive mutation, which may restrict exploration capacity. This is consistent with the observation that FIFT’s performance advantage over AFT is narrower on COMPAS than on datasets with more features (e.g., Census with 13 or Meps with 40 features), where the 25th percentile provides a larger pool of aggressively mutated dimensions. Adopting a data-driven threshold selection mechanism, such as an elbow-based cutoff on the sorted importance distribution, could address this limitation and remains for future work.

SHAP serves as a higher-fidelity, theoretically grounded alternative. It marginally outperforms PFI in absolute IDI detection (198,610 vs. 198,542 instances on average with the 25th percentile threshold, a difference of less than 0.1%). This gain incurs a computational penalty: PFI achieves the highest EGS across all configurations, peaking at an average of 8,055 IDIs/sec compared to SHAP’s 5,997 IDIs/sec (a 34.4% efficiency advantage for PFI). Given that a primary design goal of FIFT is to overcome the overhead of per-instance local queries, PFI’s simplicity, scalability, and global nature justify its selection as the default estimator; SHAP remains a viable alternative when the computational budget permits. The surrogate classifier approach underperforms both SHAP and PFI across all thresholding strategies.

5.5.3 Mutation type

To isolate the effect of our proposed IHM operator, Table 13 compares four mutation operator strategies.

  • Random Mutation (Random): relies purely on unguided uniform reset perturbations without considering feature importance.

  • Direct Importance Mutation (DIM): scales perturbation magnitude directly (proportionally) with feature importance, producing larger mutations for more important features.

  • IIM: scales perturbation magnitude inversely with feature importance, producing smaller mutations for more important features.

As shown, DIM produces the lowest IDI detection rate at an average of 107,163, which is 28.8% below even the unguided Random baseline (150,592). This result validates our core hypothesis: aggressively perturbing high-importance features disrupts the structural patterns that drive discriminatory decision boundaries, actively degrading detection performance. Conversely, IIM, which conserves high-importance features by scaling perturbations inversely, improves IDI detection to 192,821, a 28.0% increase over Random. This confirms that feature importance guidance drives most of the detection gain. IHM extends IIM by partitioning the feature space at the importance threshold: features above the threshold receive conservative inverse-scaled perturbations (exploitation), while features below undergo full uniform resets (exploration). This dual-mode design produces the highest average of 198,610 #IDI, a 3.0% improvement over IIM, indicating that the threshold-based exploit-explore partition provides a further incremental gain in IDI detection over IIM. 5.6 Complexity analysis

Let d denote the number of features, N the population size, \(G_{max}\) the maximum number of generations, and V the number of protected-attribute values used to construct counterfactuals. Computing the global feature importance vector incurs a one-time cost of \(\mathcal {O}(C_{GFI})\). For our empirical default, PFI, this cost is \(\mathcal {O}(d \cdot n \cdot r)\), where n is the validation set size and r is the number of permutation repeats. If an alternative estimator such as SHAP is used, the cost depends on the chosen explainer and the size of its background subset. Within each generation, fitness evaluation requires \(\mathcal {O}(N \cdot V)\) classifier queries, tournament selection runs in \(\mathcal {O}(N \cdot k)\) for tournament size k, and crossover and mutation each operate in \(\mathcal {O}(N \cdot d)\). The overall complexity of the evolutionary loop is therefore \(\mathcal {O}(G_{max} \cdot N \cdot (V + d))\).

To provide empirical grounding for this analysis, we observe that for a representative dataset such as Census (\(d=13\), \(n \approx 9{,}000\)), the one-time \(C_{GFI}\) computation takes approximately 20 seconds. In comparison, the evolutionary search loop, executing for \(G_{max}=5{,}000\) generations with \(N=200\) to meet the \(10^6\) sample budget, completes in approximately 140 seconds at our average throughput of 7,000 IDIs/sec (Table 8). This indicates that the global feature importance computation, while adding a one-time overhead, represents only a small fraction (approximately 12.5%) of the total testing time under typical configurations. The per-generation computational overhead introduced by the IHM guidance mechanism is also negligible (\(<1\%\) of total query time), as the importance vector is cached and reused. The computational overhead of \(C_{GFI}\) can be further reduced in practice by precomputing and caching the global feature importance vector offline, allowing the same vector to be reused across repeated testing cycles on the same classifier.

5.7 Diversity of Discovered IDIs

FIFT’s low DGR (Section 5.2) provides indirect evidence of IDI diversity: a low duplicate rate implies that the generated test cases are distributed across a broader region of the input space rather than concentrated in a narrow cluster. Two architectural features of FIFT contribute to this coverage. First, the duplicate-penalisation mechanism assigns minimum fitness to previously observed candidates, explicitly redirecting the search toward unexplored subspaces. Second, FIFT’s aggressive reset mutation on low-importance features forces candidates to explore diverse combinations of non-influential attributes while preserving the high-importance feature patterns that correlate with discrimination. In contrast, methods with high DGR (e.g., GRFT at 46.96%, ExpGA at 55.76%) tend to generate many near-identical instances that cluster around a small number of discriminatory patterns, limiting the breadth of fairness insights.

5.8 Validating the feature importance–fairness link

A core premise of FIFT is that conservative perturbation of high-importance features keeps candidates near fairness-relevant decision boundaries, whereas aggressive perturbation of the same features displaces them. We examined this premise with two analyses: a controlled hit-rate experiment that varies perturbation magnitude across feature-importance intervals, and a distributional analysis of the discovered IDIs. The mutation-type ablation (Section 5.5.3) provides a third, independent line of evidence.

Hit rate by importance interval

For each dataset, classifier, and protected attribute, we partitioned the non-protected features into quartiles by normalised PFI score (Q1 lowest, Q4 highest). For each quartile, we drew 500 seed instances from the dataset and perturbed only the features in that quartile, under two modes mirroring the two IHM branches: a conservative importance-scaled step (Equation 5, \(\alpha = 0.1\)) and an aggressive uniform reset within the feature domain. Each perturbed candidate was checked for discrimination using the counterfactual protocol of Section 3.4, and the procedure was repeated five times with different random seeds. Figure 7 reports the resulting IDI hit rates.

Three observations follow. First, for high-importance features (Q4), perturbation magnitude matters: conservative perturbation achieves a mean hit rate of 0.140 (SD = 0.122) versus 0.124 (SD = 0.128) for aggressive resets, a difference that is statistically significant under a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test across all 220 configuration–repeat pairs (median paired difference = +0.026, \(W = 9700\), \(p = 0.009\), rank-biserial \(r = 0.20\)). The effect is strongest on LR (0.128 vs. 0.064, \(p < 10^{-5}\), \(r = 0.70\)). Second, for low-importance features (Q1), the mode makes no significant difference (0.135 vs. 0.133, \(p = 0.13\)), and both remain at the level of unperturbed seeds (0.133); aggressive resets on these dimensions inject diversity without displacing candidates from discriminatory regions. Third, under conservative perturbation, the hit rate increases with the importance of the perturbed quartile (Q4 = 0.140 vs. Q1 = 0.135, \(p < 0.001\), \(r = 0.38\)), although the gradient is small relative to between-configuration variance. RF departs from the aggregate pattern (reset on Q4 gives 0.146 vs. 0.123 conservative), which we attribute to the axis-aligned structure of tree ensembles; this heterogeneity is a case where importance-guided conservatism is not advantageous, indicating possibility of interaction-driven exceptions.

The null effect for low-importance features has a structural explanation. The normalised PFI mass is heavily concentrated: across the 20 dataset–classifier configurations, the top quartile of features holds 78.7% of total importance mass on average (range 55.5%–95.6%), while the bottom quartile holds 1.2%. Resets on explore-set features therefore perturb dimensions to which the classifier is nearly indifferent, injecting population diversity without displacing candidates relative to the decision function. The same concentration explains why hit rates are nearly flat across Q1–Q3, which jointly hold 21.3% of the mass, with only Q4 standing apart.

These single-step hit rates measure only the immediate effect of one mutation on dataset-drawn seeds; the cumulative benefit of the guidance emerges through selection over generations. The mutation-type ablation quantifies that cumulative effect directly: scaling perturbations proportionally to importance (DIM) degraded detection to 28.8% below the unguided random baseline, whereas inverse scaling (IIM) improved it by 28.0% (Table 13). These results are consistent with the premise that influential features lie near decision boundaries relevant to fairness violations and that preserving their values, rather than resetting them, retains proximity to those boundaries. They do not establish that classification importance implies fairness relevance in general; proxy variables and higher-order interactions can produce violations that this heuristic would rank lower, a limitation we discuss in Section 6.

Perturbation structure of discovered IDIs

As a complementary check on the operator’s behaviour, we analysed the perturbation frequency (variance) of features within the IDIs discovered by FIFT. Figure 8 compares this frequency between low-importance and high-importance features.

Low-importance features are perturbed more frequently (mean variance = 0.070) than high-importance features (mean variance = 0.045). This confirms that the IHM operator behaves as designed, aggressively mutating low-importance features to explore the input space while conservatively perturbing high-importance features; it is a check on the mechanism rather than independent evidence for the premise itself.

5.9 Naturalness assessment of generated IDIs

Standard IDI generation can sometimes produce unrealistic counterfactuals. To assess the naturalness of our generated samples, we computed the minimum \(L_1\) distance from each IDI to the nearest training instance. Figure 9 compares the average distances across all evaluated approaches.

The mean \(L_1\) distance for FIFT (4.76) is comparable to the search-based baseline ExpGA (4.60) and lower than the surrogate-based method AFT (5.28). Methods that apply small local perturbations stay closer to the training data (MAFT = 2.33, EIDIG = 2.37, GRFT = 2.82); however, the same methods recorded duplicate generation rates of 46.96%–67.36% (Section 5.2) and detected 2.2–6.9 times fewer IDIs than FIFT (Section 5.1). The distance results therefore reflect a trade-off between input-space coverage and proximity to the data manifold rather than a uniform advantage for any method. Feasibility constraints on counterfactual generation, such as dependency-preserving perturbations, were not evaluated and remain for future work.

6 Threats to validity

This section discusses the potential threats to the validity of our study, categorised into internal, external, and construct validity.

Internal validity

Internal validity concerns factors that may affect the causal relationship between the experimental treatment and observed outcomes. One potential threat is the randomness inherent in genetic algorithms and the feature importance computation. To mitigate this, we repeated each experiment 10 independent times and reported average results. We also used fixed random seeds where applicable to ensure reproducibility.

Another concern relates to the implementation correctness of baseline methods. To address this, we adopted implementations directly from the official repositories of the baseline methods, using their recommended hyperparameter configurations. This reduces implementation bias.

The sensitivity of the IHM operator to the feature importance normalisation scheme deserves consideration. In the current implementation, feature importance scores are normalised to sum to one. For datasets with many features, individual normalised scores tend to be small, which compresses the range of the inverse scaling factor \((1 - I_j)\) and reduces the differentiation in mutation magnitude across features within the exploit mode. As a result, the primary source of IHM’s advantage over uniform mutation is the threshold-based partition into exploit and explore modes (conservative additive perturbation for high-importance features versus full uniform reset for low-importance features), rather than the fine-grained inverse scaling within the exploit branch. Alternative normalisation strategies, such as min-max scaling to [0, 1], could amplify the per-feature differentiation and constitute a possible direction for future investigation.

External validity

External validity relates to the generalisability of our findings. Our evaluation was conducted on five benchmark datasets commonly used in fairness testing literature. While these datasets represent diverse domains (banking, credit, census, criminal justice, and healthcare), they may not capture all possible real-world scenarios. In particular, the current evaluation is limited to tabular data; while FIFT’s model-agnostic design can theoretically support complex modalities like text or images given an appropriate global importance estimator (e.g., token-level SHAP), this remains to be empirically validated. Future work could extend the evaluation to additional datasets and application domains.

Similarly, we evaluated FIFT on four ML classifiers (Decision Tree, Random Forest, Logistic Regression, and Multi-Layer Perceptron). Although these classifiers are widely used in practice, the findings may not generalise to all classifier types, particularly complex deep learning architectures. However, the classifier-agnostic nature of FIFT suggests it should be applicable to other classifier types.

Construct validity

Construct validity concerns whether the metrics used adequately capture the concepts being measured. We employed multiple metrics including the #IDI, GSR, DGR, and EGS to evaluate effectiveness, redundancy, and efficiency of the framework. These metrics were well-established in the fairness testing literature and capture different dimensions of framework performance (Fan et al. 2022; Quan et al. 2025; Udeshi et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2021a, b; Wang et al. 2024).

The choice of PFI as the default feature importance estimator may also affect construct validity. While our ablation study shows that PFI offers the best balance of effectiveness and computational efficiency, the relative performance may vary depending on classifier architecture and data characteristics. PFI can be sensitive to highly correlated features. Table 14 reports pairwise Spearman correlations for the five benchmarks: mean absolute correlations are below 0.1 on four of the five datasets, while MEPS shows moderate correlation (mean \(|r| = 0.237\)) and Bank contains one perfectly correlated feature pair (max \(|r| = 1.000\)). Replacing PFI with SHAP, which accounts for feature interactions, produced a comparable overall IDI detection rate (198,610 vs. 198,542 on average; Section 5.5.1), suggesting that this sensitivity did not impair FIFT’s search guidance on the evaluated datasets. Datasets with stronger multicollinearity than these benchmarks may require an interaction-aware estimator such as SHAP.

The standard IDI assumption, that modifying only the protected attribute produces a realistic counterfactual, is a validity threat. This limitation applies to all marginal-perturbation methods. Section 5.9 quantifies this concern: FIFT’s generated IDIs lie at distances from the training data comparable to the other search-based method ExpGA (mean \(L_1\) of 4.76 vs. 4.60), although methods that perturb more conservatively (MAFT, EIDIG, GRFT) remain closer to the data manifold (2.33–2.82). Constraint-aware counterfactual generation that models dependencies between protected and non-protected attributes remains for future work.

7 Related work

7.1 Individual fairness testing

Individual fairness testing has become an important research direction within software fairness analysis, owing to its ability to draw out instance-level evidence of discriminatory behaviour that group-level metrics frequently obscure (Chen et al. 2024). Galhotra et al. (2017) introduced Themis, the first automated IFT framework, which operationalises individual fairness through random input generation and counterfactual comparison. AEQUITAS (Udeshi et al. 2018) extended this work by combining random global exploration with local perturbation search to discover IDIs in the neighbourhoods of known discriminatory instances. However, both Themis and AEQUITAS apply perturbations uniformly across all non-protected features, without prioritising those most likely to expose discriminatory behaviour.

Subsequent methods introduced more sophisticated guidance mechanisms. ExpGA (Fan et al. 2022) combines LIME-based local explanations with a genetic algorithm, using explanation results to identify high-quality seeds before applying standard genetic operators to generate IDIs at scale. While ExpGA demonstrates that explanation-guided initialisation improves seed quality, the feature importance insights it derives are not embedded into the search mechanics beyond the seeding phase, leaving the mutation and crossover operators unguided. GRFT (Quan et al. 2025) operates in two distinct phases. In the global phase, a genetic algorithm explores the input space to identify a diverse population of discriminatory seeds. In the local phase, a lightweight random search perturbs the non-protected features of each discovered seed by unit steps, discovering additional IDIs in their immediate neighbourhoods without incurring the overhead of fitness-guided evolution. This two-phase design improves efficiency on simpler classifier architectures. However, GRFT applies perturbations uniformly across all non-protected features in both phases, without regard to the varying influence that individual features exert on classifier predictions. As a result, the search does not preferentially target the feature dimensions most likely to expose discriminatory behaviour, which limits its effectiveness on more complex classifiers where the discriminatory decision boundaries are shaped predominantly by a small subset of highly influential features.

Gradient-based methods such as ADF (Zhang et al. 2021a) and EIDIG (Zhang et al. 2021b) exploit first-order gradient signals from deep neural networks to direct the search toward discriminatory decision boundaries, with EIDIG reusing prior gradient information across iterations to reduce computational overhead. MAFT (Wang et al. 2024) extends this paradigm to black-box settings by substituting true backpropagation gradients with zero-order gradient estimation, enabling gradient-guided search without access to internal model representations. AFT (Zhao et al. 2024) takes a verification-based approach, training a decision tree surrogate to partition the input space into deterministic subspaces and confining test generation within each partition to systematically expose discrimination. While each of these methods addresses specific aspects of the IFT problem, they share a common limitation: guidance signals are either local to individual instances, computationally prohibitive to recompute at scale, or confined to the structure of a surrogate model rather than the global discriminatory properties of the classifier under test.

A parallel line of research has focused on improving the quality and diversity of generated IDIs rather than the search guidance mechanism itself. Tian et al. (2026) proposed I&D, a seed selection approach that generates diverse initial IDIs using a chiral model trained on protected-attribute-mutated data, combined with SHAP-based clustering to select structurally diverse seeds prior to search. Xiao et al. (2023) introduced LIMI, which generates more natural discriminatory instances by imitating the decision boundary of the target model in the semantic latent space of a generative adversarial network, addressing the concern that IDIs deviating from the real data distribution may not reflect genuine fairness violations in practice. Mamman et al. (2024) further showed that counterfactual analysis of non-seed instances can improve detection volume and diversity. Diversity-aware approaches such as Vbt-X (Zhao et al. 2024) employ hashing-based sampling to draw test cases approximately uniformly from the solution space, prioritising the structural diversity of discovered instances over raw detection count. More recently, Kitamura et al. (2024) introduced the notion of individual fairness testing with data validity, prompting a reassessment of how detection effectiveness should be measured.

Beyond offline testing, recent work has explored the integration of fairness monitoring into active ML operations. Mamman et al. (2024a) proposed BiasTrap, a runtime verification framework that uses data augmentation and bias detection to continuously monitor model behaviour for discriminatory patterns. To facilitate corrective interventions, Mamman et al. (2024b) further introduced an explanation-guided oversight mechanism that tracks predictions on-the-fly and presents counterfactual alternatives to human reviewers, enabling responsible real-time control of deployed models.

7.2 Feature importance and fairness

The connection between feature importance and fairness has been explored in several diagnostic contexts. Ghosh et al. (2023) used global sensitivity analysis to compute fairness influence functions, quantifying feature contributions to fairness violations. Similarly, Openja et al. (2023) emphasised the need for systematic feature analysis in fairness evaluation. Recent work has also begun to explore multi-objective fairness testing. Majumder et al. (2023) investigated the relationship between individual and group fairness metrics, searching for measures that can sufficiently capture broader ethical concerns. While some frameworks aim to jointly optimise individual and group fairness outcomes, FIFT focuses exclusively on individual fairness, as such violations typically provide the most detailed and actionable evidence of model bias (Section 5.4).

Beyond the direct application of feature importance to fairness diagnostics, a parallel line of research has explored its use as a guidance signal in metaheuristic search more broadly. Evolutionary algorithms have been widely applied to software testing problems, and recent works have explored using feature importance to inform metaheuristic search (Katoch et al. 2021). For example, Herdianto et al. (2025) used importance values to derive guidance rules for Tabu Search in routing problems, while Kwakye (2024) incorporated feature importance feedback into an ensemble of metaheuristic algorithms via a Deep Q-Learning module. Similarly, Salem et al. (2025) demonstrated that data-driven initialisation and guided searches can accelerate convergence in feature selection tasks.

While the use of feature importance as a heuristic search signal is established in these broader optimisation contexts, its application to IFT presents distinct algorithmic requirements. Unlike standard adversarial example generation, which leverages feature importance to push an instance across a decision boundary by manipulating any combination of features, IFT requires discovering instances that cross the decision boundary only when the protected attribute is flipped. To address this, FIFT introduces a dual-mode mutation mechanism, the IHM operator, which distinguishes it from prior feature-guided metaheuristics. Instead of using importance scores merely for feature selection or generalised guidance probabilities, IHM partitions the perturbation landscape: it scales mutation magnitudes inversely to feature importance for highly influential features to exploit regions near discriminatory boundaries, while applying aggressive random resets to low-importance features. This dual-mode design preserves the structural characteristics of discriminatory instances while injecting the diversity needed to escape redundant local optima, adapting the principle of importance-guided search to the specific constraints of algorithmic fairness.

8 Conclusion

This paper proposes FIFT, an evolutionary individual fairness testing framework that integrates global feature importance directly into the mutation operator of a genetic algorithm. FIFT addresses three limitations of existing search-based IFT methods: uniform random feature perturbation, repeated per-instance local explanation computation, and test redundancy. FIFT uses an Importance-Guided Hybrid Mutation operator that applies perturbations scaled inversely to importance for high-importance features while randomly mutating low-importance features, thus balancing exploitation of discriminatory decision boundaries with broad input-space exploration. A complementary duplicate-penalisation mechanism further steers the evolutionary search toward novel regions. Experiments across five benchmark datasets and four ML classifiers show that FIFT detects 20.8%–190.4% more IDIs than the strongest baseline and reduces test redundancy by 84.0%–98.0% relative to the search-based baselines.

We identify two directions for future work. First, we plan to extend FIFT to address fairness testing of ML software in unstructured data domains, such as natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision (CV), where high-dimensional inputs pose additional challenges for test generation. Such an extension would require replacing the core feature-importance computation and reconsidering the mutation operator design to handle token or pixel-level semantics. Second, we plan to investigate importance-guided strategies that dynamically adjust perturbation pressure based on importance feedback during the search.
