Abstract

Network intrusion detection systems deployed in dynamic environments are vulnerable to concept drift, where changes in traffic distributions can degrade detection performance over time. This paper proposes ADAWU-IDS, an interpretable and validation-calibrated drift-response framework for concept drift-aware network intrusion detection. The framework combines Multi-Scale Drift Index (MSDI)-based drift-severity estimation, Adaptive Drift-Aware Weight Updating (ADAWU), and validation-selected response control within a chronological evaluation pipeline. Unlike fixed-response adaptive designs, ADAWU-IDS does not assume that stronger intervention is always beneficial. Candidate response configurations are calibrated on a held-out validation stream and then fixed before final chronological testing. MSDI is used as an auxiliary drift-severity signal rather than as a standalone binary drift detector, while ADAWU adjusts ensemble contributions according to recent predictive behavior, drift severity, and temporal relevance. Under a chronological CICIDS2017 protocol, the validation-selected configuration, denoted as ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated, shows modest improvements over Static LSTM in Weighted F1 and Attack F1. However, these improvements are not statistically significant at the p< 0.05 level, and the calibrated framework does not consistently improve Attack Recall or reduce the false-negative rate. Classical online ensemble baselines, including Dynamic Weighted Majority, Online Bagging, and Leveraging Bagging, achieve stronger raw predictive performance under the same protocol. The ablation results show that mandatory hierarchical retraining can over-adapt to short-term chunk behavior and harm attack detection. Therefore, hierarchical response is reformulated as an optional response action selected through validation calibration. Overall, ADAWU-IDS provides a transparent and configurable framework for analyzing drift-response behavior in intrusion detection, with practical value in drift-severity interpretation, response calibration, and component-level diagnosis. The evaluation reports paired statistical tests, ablation analysis, segment-wise drift-window results, computational cost, and drift-detection diagnostics. The implementation is available at: https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ADAWU-IDS-800B.

1 Introduction

The exponential growth of network connectivity and digital services has made network security a critical requirement for modern information infrastructures. Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) are widely used as a frontline defense mechanism for identifying malicious activities from complex network traffic patterns (Chinnasamy et al. 2025; Anis et al. 2025; Hozouri et al. 2025). Traditional NIDS approaches, including signature-based, anomaly-based, and machine-learning-based methods, often rely on a static learning paradigm in which a model is trained on historical data and then deployed without sufficient adaptation (Shyaa et al. 2024; Seth et al. 2024). However, real network environments are inherently non-stationary. Evolving attack strategies, changes in network services, shifting user behavior, and variations in traffic composition can continuously alter the underlying data distribution, leading to concept drift (Shyaa et al. 2024; Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2024).

Concept drift is particularly challenging for intrusion detection because predictive degradation may directly increase missed attacks or false alarms. A detector trained on historical traffic may perform well under similar conditions but fail when the distribution of benign or malicious traffic changes over time (Seth et al. 2024; Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Cai et al. 2025). This issue is not limited to NIDS. Recent anomaly detection and condition-monitoring studies have also shown that distribution shift, domain shift, limited labeled data, and complex deployment environments can substantially affect model reliability in practical monitoring systems (Yan et al. 2025a, b, 2024). These studies suggest that robust anomaly detection requires not only accurate classifiers, but also explicit mechanisms for monitoring distributional changes, adapting model behavior, and reporting failure cases under changing operating conditions.

Existing drift-aware learning methods can be broadly categorized into passive adaptation, active drift detection, and adaptive ensemble learning (Swarnalatha et al. 2025; Thockchom et al. 2023). Passive methods continuously update models as new data arrive, but they may overfit temporary fluctuations or noisy labels. Active methods monitor performance or distributional indicators and trigger adaptation when a change is detected, but their effectiveness often depends on threshold choices and the detectability of the drift event (Zhang et al. 2026; Abdulrahaman et al. 2026). Adaptive ensembles, such as Dynamic Weighted Majority, Online Bagging, and related online learning methods, adjust the influence of base learners over time and have shown strong performance under non-stationary data streams (Swarnalatha et al. 2025; Thockchom et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2024). Nevertheless, many adaptive IDS studies still face three limitations.

First, drift is often characterized using a single indicator, such as prediction error, window-level statistics, or a one-dimensional distributional distance. Such indicators may be insufficient for describing heterogeneous changes in network traffic, where feature distributions, class-conditional structures, and attack composition may evolve differently (Cai et al. 2025; Chu et al. 2023). Second, ensemble adaptation is frequently driven mainly by recent predictive performance, while the severity of distributional change and temporal relevance of models are not always explicitly represented (Cai et al. 2025; Yu et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2025). Third, many adaptive systems use fixed response rules once drift is detected. However, unconditional or overly aggressive adaptation can be harmful when short-term chunk labels are noisy, imbalanced, or not representative of future traffic. Therefore, response control should be calibrated rather than assumed to be universally beneficial.

To address these issues, this paper proposes ADAWU-IDS, an interpretable and validation-calibrated drift-response framework for concept drift-aware network intrusion detection. The motivation of ADAWU-IDS is not to replace strong accuracy-oriented online ensemble classifiers such as Dynamic Weighted Majority, Online Bagging, or Leveraging Bagging. These methods are highly effective when the primary objective is raw predictive performance under non-stationary streams. However, in practical intrusion detection deployment, prediction accuracy alone is often insufficient. Security operators may also need to understand whether a performance change is associated with distributional drift, how severe the drift is, whether an adaptive response should be triggered, and whether stronger intervention may actually harm later detection due to noisy labels, short-term imbalance, or non-representative attack patterns.

From this perspective, ADAWU-IDS is designed to address a complementary deployment need: transparent and configurable drift-response analysis. The framework combines a Multi-Scale Drift Index (MSDI), an Adaptive Drift-Aware Weight Update mechanism (ADAWU), and a validation-selected response policy within a chronological IDS evaluation pipeline. MSDI summarizes feature-group and class-conditional distributional changes as an interpretable drift-severity signal. ADAWU adjusts ensemble contributions by jointly considering recent predictive behavior, drift-severity information, and temporal relevance. The response policy is not treated as a fixed or mandatory intervention; instead, candidate configurations are calibrated on a held-out chronological validation stream before final testing. This design allows the framework to examine when adaptive components are useful, when they are harmful, and how response actions should be selected before deployment.

The practical value of ADAWU-IDS therefore lies in its diagnostic and calibration-oriented role rather than in claiming universal predictive superiority. Strong online ensemble baselines may achieve better attack-detection metrics in some settings, especially when the objective is minimizing false negatives or maximizing aggregate F1 scores. ADAWU-IDS, by contrast, provides additional information about drift severity, model temporal relevance, component interaction, and response-policy sensitivity. Such information is useful in deployment scenarios where interpretability, auditability, response control, and failure-case analysis are important alongside predictive performance.

The methodological novelty of ADAWU-IDS lies in the explicit coupling of drift-severity estimation, adaptive weight control, and validation-selected response policy in a temporally ordered IDS pipeline. First, unlike methods that rely mainly on prediction error or a binary drift alarm, MSDI provides a severity-oriented view of distributional deviation by integrating feature-level and class-conditional information. Second, unlike conventional online ensemble methods that primarily update learners according to recent classification performance, ADAWU incorporates drift severity and temporal relevance into the ensemble weighting process. Third, unlike fixed-response adaptive IDS designs, ADAWU-IDS does not assume that stronger adaptation is always beneficial. Instead, it evaluates candidate response actions through validation calibration and then fixes the selected configuration before final chronological testing.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

  • We propose MSDI as an interpretable drift-severity signal for chronological IDS streams. Rather than treating drift detection as a standalone binary decision, MSDI summarizes temporal distributional deviation from complementary feature-group and class-conditional perspectives.

  • We introduce ADAWU, a drift-aware ensemble weighting mechanism that updates learner contributions by combining recent predictive behavior, MSDI-based drift severity, and temporal relevance.

  • We reformulate hierarchical response as a validation-selected response policy rather than a mandatory intervention. Candidate configurations, including weighting parameters, drift thresholds, and optional response actions, are calibrated on a held-out chronological validation stream before final testing.

  • We provide a conservative chronological evaluation that reports repeated runs, paired statistical tests, ablation analysis, segment-wise drift-window results, drift-detection diagnostics, computational cost, and failure-case discussion. This evaluation clarifies both the potential and the limitations of ADAWU-IDS relative to Static LSTM and strong online ensemble baselines.

Overall, ADAWU-IDS should be interpreted as a transparent, configurable, and calibration-aware framework for studying drift-response behavior in intrusion detection systems. It is not intended to be a universally superior IDS classifier. Instead, it contributes a structured way to connect drift-severity signals, adaptive weighting, and response-policy selection under chronological evaluation, thereby supporting more interpretable and cautious deployment of adaptive IDS systems under concept drift.

The experimental results show that the validation-calibrated ADAWU-IDS configuration provides modest improvements over Static LSTM in Weighted F1 and Attack F1, but the improvements are not statistically significant at the \(p < 0.05\) level. The calibrated framework does not consistently reduce false negatives, and classical online ensemble baselines achieve stronger raw predictive performance. These findings motivate a calibration-oriented interpretation of ADAWU-IDS: the framework is most useful for analyzing how drift-severity information, adaptive weighting, and response-policy selection interact under chronological IDS evaluation. These results suggest that ADAWU-IDS is most useful in deployment settings where drift interpretation, response calibration, and component-level diagnosis are required in addition to raw predictive accuracy.

Sections 4 and 5 describe the system implementation and experimental design, respectively. Section 6 analyzes the evaluation results, followed by a discussion in Section 7. Finally, Section 8 concludes the paper.

2 Related work

This section reviews related studies from four perspectives: static network intrusion detection, concept drift detection and adaptation, adaptive ensemble learning for non-stationary streams, and recent distribution-shift-aware anomaly detection studies. The purpose is to clarify the methodological context of ADAWU-IDS and to distinguish the proposed framework from prior adaptive weighting and drift-response approaches.

2.1 Static network intrusion detection

Conventional NIDS predominantly rely on signature-based detection, employing pattern matching against established attack profiles. Although highly effective for identifying documented threats, these systems are inherently vulnerable to zero-day exploits and rapidly evolving attack variants. To mitigate this vulnerability, machine learning approaches have been widely adopted to model normal and malicious traffic patterns from historical data. Traditional algorithms, such as Support Vector Machines, decision trees, and random forests, have demonstrated considerable efficacy in NIDS applications (Chinnasamy et al. 2025; Hozouri et al. 2025). Furthermore, deep learning architectures, notably Long Short-Term Memory networks and Convolutional Neural Networks, have shown strong capability in extracting complex temporal and spatial dependencies within network traffic (Hore et al. 2024; Bamber et al. 2025; Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997).

Despite these advancements, the majority of existing methods are constrained by a “train-once-deploy” paradigm predicated on the assumption of stationary data distributions (Shyaa et al. 2024; Seth et al. 2024; Cai et al. 2025). In real-world network environments, this assumption is frequently violated, inevitably leading to severe model degradation as underlying data concepts drift over time (Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2024; Jemili et al. 2024).

2.2 Concept drift detection and adaptation

Concept drift refers to changes in the underlying data distribution over time, which may degrade the predictive performance of models trained on historical data (Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Abdulrahaman et al. 2026; Cai et al. 2024).

Passive adaptation methods continuously update the learning model without explicit drift detection mechanisms, typically employing sliding windows or instance weighting techniques (Jemili et al. 2024). While computationally efficient, these approaches are prone to overfitting temporary data fluctuations and noise during stable periods.

Active detection methods explicitly monitor performance or data distributions to trigger model updates only when a change is confirmed (Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Mahdi et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2025). Representative algorithms, such as the Drift Detection Method (DDM), Early Drift Detection Method (EDDM), and Adaptive Windowing (ADWIN), attempt to balance stability and adaptability (Gama et al. 2004; Baena-Garcıa et al. 2006; Bifet and Gavalda 2007). However, they frequently struggle with significant detection delays and extreme sensitivity to predefined thresholds (Cai et al. 2024).

Ensemble learning approaches maintain a dynamic combination of multiple base classifiers, adjusting their influence through weight updates (Swarnalatha et al. 2025; Thockchom et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2024). Algorithms like Dynamic Weighted Majority (DWM) and Online Bagging have demonstrated robust performance in non-stationary environments. Nevertheless, a major limitation of current ensemble techniques is their reliance on oversimplified weight update mechanisms—often based exclusively on recent predictive accuracy—thereby neglecting critical factors such as specific drift characteristics and historical model staleness (Cai et al. 2025; Guerrero Cano et al. 2026; Chen et al. 2025).

More recent advances have explored model-internal drift detection via parameter monitoring and probabilistic frameworks for distinguishing real drift from virtual drift (Mahdi et al. 2024). While theoretically promising, these methods currently lack seamless integration with comprehensive drift quantification metrics and adaptive ensemble structures (Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Cai et al. 2025; Guerrero Cano et al. 2026).

2.3 Adaptive ensemble learning under non-stationary data

Adaptive ensemble learning is a major direction for handling non-stationary data streams. Instead of relying on a single static classifier, ensemble methods maintain multiple base learners and adjust their contribution over time. Dynamic Weighted Majority (DWM) reduces the influence of poorly performing classifiers and can add or remove learners when the stream changes. Online Bagging approximates bootstrap aggregation in an online setting, while Leveraging Bagging strengthens diversity through resampling and drift-aware updating. These methods are widely used because they provide strong predictive performance and efficient online adaptation under many non-stationary settings.

However, classical adaptive ensembles usually update model weights mainly according to recent prediction errors or online classification performance. This design is effective for accuracy-oriented adaptation, but it may provide limited interpretability regarding why a specific response is triggered and how drift severity affects model behavior. In IDS applications, such interpretability is important because operational decisions often need to distinguish between mild traffic fluctuation, severe attack-distribution shift, and temporary class imbalance.

Another challenge is response control. Stronger interventions, such as retraining or aggressive model replacement, are not always beneficial. If the current chunk contains noisy labels, short-term imbalance, or a non-representative attack pattern, unconditional adaptation may overfit the current chunk and harm later detection. Therefore, adaptive IDS frameworks should not assume that all response components are always beneficial. Instead, response actions should be calibrated on validation data and evaluated through ablation analysis, statistical testing, and failure-case discussion.

2.4 Drift-aware and dynamic learning frameworks for NIDS

In the specific context of NIDS, streaming learning algorithms have been deployed to process sequential data without retaining extensive historical logs (Jin et al. 2024; Singh et al. 2024). For instance, Hoeffding Trees and their variants provide efficient incremental learning capabilities but generally exhibit poor autonomous drift identification. To overcome this, hybrid systems that couple explicit drift detection with incremental updates have emerged (Cai et al. 2025; Jemili et al. 2024; Chu et al. 2023). Yet, these systems predominantly rely on uniform, “one-size-fits-all” response strategies, executing identical adaptation procedures regardless of the actual severity or type of the detected drift (Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2026; Guerrero Cano et al. 2026).

The proposed ADAWU-IDS framework is designed to address these limitations from a calibration-oriented perspective. MSDI summarizes drift severity using feature-level and class-conditional information. ADAWU updates ensemble weights using recent predictive behavior, drift-severity information, and temporal relevance. More importantly, the response policy is not treated as an always-active hierarchical mechanism. Instead, candidate response configurations are selected on a held-out validation stream and then fixed before final chronological testing. This design distinguishes ADAWU-IDS from methods that assume a fixed response action for all detected drift events (Thein et al. 2024; Nassreddine et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2024).

2.5 Recent distribution-shift-aware anomaly detection studies

Recent studies outside network intrusion detection also highlight the importance of robustness under complex deployment environments, limited labeled data, and domain shift. In railway arc detection, multimodal imitation learning has been used to combine visible-light and infrared features, transferable feature learning, and optimal-transport-based representation alignment to improve robustness under glare, reflection, weather interference, and small-target variation (Yan et al. 2025a0. Other multimodal learning studies have introduced distribution constraints and knowledge distillation to improve robustness and computational efficiency under uncertain degradation and long-tail data distributions (Yan et al. 2024).

Limited-data anomaly detection has also attracted increasing attention. A DDPM-based multimodal arc detection framework uses self-supervised pretraining on unlabeled data and audiovisual semantic cues to improve detection under sparse labeled samples (Yan et al. 2025b). However, the same study also shows that practical deployment may be affected by high computational cost, multimodal hardware dependency, and limited interpretability. These concerns are relevant to adaptive IDS deployment, where adaptation cost, data availability, and explanation of failure cases are critical.

Domain-shift-aware unsupervised anomaly detection provides another relevant perspective. Transformer and graph convolution-based anomalous sound detection has been studied under domain shifts, where the model must distinguish normal and anomalous distributions despite changes in operating conditions (Yan et al. 2024). This line of work reinforces the need for adaptive feature alignment, domain-aware modeling, and explicit evaluation under shifted conditions.

Although these studies are not designed for NIDS, they reflect a broader trend in anomaly detection: robustness should be evaluated not only by aggregate predictive accuracy, but also by adaptation behavior, calibration strategy, computational overhead, and failure modes under distribution shift. This motivates the positioning of ADAWU-IDS as an interpretable and validation-calibrated drift-response framework rather than a universally superior classifier.

2.6 Research gap and positioning of ADAWU-IDS

The above review suggests that existing adaptive and drift-aware methods have made important progress, but several gaps remain for concept drift-aware intrusion detection. First, drift signals are often treated as binary triggers rather than severity-oriented diagnostic information. Second, ensemble weight updates are frequently driven mainly by recent predictive performance and do not explicitly combine drift severity and temporal relevance. Third, response actions such as retraining are often predefined, although their effectiveness may depend strongly on the dataset, attack distribution, and validation behavior. Fourth, many studies report aggregate performance without sufficient statistical validation, component-interaction analysis, false-negative discussion, or computational trade-off analysis.

ADAWU-IDS is proposed to address these gaps in a transparent and testable manner. Its novelty does not lie in claiming universal predictive superiority over all adaptive ensembles. Instead, the contribution lies in coupling MSDI-based drift-severity estimation, ADAWU-based dynamic weight control, and validation-selected response policy within a chronological IDS evaluation pipeline. This formulation allows the framework to expose when adaptive components are useful, when they are harmful, and how response actions should be calibrated before final testing.

3 Methodology

In this section, we present the proposed ADAWU-IDS framework. We first formulate intrusion detection under non-stationary traffic as a temporally ordered classification problem. We then describe the LSTM-based detection backbone and introduce the main methodological components of the framework: the Multi-Scale Drift Index (MSDI), the Adaptive Drift-Aware Weight Update (ADAWU) mechanism, and a validation-selected response policy. Finally, we summarize how these components are integrated into a unified dynamic ensemble learning process.

3.1 Problem formulation

Network intrusion detection in real-world environments is inherently non-stationary (Seth et al. 2024; Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025). As network services, user behavior, and attack strategies evolve over time, the joint distribution of traffic features and labels may change, causing predictive models trained on historical data to degrade after deployment (Luo et al. 2024; Cai et al. 2025; Chu et al. 2023). This problem is particularly important for intrusion detection systems, where delayed adaptation can lead to a substantial loss of detection capability under newly emerging traffic conditions (Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Haddane et al. 2026).

In this work, we consider a temporally ordered intrusion detection setting in which network traffic arrives as a sequence of consecutive data chunks, \(D_1, D_2, \dots , D_T\), where each chunk corresponds to a consecutive observation period in the evolving traffic stream. Let \(\mathcal {X} \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{L \times d}\) denote the input feature space, where L is the sequence length and d is the feature dimension. In the current implementation, L=10 and d=78. Let \(\mathcal {Y} = \{0, 1\}\) denote the binary label space, where 0 represents benign traffic and 1 represents malicious traffic. For each temporal chunk \(D_t\), samples are drawn from a time-dependent joint distribution \(P_t(X, Y)\), which may vary over time due to concept drift.

The objective of this study is to design an adaptive intrusion detection framework that can be analyzed under temporally evolving traffic conditions. Specifically, given an initial training stage and an incoming sequence of temporal chunks, the framework should: (1) provide an interpretable signal for characterizing distributional changes, (2) adjust the contribution of ensemble members according to recent predictive behavior and drift-severity information, and (3) evaluate whether validation-calibrated adaptation can mitigate performance degradation compared with static deployment without assuming universal superiority over all adaptive baselines.

To achieve this objective, we propose ADAWU-IDS, a drift-aware adaptive ensemble framework that combines explicit drift-severity estimation, dynamic weight adjustment, and validation-selected response control. The framework is intended to support transparent drift-response behavior under evolving traffic distributions rather than to replace all existing online ensemble methods in raw predictive performance.

3.2 Base LSTM detection model

As the predictive backbone of the proposed framework, we adopt Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks to model temporal dependencies in network traffic sequences (Hore et al. 2024; Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997; Devendiran and Turukmane 2024). LSTM is suitable for this task because intrusion patterns often evolve across consecutive packets or flow observations rather than appearing as isolated feature values (Bamber et al. 2025; Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997). Compared with purely static classifiers, recurrent architectures are better suited to capturing short-term temporal structure in sequential traffic data.

For each input sample, a sequence of length L with feature dimension d is fed into the LSTM model. The hidden representation is updated recurrently over time through the standard gating mechanism, including the forget gate, input gate, output gate, cell state, and hidden state. The final hidden representation is then passed to a classification head to produce the predicted traffic label.

In the current implementation, each base learner uses a two-layer LSTM architecture followed by normalization, regularization, and a final fully connected classifier. Concretely, the model consists of a first LSTM layer with 64 hidden units and sequence output, a second LSTM layer with 32 hidden units and final-state output, a dense layer with 64 neurons and ReLU activation, a dropout layer with dropout rate 0.2, batch normalization layers for training stabilization, and a two-neuron softmax output layer. Model training uses the Adam optimizer with sparse categorical cross-entropy loss.

This design serves two purposes. First, it provides a temporally aware detection backbone suitable for sequence-based intrusion analysis. Second, it enables adaptive ensemble control, where multiple LSTM-based learners trained under different initializations can contribute unequally as the traffic distribution evolves (Swarnalatha et al. 2025; Thockchom et al. 2023; Nassreddine et al. 2025).

3.3 Multi-scale drift index (MSDI)

A key challenge in adaptive intrusion detection is that distributional change may appear in multiple forms (Mahdi et al. 2024). Drift may affect low-level feature distributions, class-conditional structure, or the overall relationship between benign and malicious traffic. Relying on a single indicator may therefore provide an incomplete view of temporal change. To address this issue, we introduce the Multi-Scale Drift Index (MSDI), which quantifies distributional change from complementary perspectives and provides a structured signal for adaptation (Hu et al. 2025).

It is important to clarify that MSDI is not treated as a complete standalone drift detector. Instead, it is used as a drift-severity signal that supports adaptive weighting and response-policy calibration. Its diagnostic value is assessed empirically in the drift-detection analysis rather than assumed a priori.

In practical deployment, MSDI should be interpreted as an auxiliary severity indicator rather than as an autonomous alarm module. A high MSDI value indicates that the current traffic chunk differs from the reference state in feature-level or class-conditional structure, but it does not by itself prove that the predictive decision boundary has changed. Therefore, MSDI is used to modulate ensemble weights and to support response-policy calibration, while operational decisions should also consider predictive performance, false-negative behavior, computational constraints, and analyst-defined risk tolerance.

3.3.1 Feature-group drift

Let the feature set be partitioned into G semantically meaningful groups. In the current implementation, these groups correspond to flow statistics, packet-size descriptors, directional packet features, TCP flag features, and temporal behavior features. For the g-th feature group, we compare its empirical distribution in the current chunk \(D_t\) with that in a reference chunk \(R_t\), which represents the recent stable state (Bifet and Gavalda 2007).

For each feature f in group g, we compute a normalized one-dimensional Wasserstein distance:

$$\begin{aligned} \delta _t^{(g,f)} = \min \left( 1, \frac{W_1\left( P_t^{(g,f)}, Q_t^{(g,f)}\right) }{\sigma _R^{(g,f)} + \varepsilon }\right) \end{aligned}$$
(1)

where \(P_t^{(g,f)}\) and \(Q_t^{(g,f)}\) denote the empirical distributions of feature f in the current chunk and the reference chunk, respectively, \(W_1(\cdot , \cdot )\) is the one-dimensional Wasserstein distance (Cai et al. 2025), \(\sigma _R^{(g,f)}\) is the standard deviation of the reference feature distribution, and \(\varepsilon \) is a small positive constant for numerical stability.

The group-level score is then defined as

$$\begin{aligned} S_t^{(g)} = \frac{1}{|G_g|} \sum _{f \in G_g} \delta _t^{(g,f)} \end{aligned}$$
(2)

where \(G_g\) denotes the set of features in group g. The feature-group drift component is obtained by averaging across groups:

$$\begin{aligned} S_t^{\text {feat}} = \frac{1}{G} \sum _{g=1}^{G} S_t^{(g)} \end{aligned}$$
(3)

This component captures drift in the input space at the grouped feature level while preserving interpretability across different functional subsets of network traffic descriptors.

3.3.2 Class-conditional drift

Feature-level change alone does not fully characterize concept drift, because the relationship between features and labels may also evolve. To capture this aspect, we compute a compact class-conditional drift summary. For each class \(c \in \mathcal {Y}\), let \(\mu _{t,c}\) and \(\mu _{R,c}\) denote the class-wise mean feature vectors estimated from the current chunk and the reference chunk, respectively. The class-conditional drift for class c is defined as

$$\begin{aligned} S_{t,c}^{\text {class}} = \min \left( 1, \frac{\left\| \mu _{t,c} - \mu _{R,c}\right\| _2}{\left\| \mu _{R,c}\right\| _2 + \varepsilon }\right) \end{aligned}$$
(4)

The overall class-conditional component is then

$$\begin{aligned} S_t^{\text {class}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal {Y}|} \sum _{c \in \mathcal {Y}} S_{t,c}^{\text {class}} \end{aligned}$$
(5)

This term does not attempt to reconstruct the full within-class distribution. Instead, it provides a compact and interpretable summary of how class-conditional feature structure changes over time.

3.3.3 Global integration

The final MSDI score integrates the feature-group and class-conditional components (Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Cai et al. 2025; Hu et al. 2025):

$$\begin{aligned} \text {MSDI}_t = \eta \, S_t^{\text {feat}} + (1 - \eta ) \, S_t^{\text {class}}, \end{aligned}$$
(6)

where \(\eta \in [0, 1]\) controls the relative importance of the two views.

In practice, the resulting score is normalized to the interval [0, 1], and larger values indicate stronger deviation between the current chunk and the reference state. In the following sections, \(\text {MSDI}_t\) denotes this normalized drift score for simplicity. This score serves as a drift-severity signal used by the adaptive weight update mechanism and by candidate response policies during validation calibration.

3.4 Adaptive drift-aware weight update (ADAWU)

Once distributional change is quantified, the ensemble should respond by adjusting the contribution of its base learners (Swarnalatha et al. 2025; Thockchom et al. 2023). To this end, we propose Adaptive Drift-Aware Weight Updating (ADAWU), a dynamic reweighting mechanism that continuously updates model weights according to recent predictive behavior, drift-related information, and temporal relevance.

Assume that the ensemble contains K base learners. Let \(w_{k,t}\) denote the weight of learner k at time t. In the current framework, the updated weight is computed from three components.

First, a performance contribution term reflects the recent predictive quality of learner k:

$$\begin{aligned} P_{k,t} = \alpha \, \text {Perf}_{k,t}, \end{aligned}$$
(7)

where \(\text {Perf}_{k,t}\) denotes a recent performance summary of learner k and \(\alpha \) is its scaling coefficient.

Second, a drift-conditioned reliability term incorporates the current drift signal and adaptation confidence:

$$\begin{aligned} R_t = \beta \left( 1 - \text {MSDI}_t\right) + \gamma \left( 1 - C_t\right) \end{aligned}$$
(8)

where \(C_t \in [0, 1]\) denotes the confidence of the current adaptation context, and \(\beta , \gamma \) are balancing coefficients. Under this formulation, stronger drift or lower confidence leads to a more conservative contribution from this term.

Third, a temporal decay factor is used to moderate the influence of stale historical context:

$$\begin{aligned} T_t = \exp (-\lambda t), \end{aligned}$$
(9)

where \(\lambda \) is the decay rate.

Following the current implementation logic, the intermediate score of learner k can be written as

$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{w}_{k,t} = \alpha \, \text {Perf}_{k,t} + \beta \left( 1 - \text {MSDI}_t\right) + \gamma \, T_t \left( 1 - C_t\right) \end{aligned}$$
(10)

To avoid model starvation, a minimum weight threshold \(w_{\min }\) is imposed:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{w}_{k,t} = \max \left( w_{\min }, \tilde{w}_{k,t}\right) . \end{aligned}$$
(11)

The final normalized weight is then obtained as

$$\begin{aligned} w_{k,t} = \frac{\hat{w}_{k,t}}{\sum _{j=1}^{K} \hat{w}_{j,t}}. \end{aligned}$$
(12)

This design allows the ensemble to adapt gradually rather than switching abruptly between models. Learners that remain useful under the current traffic condition retain larger influence, whereas learners that become less reliable under evolving distributions are downweighted.

It is important to note that ADAWU primarily governs continuous ensemble reweighting during online operation. More aggressive response actions, such as localized retraining or broader model intervention, are not assumed to be always beneficial. They are treated as candidate response actions and are selected only through validation-based calibration, as described in Section 3.5.

3.5 Validation-selected response policy

In non-stationary intrusion detection, not all drift events require the same response (Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2026; Guerrero Cano et al. 2026). Some changes are mild and may be handled through lightweight reweighting, whereas stronger distributional shifts may require more substantial intervention (Jamshidi et al. 2025). However, applying stronger intervention unconditionally can also be harmful. If the current chunk contains noisy labels, short-term imbalance, or a non-representative attack pattern, aggressive retraining may over-adapt to that chunk and degrade attack detection in later segments.

For this reason, the ADAWU-IDS framework does not treat hierarchical response as an always-active core module. Instead, response behavior is formulated as a validation-selected policy. A response policy defines how the framework reacts to the current drift-severity score and adaptation context. Candidate policies may include: 1. weight-only adaptation, where ADAWU updates ensemble weights without retraining; 2. mild response, where only lightweight weight adaptation is applied; 3. moderate response, where localized incremental retraining or targeted parameter refresh may be enabled; 4. severe response, where stronger intervention may be enabled when the validation stream indicates that such intervention is beneficial.

During calibration, candidate configurations differ in the weighting coefficients, drift thresholds, temporal decay setting, minimum weight constraint, and whether stronger response actions are enabled. Each candidate configuration is evaluated only on the held-out validation stream. The final configuration is selected according to an IDS-oriented validation score that emphasizes Attack F1 and Attack Recall while penalizing false negatives. The exact calibration protocol and the selected configuration are reported in Section 5.4.

After validation, the selected response policy is fixed before final testing. No information from the final test stream is used to select thresholds, weighting coefficients, or response actions. This design is intended to prevent test leakage and to avoid treating stronger intervention as universally beneficial. Under the CICIDS2017 protocol used in this study, the validation-selected configuration retains MSDI-guided dynamic weighting but disables hierarchical retraining. Therefore, hierarchical response is best understood as an optional candidate response action rather than a mandatory component of ADAWU-IDS.

3.6 Dynamic ensemble learning framework

The complete ADAWU-IDS framework integrates LSTM-based base learners, MSDI-based drift-severity estimation, ADAWU-based dynamic weight updating, and validation-selected response control into a unified online learning process (Thockchom et al. 2023).

At initialization, the framework trains K diverse LSTM-based learners from the available historical data and assigns them initial ensemble weights. During online operation, the system processes temporally ordered chunks sequentially (Jemili et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2025). For each incoming chunk \(D_t\), the framework first produces ensemble predictions using the current model weights. This order is important: the current chunk is first predicted using the model state available before observing its labels, and only after prediction are labels used for updating under the online evaluation protocol.

The framework then evaluates distributional change by comparing the current chunk with the recent reference state and computes the corresponding \(\textrm{MSDI}_t\). Based on the drift-severity signal, recent predictive behavior, and adaptation context, ADAWU updates the ensemble weights to reflect current learner usefulness and temporal relevance. Finally, the framework follows the adaptation strategy selected during validation. When the selected strategy only supports weight-based adaptation, the system updates learner weights without retraining. Stronger actions, such as retraining or model refresh, are used only if they have been enabled during validation calibration and their predefined conditions are satisfied.

Through this process, ADAWU-IDS aims to maintain detection capability under evolving traffic conditions by combining explicit drift estimation, continuous ensemble reweighting, and severity-aware intervention within a single adaptive framework.

4 System design and implementation

This section describes the implementation pipeline of ADA-WU-IDS. The system is designed as a temporally ordered drift-response framework, in which prediction is performed before any update on the current chunk. This protocol avoids using current labels before prediction and better reflects online deployment conditions. The framework consists of five main stages: data preprocessing and sequence construction, initial ensemble training, online prediction, drift-severity estimation and dynamic weight updating, and validation-selected response control.

4.1 Architecture overview

The proposed ADAWU-IDS framework is implemented as a chronological drift-response pipeline. Unlike a static deployment setting, in which a trained model is directly applied to all future traffic, ADAWU-IDS processes incoming traffic chunks sequentially and updates its adaptive state only after predictions for the current chunk have been produced. This design is important for avoiding test-label leakage and for reflecting a realistic online evaluation setting.

Figure 1 summarizes the chronological workflow of ADAWU-IDS. Each incoming traffic chunk is first processed and evaluated using the current ensemble state before its labels are observed. After prediction, delayed labels become available and are used for online evaluation, MSDI-supported drift-severity analysis, and ADAWU-based weight updating. Any stronger response action is applied only when selected through validation calibration. The updated ensemble and reference state are then carried forward to the next chunk.

4.2 Data preprocessing module

The preprocessing module converts raw network traffic records into sequence-based model inputs compatible with the LSTM backbone (Hore et al. 2024; Sajid et al. 2024; Dhanvijay et al. 2025). In the current study, the CICIDS2017 dataset is processed through four main steps.

First, a subset of 78 discriminative traffic features is retained (Xu et al. 2024; Mary et al. 2024). These features cover flow-level statistics, packet-size descriptors, directional packet information, TCP flag behavior, and temporal characteristics. This selection is consistent with the grouped feature design later used by MSDI.

Second, missing-value handling and feature normalization are applied before model training and evaluation. In the current pipeline, missing values are imputed and the resulting features are standardized using statistics estimated from the initialization-stage data only. This prevents leakage of future evaluation information into the preprocessing stage.

Third, the feature vectors are reshaped into fixed-length sequences (Hore et al. 2024; Bamber et al. 2025; Devendiran and Turukmane 2024). The current implementation uses sequence length L = 10, so that each input sample contains ten consecutive observations with 78 features per step. This representation allows the downstream LSTM model to capture short-range temporal dependencies across adjacent traffic observations.

Fourth, the preprocessed data are organized into temporally ordered chunks for subsequent evaluation and adaptation. This chunk-based design is important because the framework operates under chronological distribution shift rather than under i.i.d. sampling assumptions.

Overall, the preprocessing module serves two purposes: it produces stable inputs for the predictive backbone and establishes the temporal organization required for drift-aware evaluation.

4.3 Model training module

The training module initializes the predictive backbone of ADAWU-IDS before online adaptation begins. The current implementation follows a two-stage procedure.

First, the system constructs LSTM-based base learners using the architecture described in Section 3.2. Each model is initialized independently so that the ensemble contains diversified learners rather than identical copies of the same network instance (Swarnalatha et al. 2025; Thockchom et al. 2023; Nassreddine et al. 2025). In the training code, multiple learners are created with different random seeds, which improves the potential usefulness of later adaptive reweighting.

Second, the initialized learners are trained on the same initialization-stage data under a consistent optimization protocol. During ensemble initialization, the framework uses bootstrap-like resampling to provide each learner with a slightly different training view, further increasing ensemble diversity. No validation or test data are used during initial training. The held-out validation stream is reserved for response-policy calibration.

In the current experimental pipeline, the ensemble contains three base learners with uniform initial weights. The purpose of this design is not to maximize architectural heterogeneity, but to provide a controlled setting in which weight adaptation can be examined clearly. After training is completed, the initialization-stage data are also used to establish the initial reference state for later drift detection.

This module therefore provides the offline foundation of ADAWU-IDS: a set of LSTM-based learners that share the same general architecture and task objective, but retain sufficient diversity to support adaptive ensemble control during online deployment.

4.4 Online prediction and update protocol

The online adaptation module is the central operational component of ADAWU-IDS. It combines drift monitoring, adaptive weight control, and applies the validation-selected response policy fixed before final testing within a chunk-based chronological evaluation setting.

The module contains three interconnected components:

Drift monitoring component: The component combines MSDI with complementary performance-based and distribu-tion-based signals (Hu et al. 2025; Mahdi et al. 2024). In the current code structure, drift evidence may arise from three sources: predictive degradation on the current chunk, distributional deviation from the reference state, and the MSDI score itself. These signals are aggregated into a confidence-aware decision that determines whether the current chunk shows meaningful distributional deviation from the recent reference state. This component is used for drift-severity monitoring and response support, rather than as a standalone binary drift detector.

Weight updater: The weight updater executes ADAWU to rebalance the contribution of ensemble members over time (Thockchom et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2024). For each base learner, the update considers recent predictive behavior, the current drift score, adaptation confidence, and a temporal decay factor. The resulting weights are normalized and constrained by a minimum weight threshold to avoid model starvation. This mechanism allows the ensemble to adapt continuously without forcing abrupt model switching in every window.

Response-control module: The response-control module applies the response policy selected on the held-out validation stream. In the calibrated CICIDS2017 configuration, this policy relies on MSDI-guided dynamic weighting and disables hierarchical retraining. Stronger intervention is therefore treated as an optional candidate action rather than a mandatory response.

Operationally, the online adaptation module processes each incoming chunk in sequence. It first obtains the current prediction result, then estimates drift, updates ensemble weights, determines drift severity, and finally applies stronger adaptation only when necessary. This staged design is important because it separates routine monitoring and reweighting from higher-cost adaptation actions, thereby supporting the deployment-oriented trade-off between robustness and efficiency.

4.5 Drift-severity estimation and dynamic weight updating

After each chunk is evaluated, MSDI is computed to summarize the degree of distributional change between the current chunk and the reference state. As described in Section 3.3, MSDI combines feature-level and class-conditional information to provide a structured drift-severity signal. The resulting value is not treated as a perfect drift detector; rather, it is used as diagnostic information for adaptive weighting and response control.

The ADAWU module then updates the ensemble weights. Learners with stronger recent predictive behavior retain greater influence, while learners that become less reliable under changing traffic conditions are downweighted. The weight update also incorporates drift-severity information and temporal relevance, allowing the ensemble to adapt gradually instead of switching abruptly between models.

To avoid learner starvation, a minimum weight constraint is used before normalization. This prevents any base learner from being completely eliminated due to temporary degradation and allows it to regain influence if its later predictive behavior improves. All experiments were conducted on a machine equipped with 16 vCPU Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6430 CPU and RTX 4090(24GB) GPU.

4.6 Validation-selected response control

The response-control module determines whether the system should rely only on dynamic weighting or apply stronger adaptation actions. In the proposed implementation, response behavior is not fixed a priori. Candidate configurations are evaluated on a held-out validation stream, and the selected configuration is fixed before final testing.

This design is important because stronger response actions, such as retraining or aggressive model intervention, may be harmful when the current chunk is noisy, highly imbalanced, or not representative of future traffic. Therefore, ADAWU-IDS treats hierarchical response as an optional candidate action rather than a mandatory core module.

During final testing, the system applies only the validation-selected response policy. No information from the final test stream is used to choose thresholds, weighting coefficients, or response actions. Under the CICIDS2017 protocol used in this study, the selected configuration retains MSDI-guided dynamic weighting and disables hierarchical retraining. The final test-time configuration is denoted as ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated in the experimental sections. This name refers to the validation-selected instance of the ADAWU-IDS framework, rather than to a separate model architecture.

4.7 Implementation and reproducibility considerations

The implementation is organized to ensure that training, validation, and test operations remain separated. Initial training is performed only on the designated training stream. Validation data are used only for calibration of response configuration and adaptive-control parameters. Final test segments are used only after the configuration has been fixed.

All adaptive methods in the final evaluation follow the same chronological protocol: the model first predicts the current chunk, and only then uses the current labels for updating. This protocol is applied consistently to ADAWU-IDS and the online ensemble baselines to support fair comparison.

For reproducibility, the experimental section reports the chronological data split, the evaluated baselines, the selected validation configuration, repeated-run results, statistical tests, ablation settings, segment-wise performance, drift-detection diagnostics, and computational cost. These components are reported to clarify not only the predictive behavior of ADAWU-IDS, but also its calibration behavior, component interactions, and deployment trade-offs.

5 Experimental design

This section describes the experimental protocol used to evaluate ADAWU-IDS under temporally ordered concept drift conditions. The evaluation is designed to avoid information leakage between training, validation, and test streams. All adaptive methods follow the same online protocol: each temporal chunk is first predicted using the current model state, and the labels of that chunk are used only after prediction for model updating or adaptation. CICIDS2017 is used as the main chronological evaluation dataset, while UNSW-NB15 is retained as a secondary dataset for cross-dataset generalization analysis.

5.1 Dataset and chronological partitioning

The experiments are conducted on two widely used intrusion detection datasets: CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15. CICIDS2017 contains benign and attack traffic collected over multiple days, making it suitable for evaluating concept drift-aware intrusion detection under temporally ordered conditions. UNSW-NB15 is used as an additional dataset to examine whether the proposed framework generalizes beyond CICIDS2017 and to assess dataset-specific limitations.

For CICIDS2017, this study adopts a strictly chronological partitioning strategy instead of random train-test splitting. Tuesday and Wednesday are used for initial training. Thursday WebAttacks is used as the validation stream for hyperparameter calibration and response-policy selection. Thursday Infiltration, Friday Morning, and Friday PortScan are used only for final testing. No samples from the final test segments are used during training, validation, hyperparameter selection, or response-policy calibration.

This chronological design has two purposes. First, it preserves the temporal order of traffic evolution and prevents information leakage caused by random shuffling. Second, it allows the evaluation to examine whether an adaptive framework calibrated on a held-out validation stream can generalize to later unseen traffic and attack segments.

UNSW-NB15 is retained as a secondary cross-dataset generalization case. Unlike CICIDS2017, it does not provide the same multi-day chronological structure for constructing a directly comparable drift stream with separate training, validation, and later attack windows. Therefore, the UNSW-NB15 results are interpreted as robustness and generalization evidence rather than as the primary validation-calibrated chronological drift evaluation. This distinction is important because the proposed framework may require dataset-specific calibration when transferred to traffic distributions different from CICIDS2017.

The preprocessing procedure follows the same general principles for both datasets. Non-numeric identifiers are removed when appropriate, categorical attributes are encoded, missing or invalid values are handled, and numerical features are normalized using statistics estimated only from the training portion. For sequence-based LSTM input, traffic records are transformed into fixed-length temporal sequences. The same preprocessing logic is applied consistently to ADAWU-IDS and the compared baselines within each dataset.

Because this study focuses on preventing test leakage and evaluating validation-calibrated adaptation under chronological drift, CICIDS2017 is used for the main fair comparison, validation calibration, statistical testing, ablation study, segment-wise drift-window analysis, drift-detection diagnostics, and computational-cost analysis. UNSW-NB15 is discussed as a secondary generalization case, especially in relation to dataset-specific calibration and the limitations of ADAWU-IDS under different traffic distributions.

5.2 Baselines and comparative methods

The following baselines and compared methods are evaluated under the same chronological protocol whenever applicable.

Static LSTM is used as the non-adaptive baseline. It is trained on the initial training stream and then directly evaluated on the test stream without online adaptation. This baseline measures the degradation of a static temporal classifier under later traffic shifts.

ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated denotes the validation-selected configuration of the proposed ADAWU-IDS framework. The configuration is selected only on the CICIDS2017 Thursday WebAttacks validation stream and then fixed before final CICIDS2017 testing. This name refers to the calibrated instance of ADAWU-IDS rather than to a separate model architecture.

Full default ADAWU denotes the full configuration in which MSDI, dynamic weighting, and hierarchical response are all enabled according to the initial design. This configuration is included in the ablation study to examine whether the complete uncalibrated response mechanism is always beneficial.

DWM, Online Bagging, and Leveraging Bagging are included as classical online ensemble baselines for non-stationary data streams. These methods are evaluated under the same prediction-before-update protocol as ADAWU-IDS in the CICIDS2017 chronological experiment. They are included to provide a fair comparison with established adaptive ensemble methods rather than only with a static baseline.

Additional ablation variants are evaluated to analyze component contributions and interactions, including variants without MSDI, without dynamic weighting, and without hierarchical response. The ablation design is described in Section 5.5, and the results are reported in Section 6.4.

For UNSW-NB15, the comparison follows the dataset protocol used for the secondary generalization experiment. Because UNSW-NB15 does not provide the same chronological validation structure as CICIDS2017, its results are interpreted cautiously and are not used to claim universal cross-dataset superiority.

5.3 Online evaluation protocol and metrics

All adaptive methods in the CICIDS2017 experiment are evaluated using a chunk-based online protocol. For each incoming test chunk, the method first predicts the labels of the current chunk using the model state available before observing the current labels. After prediction, the true labels of the chunk are revealed and used for evaluation, weight updating, and any response action allowed by the method.

This prediction-before-update order is applied consistently to ADAWU-IDS and all adaptive baselines. It prevents the model from using labels from the current chunk before making predictions on that same chunk. Therefore, the reported results reflect online adaptation behavior rather than retrospective optimization.

For ADAWU-IDS, MSDI is computed after prediction using the current chunk and the reference state. ADAWU then updates ensemble weights for subsequent chunks. If stronger response actions are enabled by the selected policy, they are applied only after the current chunk has already been evaluated. In the final calibrated configuration selected in this study, hierarchical retraining is disabled and the framework relies on MSDI-guided dynamic weighting.

The evaluation reports both aggregate classification metrics and IDS-sensitive attack-detection metrics. Weighted F1 is used to summarize overall classification performance under class imbalance. However, because intrusion detection is especially sensitive to missed attacks, Weighted F1 alone is insufficient. Therefore, Attack Recall, Attack F1, and false-negative rate are also reported.

Attack Recall measures the proportion of attack samples correctly detected:

$$\begin{aligned} \text {Attack Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \end{aligned}$$
(13)

Attack F1 summarizes the balance between attack precision and attack recall:

$$\begin{aligned} \text {Attack F1} = 2 \times \frac{\text {Attack Precision} \times \text {Attack Recall}}{\text {Attack Precision} + \text {Attack Recall}} \end{aligned}$$
(14)

The false-negative rate is defined as:

$$\begin{aligned} \text {FNR} = \frac{FN}{TP + FN} \end{aligned}$$
(15)

where TP denotes attack samples correctly classified as attacks and FN denotes attack samples incorrectly classified as benign. Lower FNR is particularly important for IDS deployment because false negatives correspond to missed attacks.

For drift-detection diagnostics, precision, recall, and false alarm rate are reported when comparing detected drift alarms with coarse segment-level drift annotations. These diagnostic results are used only to assess the usefulness of MSDI as a drift-severity signal. They are not treated as proof that MSDI is a complete standalone drift detector.

Computational cost is measured as mean latency per chunk. This metric is used to compare the adaptation overhead of ADAWU-IDS with Static LSTM and classical online ensemble baselines.

5.4 Hyperparameter calibration and response policy selection

To address the sensitivity of adaptive parameters and response thresholds, ADAWU-IDS uses a held-out validation stream for calibration in the main CICIDS2017 experiment. The training, validation, and test streams are strictly separated. Tuesday and Wednesday are used for initial training, Thursday WebAttacks is used only for validation-based calibration, and Thursday Infiltration, Friday Morning, and Friday PortScan are used only for final testing.

All adaptive parameters are fixed before final testing. No information from the final test segments is used to select weighting coefficients, drift thresholds, minimum weight constraints, or response actions. This design is intended to avoid evaluation leakage and to ensure that the final results reflect a validation-selected configuration rather than test-set tuning.

The calibrated parameters include the performance coefficient \(\alpha \), the MSDI-related drift-severity coefficient \(\beta \), the confidence / temporal-relevance coefficient \(\gamma \), the temporal decay factor \(\lambda \), the minimum weight constraint \(w_{\min }\), the drift-threshold setting, and whether hierarchical retraining is enabled. These parameters directly control the behavior of ADAWU and the response policy. Therefore, they are selected using only the held-out validation stream before final chronological evaluation.

Table 1 summarizes the candidate parameter settings considered during validation-only calibration. The candidate settings were designed to cover conservative, default, and attack-sensitive adaptation behaviors. Conservative settings assign less influence to short-term adaptation and reduce the probability of aggressive response. Default settings follow the initial ADAWU design. Attack-sensitive settings place greater emphasis on Attack F1, Attack Recall, and false-negative reduction during validation selection.

The validation score is an attack-sensitive objective that combines Attack F1, Attack Recall, and FNR with fixed weights. It favors configurations with stronger attack detection and fewer missed attacks. The same scoring rule is applied to all candidate configurations on the Thursday WebAttacks validation stream. The final test stream is never used to modify the scoring rule, the candidate set, or the selected configuration.

As shown in Table 2, the validation stream selects the weight_only_attack_sensitive configuration. This configuration retains MSDI-guided dynamic weighting but disables hierarchical retraining. Compared with the full hierarchical configurations, the selected configuration achieves the best attack-sensitive validation score, the highest Attack Recall, the highest Attack F1, and the lowest false-negative rate on the validation stream. This result indicates that stronger hierarchical response is not automatically beneficial and should be selected through validation calibration rather than enabled unconditionally.

No final test-segment information is used during calibration. After the validation-selected configuration is fixed, it is applied unchanged to Thursday Infiltration, Friday Morning, and Friday PortScan. The final test-time configuration is denoted as ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated. This name refers to the validation-selected instance of the ADAWU-IDS framework rather than to a separate model architecture.

It should be noted that this validation-only calibration does not claim universal optimality of the selected coefficients or thresholds. Instead, it provides a leakage-free procedure for selecting a fixed response policy before chronological testing. Although the candidate settings cover conservative, default, and attack-sensitive behaviors, the current study does not exhaustively evaluate all possible parameter combinations across multiple datasets and drift regimes. Broader sensitivity analysis under gradual, recurring, and class-conditional drift remains an important direction for future work.

5.5 Ablation study design, statistical validation, and reporting structure

To examine the contribution and interaction of the proposed components, an ablation study is conducted in addition to the main chronological comparison. The ablation study compares the validation-selected ADAWU-IDS configuration with the full default ADAWU configuration and component-removal variants. Specifically, the evaluated variants include ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated, Full default ADAWU, w/o MSDI, w/o Dynamic Weighting, and w/o Hierarchical Response.

The purpose of the ablation study is not only to determine whether each component improves performance individually, but also to identify harmful component interactions. This is important because adaptive mechanisms may not always be beneficial under non-stationary and imbalanced IDS streams. In particular, stronger response actions such as hierarchical retraining may over-adapt to short-term chunk labels when the current chunk is noisy, imbalanced, or not representative of later traffic. Therefore, the ablation study is used to evaluate whether the full response mechanism should be retained as an always-active module or treated as a candidate response action selected through validation calibration.

To assess variability, the main CICIDS2017 experiments are repeated across three random seeds. The reported results are based on the mean performance across these runs. In addition, paired statistical tests are performed between ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated and Static LSTM on the main IDS-sensitive metrics. A p-value threshold of 0.05 is used. If the paired comparison does not reach this threshold, the result is interpreted as an improvement trend rather than statistically significant superiority.

This design follows a conservative interpretation strategy. The purpose is not to overstate the performance of ADAWU-IDS, but to determine whether the validation-calibrated framework provides reliable improvement over static deployment, how its components interact, and how it compares with established online ensemble baselines.

The experimental results are reported in Section 6. The section first presents the chronological comparison among Static LSTM, ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated, and representative online ensemble baselines, followed by paired statistical testing to assess whether the observed differences are statistically reliable. It then reports segment-wise drift-window results to examine temporal performance variation under drift, and provides ablation analysis to clarify the effects and interactions of the main adaptive components. Additional drift-detection diagnostics are included to interpret the behavior of MSDI as an auxiliary drift-severity signal rather than as a standalone detector. Computational cost is discussed separately in Section 7.2 to clarify the practical overhead introduced by the adaptive framework. The UNSW-NB15 results are presented as a secondary cross-dataset generalization analysis and are interpreted as evidence of dataset-specific limitations rather than universal transferability.

This reporting structure is designed to address not only aggregate performance, but also statistical robustness, false-negative behavior, component-level limitations, drift-detec-tion reliability, cross-dataset generalization, and deployment trade-offs.

5.6 Reproducibility and code availability

To improve reproducibility, the released repository includes the preprocessing scripts, chronological split configuration, model-training code, validation-calibration procedure, baseline implementations, and scripts for reproducing the main tables and figures. The chronological experiments can be reproduced by following three steps. First, the CICIDS2017 data should be prepared using the provided preprocessing script and the reported 78-feature list. Second, the validation-calibration script should be executed to select the response configuration without using the final test stream. Third, the chronological evaluation script should be run with the fixed configuration and the random seeds reported in this paper.

The repository also documents the sequence length, chunk size, random seeds, selected hyperparameters, baseline settings, and commands used to generate the statistical tests, ablation results, drift-detection diagnostics, and computational-cost analysis. This structure is intended to make the chronological evaluation protocol transparent and to reduce ambiguity in reproducing the reported results.

6 Experimental results

This section reports the experimental results of ADAWU-IDS under the fair chronological CICIDS2017 protocol. The evaluation focuses on five aspects: overall comparison with static and adaptive baselines, statistical validation, segment-wise drift-window performance, ablation analysis, and drift-detection diagnostics. All adaptive methods follow the same online evaluation order: each method first predicts the current chunk and then uses the current chunk labels for updating. The validation stream is used only for calibration, and the final test segments are not used for selecting thresholds, weighting coefficients, or response actions.

The results are interpreted according to the positioning adopted in this study. The objective is not to demonstrate universal predictive superiority over all adaptive baselines. Instead, the experiments examine whether the validation-calibrated ADAWU-IDS configuration provides interpretable drift-response behavior and whether it can mitigate degradation relative to a static LSTM baseline under a chronological test protocol.

6.1 Fair chronological comparison

Table 3 presents the main comparison under the fair chronological CICIDS2017 protocol. The evaluated methods include the Static LSTM baseline, the validation-calibrated ADAWU-IDS configuration, and three classical adaptive online ensemble baselines: Dynamic Weighted Majority, Online Bagging, and Leveraging Bagging. Instead, ADAWU-IDS should be interpreted as an interpretable and validation-calibrated drift-response framework that provides modest improvement over a static LSTM baseline in selected F1-based metrics, while remaining less competitive than established online ensemble baselines in terms of attack recall and false-negative reduction.

These segment-wise results reinforce the overall conclusion. ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated can provide modest F1-based improvement over Static LSTM in certain drift-affected windows, but it does not consistently improve attack recall or reduce false negatives. The results also show that classical online ensemble methods remain more effective when the primary objective is raw predictive performance under chronological drift.

6.4 Ablation study and component interaction

Table 6 presents the ablation study used to examine the contribution and interaction of the main ADAWU-IDS components. The evaluated configurations include the validation-calibrated configuration, the full default ADAWU configuration, and variants without MSDI, without dynamic weighting, and without hierarchical response.

The most important observation is that the full default ADAWU configuration performs poorly. Its Weighted F1 is 0.6967, Attack Recall is 0.0001, Attack F1 is 0.0002, and FNR is 0.9999. This indicates that the mandatory hierarchical response in the default configuration can severely harm attack detection under the current chronological CICIDS2017 setting.

This result reveals an important component interaction issue. Stronger intervention is not necessarily beneficial when applied unconditionally. If hierarchical retraining or aggressive response is triggered under short-term chunk labels that are imbalanced, noisy, or not representative of future traffic, the model may over-adapt to the current chunk and lose its ability to detect attack traffic in later segments. Therefore, hierarchical response should not be treated as an always-active core component.

The validation-calibrated configuration avoids this failure by disabling hierarchical retraining and retaining the weight-only attack-sensitive configuration. This result supports the design choice of selecting response actions through validation calibration rather than assuming that stronger adaptation is universally useful.

The ablation results also show that removing MSDI, dynamic weighting, or hierarchical response produces results close to the calibrated configuration under the current CICIDS2017 setting. This indicates that the marginal contribution of MSDI and dynamic weighting is limited in this particular protocol. Therefore, MSDI should be interpreted as a drift-severity signal that supports transparent response analysis rather than as a standalone guarantee of predictive improvement.

Overall, the ablation study clarifies the role of the ADAWU-IDS components. The main value of the framework lies in exposing and controlling drift-response behavior through validation calibration. The results do not support the claim that every adaptive component is independently beneficial in all settings. Instead, they show that adaptive response actions require careful calibration and that component interactions can strongly affect IDS performance.

6.5 Drift detection diagnostic analysis

Table 7 reports the drift-detection diagnostic results of MSDI under the chronological CICIDS2017 protocol. This analysis evaluates whether the MSDI signal aligns with drift-affected regions at the segment level. The purpose is not to claim that MSDI is a complete standalone drift detector, but to examine its usefulness as a diagnostic drift-severity signal.

The results show that MSDI does not achieve robust standalone drift detection under the current evaluation setting. The diagnostic precision is 0.2564, recall is 0.3448, and false alarm rate is 0.5918. These values indicate that the MSDI signal captures only part of the drift-affected behavior and also produces a substantial number of false alarms.

Several factors may explain this result. First, the available drift labels are coarse and segment-level, whereas distributional change may occur gradually or locally within a segment. Second, network traffic drift may be class-conditional, meaning that changes in attack traffic and benign traffic may not occur at the same time or with the same magnitude. Third, recurring or gradual drift may not produce a sharp distributional boundary, making it difficult for a distance-based signal to align perfectly with drift labels.

Therefore, MSDI should not be interpreted as a perfect drift detector. In this study, it is used as a drift-severity signal for adaptive weighting and response-policy analysis. The diagnostic results support a cautious interpretation: MSDI can provide interpretable information about distributional change, but it requires further improvement and validation under more challenging drift scenarios, including gradual drift, recurring drift, and class-conditional drift.

In summary, the experimental results provide a conservative but informative assessment of ADAWU-IDS. The validation-calibrated configuration shows modest improvement over Static LSTM in Weighted F1 and Attack F1, but the improvement is not statistically significant and does not translate into consistent false-negative reduction. Classical online ensemble baselines remain stronger in raw predictive performance. The ablation study further shows that mandatory hierarchical response can be harmful, while the drift-detection diagnostics indicate that MSDI should be treated as a drift-severity signal rather than a complete detector. These findings support the positioning of ADAWU-IDS as an interpretable and validation-calibrated drift-response framework, not as a universally superior intrusion detection classifier.

7 Discussion

This section discusses ADAWU-IDS from four deployment-oriented perspectives: its practical motivation relative to strong online ensemble baselines, the interpretation of MSDI as a drift-severity signal, cross-dataset generalizability, and deployment trade-offs involving false-negative risk, calibration dependence, and computational cost.

7.1 Practical motivation relative to strong online ensemble baselines

The comparison with classical online ensemble baselines clarifies the complementary role of ADAWU-IDS. Dynamic Weighted Majority, Online Bagging, and Leveraging Bagging remain stronger choices when the primary deployment objective is raw predictive performance, especially Attack Recall and false-negative reduction. ADAWU-IDS addresses a different deployment need: transparent drift-response analysis, response-policy calibration, and component-level diagnosis under chronological traffic evolution. These results indicate that when the primary deployment goal is to maximize immediate predictive accuracy or minimize missed attacks under limited computational cost, classical online ensembles remain highly competitive choices.

The motivation of ADAWU-IDS is therefore complementary rather than purely competitive. Classical online ensembles are effective because they update learner influence or sample weighting according to recent prediction behavior. However, their adaptation process is usually less explicit in explaining whether a performance change is associated with distributional drift, how severe the drift is, and whether stronger intervention should be applied or avoided. In practical IDS deployment, these questions can be important because operators may need not only a prediction output, but also diagnostic information about traffic evolution, model temporal relevance, and response-policy sensitivity.

ADAWU-IDS addresses this deployment need by coupling drift-severity estimation, adaptive weight control, and validation-selected response policy in a temporally ordered IDS pipeline. MSDI provides an interpretable indication of distributional pressure from feature-group and class-conditional perspectives. ADAWU uses recent predictive behavior, MSDI-based drift severity, and temporal relevance to adjust ensemble contributions. The response policy is selected on a held-out chronological validation stream rather than being treated as a mandatory intervention. This design makes it possible to examine when adaptive components are useful, when they become harmful, and how response actions should be calibrated before final testing.

Thus, the practical value of ADAWU-IDS lies in transparent drift-response analysis and deployment calibration. It is most relevant when interpretability, auditability, response control, and failure-case analysis are required alongside predictive performance. This role complements accuracy-oriented online ensembles rather than replacing them in low-latency attack detection.

7.2 Practical interpretation and robustness of MSDI

MSDI is designed as an auxiliary drift-severity signal rather than a standalone binary drift detector. Its purpose is to summarize the magnitude of temporal distributional deviation from complementary feature-group and class-conditional perspectives, and to provide interpretable information for downstream adaptive weighting and response-policy calibration. Therefore, a high MSDI value should not be interpreted automatically as evidence that retraining is required, and a low MSDI value does not necessarily imply that the classifier will remain reliable.

The behavior of MSDI depends on the type and severity of the underlying drift. Under abrupt and distributionally separable drift, feature-group and class-conditional deviations are more likely to increase simultaneously, making MSDI easier to interpret as a strong drift-severity signal. Under gradual drift, the change may accumulate slowly across consecutive chunks, and the MSDI trajectory may show a progressive increase rather than an isolated peak. Under recurring drift, MSDI may increase when the current traffic distribution departs from the recent reference window, even if a similar pattern appeared earlier in the stream. This reflects the local-reference nature of the current implementation. Under class-conditional drift, MSDI may be informative when the feature structure of benign or malicious samples changes, but its reliability depends on the availability and representativeness of class labels within each chunk.

MSDI and predictive degradation are not necessarily synchronized. A high MSDI value with limited performance loss may indicate covariate shift, benign traffic-composition changes, or distributional movement that does not substantially affect the decision boundary. Conversely, a low or moderate MSDI value with clear attack-detection degradation may occur when the drift mainly affects rare attack patterns, minority-class behavior, or class boundaries that are not fully captured by aggregate distributional summaries. For this reason, MSDI should be interpreted together with IDS-sensitive metrics such as Attack Recall, false-negative rate, Attack F1, and segment-wise post-drift performance. Table 8 summarizes the expected interpretation of MSDI under different drift scenarios.

In the proposed framework, MSDI is therefore used as a response-support signal. It contributes drift-severity information to ADAWU-based weight updating and helps analyze when adaptive intervention may be useful. However, the final response policy is not triggered solely by MSDI. Candidate response configurations are selected through chronological validation calibration, and the selected configuration is fixed before final testing. This design reduces the risk of treating every distributional deviation as requiring stronger intervention, which is particularly important when short-term chunks are noisy, imbalanced, or not representative of future traffic. Therefore, MSDI is most informative when distributional deviation is aligned with observable feature-group or class-conditional changes, but it should be interpreted together with attack-sensitive predictive metrics when drift affects rare attacks or decision boundaries.

7.3 Cross-dataset generalizability and dataset-specific calibration

The UNSW-NB15 evaluation indicates that the effectiveness of ADAWU-IDS is strongly affected by dataset-specific distributional structure and calibration conditions. Unlike CICIDS2017, which contains more temporally concentrated attack periods and clearer traffic-phase changes, UNSW-NB15 presents a more mixed traffic composition, different attack-category distribution, and different feature-generation process. As a result, a response policy calibrated under one chronological traffic stream should not be assumed to transfer directly to another dataset. This observation is important because it shows that drift-aware IDS frameworks require not only adaptive mechanisms, but also dataset-aware calibration before deployment.

A key reason is that distributional deviation and classification difficulty may not be aligned in the same way across datasets. MSDI measures feature-group and class-conditional distributional changes relative to a recent reference state. This signal can be informative when distributional movement corresponds to meaningful changes in the classification problem. However, on UNSW-NB15, the traffic distribution may contain more overlapping attack and benign patterns, more mixed attack categories, or less clearly separated temporal drift segments. In such cases, an increase in MSDI may indicate distributional pressure without necessarily identifying the changes that most affect the decision boundary. Conversely, security-critical degradation may occur in minority attack patterns even when the aggregate MSDI value remains moderate.

This mismatch helps explain why explicit drift-response control may be less effective than classical online ensemble adaptation on UNSW-NB15. Methods such as Dynamic Weighted Majority, Online Bagging, and Leveraging Bagging respond directly to recent prediction errors or resam-pling-based online updates. When the main objective is raw predictive performance, especially Attack Recall and false-negative reduction, such error-driven mechanisms can adapt more directly to the observed classification behavior. ADAWU-IDS, by contrast, introduces an additional drift-severity signal and response-control mechanism. These components provide diagnostic value, but they may also amplify short-term imbalance, noisy chunk behavior, or non-representative distributional changes when the calibration stream does not adequately match the final test stream.

The ablation behavior on UNSW-NB15 should therefore be interpreted as evidence of dataset-dependent component interaction. If removing MSDI or weakening adaptive response improves performance on a specific dataset, this does not necessarily mean that the component is always invalid. Rather, it indicates that the benefit of MSDI-based weighting depends on whether the drift-severity signal is aligned with attack-sensitive predictive degradation. When this alignment is weak, simpler error-driven online ensembles may provide stronger classification performance. This finding supports a calibration-aware interpretation of ADAWU-IDS and cautions against direct cross-dataset transfer without reconfiguration.

The UNSW-NB15 results also highlight the limitation of the current calibration strategy. The selected response policy, drift threshold, and weighting parameters are calibrated on a held-out chronological validation stream. This procedure reduces overclaiming and prevents response actions from being treated as automatically beneficial. However, the candidate search space remains limited, and the validation stream may not cover all drift regimes or attack-composition changes encountered in another dataset. Therefore, the selected configuration should be regarded as dataset-specific rather than universally transferable.

These results suggest that ADAWU-IDS should not be deployed on a new dataset by directly transferring the CICIDS2017-calibrated configuration; instead, dataset-specific validation calibration is necessary. In practical deployment, ADAWU-IDS should be recalibrated when applied to a new network environment. Such recalibration should consider not only aggregate metrics such as Accuracy and Weighted F1, but also attack-sensitive objectives including Attack Recall, false-negative rate, and Attack F1. Future work should further investigate which dataset characteristics make MSDI-based response control beneficial, neutral, or harmful. Controlled studies involving different attack distributions, temporal drift structures, class-imbalance levels, and recurring or gradual drift patterns would help clarify the conditions under which diagnostic drift-response frameworks provide advantages beyond classical online ensemble baselines.

7.4 Deployment trade-offs, limitations, and future implications

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated provides only modest improvements over Static LSTM in selected F1-based metrics, and these improvements are not statistically significant at the p< 0.05 level. More importantly for IDS deployment, the calibrated framework does not consistently improve Attack Recall or reduce the false-negative rate. Since missed attacks are often more costly than benign misclassification in security applications, in high-risk settings, deployment decisions should prioritize Attack Recall and false-negative reduction over aggregate F1 gains.

Second, adaptive response introduces additional computational cost. The calibrated ADAWU-IDS configuration requires more processing time per chunk than the classical online ensemble baselines. This overhead is mainly associated with multi-learner LSTM inference, MSDI computation, adaptive weight updating, and response-control logic. Therefore, in latency-sensitive environments where the main requirement is fast and accurate classification, simpler online ensembles may be more suitable. Its added value is strongest when operators require interpretable drift diagnosis and calibrated response analysis.

Third, the validation-calibration strategy depends on the representativeness of the validation stream. If the validation period does not contain drift patterns similar to those encountered during final deployment, the selected response policy may be suboptimal. A limited candidate search space can also restrict the achievable performance of the calibrated configuration. These limitations are especially relevant for cross-dataset transfer, where traffic composition, attack distribution, and temporal drift structure may differ substantially from the calibration data.

Fourth, MSDI should be viewed as a complementary severity indicator rather than a complete replacement for performance-based monitoring. As discussed in Section 7.2, MSDI may respond strongly to distributional changes that do not affect classification performance, and it may be less sensitive to rare but security-critical decision-boundary changes. Practical deployment should therefore combine MSDI with attack-sensitive predictive metrics, uncertainty monitoring, and failure-case inspection.

Future work should address these limitations in several directions. First, larger repeated-run studies and broader uncertainty analysis would improve confidence in the obser-ved trends. Second, controlled experiments involving gradual, recurring, class-conditional, and rare-attack drift would provide a more systematic assessment of MSDI robustness. Third, stronger attack-sensitive calibration objectives could be explored to prioritize Attack Recall and false-negative reduction rather than aggregate F1 alone. Fourth, lightweight backbones or incremental update mechanisms could reduce computational cost. Finally, broader cross-dataset and real-world streaming evaluations are needed to identify when diagnostic drift-response frameworks such as ADAWU-IDS provide practical benefits beyond accuracy-oriented online ensemble baselines.

8 Conclusion and future work

This study proposed ADAWU-IDS, an interpretable and validation-calibrated drift-response framework for concept drift-aware network intrusion detection. The framework couples MSDI-based drift-severity estimation, ADAWU-based dynamic ensemble weighting, and validation-selected response control within a chronological IDS evaluation pipeline. Unlike fixed-response adaptive designs, ADAWU-IDS does not assume that stronger intervention is always beneficial. Instead, candidate response configurations are calibrated on a held-out validation stream before final chronological testing. The contribution of ADAWU-IDS is therefore not a new standalone classifier or a universally superior online ensemble, but a calibrated framework that explicitly links drift-severity estimation, adaptive weight control, and response-policy selection under chronological IDS evaluation.

The experimental results provide a cautious understanding of the framework. Under the chronological CICIDS2017 protocol, ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated shows modest improvements over Static LSTM in Weighted F1 and Attack F1. However, these improvements are not statistically significant at the p< 0.05 level. The calibrated framework does not consistently improve Attack Recall or reduce the false-negative rate, and classical online ensemble baselines achieve stronger raw predictive performance under the same chronological protocol. These findings indicate that ADAWU-IDS should not be interpreted as a universally superior IDS classifier.

The ablation and diagnostic analyses further clarify the role of each adaptive component. MSDI provides an interpretable drift-severity signal that helps characterize distributional deviation, but it should not be used as a standalone binary drift detector. ADAWU supports continuous ensemble reweighting by incorporating recent predictive behavior, drift-severity information, and temporal relevance. In contrast, stronger response actions such as retraining or model refresh may over-adapt to short-term chunk behavior when applied unconditionally. Therefore, hierarchical response is better formulated as an optional response action selected through validation calibration rather than as a mandatory component.

Overall, the main value of ADAWU-IDS lies in its transparent drift-response structure, validation-based configurability, and interpretable adaptation behavior. The framework provides a testable pipeline for examining when adaptive components are useful, when they are harmful, and how response actions should be selected before deployment. This positioning is particularly relevant for intrusion detection under non-stationary traffic, where aggregate accuracy alone is insufficient and must be considered together with attack-recall behavior, false-negative risk, computational cost, and failure cases.

Future work will focus on four directions. First, stronger attack-recall-oriented optimization is needed to reduce false negatives under severe attack-distribution shifts. Second, more efficient online adaptation strategies should be explored to reduce computational overhead in latency-sensitive deployment scenarios. Third, MSDI can be further improved by incorporating more fine-grained drift annotations and evaluations under gradual, recurring, and class-conditional drift. Fourth, broader validation across additional datasets, longer traffic streams, and real-world deployment environments is required to assess the generalizability of validation-selected drift-response policies.
