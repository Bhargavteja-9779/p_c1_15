Abstract

Spectrum-based software fault localization methods perform well in single-fault scenarios, but encounter significant challenges in multi-fault scenarios. These challenges include difficulties in measuring test case similarity, instability caused by random clustering initialization, and the limitations of fixed cluster numbers in capturing complex fault distributions. To resolve these issues, we propose an Iterative Self-Organizing Clustering Method for Multi Fault Localization (ISOMFL). ISOMFL constructs vector representations of failed test cases and refines the Footrule distance metric to better distinguish differences between test cases. It also utilizes a dissimilarity matrix to identify optimal initial cluster centers, reducing the impact of random initialization. Furthermore, the method dynamically adjusts the number of clusters through a self-organizing mechanism, ensuring that clustering results more accurately reflect the actual fault distribution. Experimental evaluations on six fault projects from the SIR and Faults4J databases demonstrate that ISOMFL achieves consistently competitive multi-fault localisation performance compared with existing approaches, while exhibiting more robust and stable behavior across diverse fault distributions. To support reproducibility, the source code is publicly available at https://github.com/jrd12138/ISOMFL.git.

1 Introduction

Existing software multi-fault localisation methods based on cluster analysis aim to group failed test cases into fault-centered clusters, where test cases within the same cluster are associated with the same fault, while those in different clusters correspond to distinct faults (Zhang et al. 2025). Spectrum-based fault localisation (SBFL) methods have been widely proposed as general techniques to reduce the cost and complexity of fault localisation (Jones et al. 2002; Laghari et al. 2016; Passos et al. 2015; Orban 2025). These methods construct a program spectrum using execution trace information, calculate the suspiciousness of program statements based on statistical spectrum data, and generate fault reports to assist developers in identifying faulty statements. However, SBFL methods are primarily designed for single-fault scenarios (Landsberg et al. 2018; Abreu et al. 2009; Souza et al. 2016; Heiden et al. 2019), significantly limiting their effectiveness in multi-fault cases (Keller et al. 2017; Digiuseppe and Jones 2011). The presence of multiple faults often introduces interference between fault locations, leading to overlapping behaviors in test cases. This interference complicates the fault localisation process, making it more time-consuming, computationally expensive, and less accurate (Yoo 2012).

To enhance the applicability of spectrum-based techniques in multi-fault scenarios, several approaches have been proposed. Jones et al. (2007) pioneered the concept of parallel debugging, which clusters failed test cases into fault-focused groups and assigns them to different developers for simultaneous fault localization, establishing a foundational framework for clustering-based multi-fault localization. Steimann and Frenkel (2012) applied integer linear programming algorithms to partition coverage matrices into smaller independent sub-problems, but their approach did not attempt to locate all faulty statements and faced scalability challenges. Liu et al. (2016) introduced an iterative debugging method based on a simulated link model, which employs decision trees to cluster failed test cases caused by individual faults. While this method improves fault isolation, it requires multiple debugging iterations, which can be labor-intensive and time-consuming. Similarly, Chen et al. (2017) proposed a multi-objective optimisation feature selection (MOFES) method for fault prediction, aiming to minimise feature subsets while improving the prediction model’s performance. Despite these advancements, these methods face common limitations in terms of scalability, robustness to initialization sensitivity, and the ability to jointly address distance measurement and dynamic cluster number determination, which collectively limit their practical applicability.

Despite these advancements, three critical challenges remain unresolved in multi-fault localization.First, measuring the distance between failed test cases effectively remains a persistent issue (An et al. 2021), as traditional distance metrics often fail to capture nuanced relationships in multi-fault scenarios. Second, determining the correct number of clusters when the number of faults is unknown is a complex problem, particularly in software systems with highly variable fault distributions (Cao et al. 2023). Third, clustering-based methods are often prone to convergence to local minima due to the random initialisation of cluster centers, which undermines the stability and reliability of results (Song et al. 2022). These challenges collectively hinder the accuracy, efficiency, and robustness of existing multi-fault localisation methods.

To address these issues, this study proposes the Iterative Self-Organizing Clustering-based Multi-Fault Localization (ISOMFL) method. ISOMFL eliminates the need for manually determining the number of clusters by enhancing the K-means algorithm with dynamic merging and splitting operations, enabling it to adaptively adjust the number of clusters based on the fault distribution—laying the foundation for efficient and accurate multi-fault localization.

This study introduces a novel technique for efficiently localising multiple faults in software and makes the following significant contributions:

  • A software multi-fault localisation method, ISOMFL, is proposed that enhances K-means with dynamic merging and splitting operations to adaptively determine cluster numbers without prior fault count knowledge, achieving more robust and accurate multi-fault localisation.

  • An improved Footrule distance measurement formula is developed, assigning greater weight to highly suspicious statements and less weight to less suspicious ones, enabling better measurement of the distance between failed test cases.

  • A dissimilarity matrix is used to identify data points with high mean dissimilarity as initial clustering centers, improving clustering performance and addressing random initialization issues.

Built to boost software multi-fault localization efficacy, the framework has three optimizations: iterative self-organizing clustering, improved Footrule formula, dissimilarity matrix for initialization.

2 Related work

In this section, we review the existing literature relevant to our work. We begin by examining foundational spectrum-based fault localisation techniques, followed by a discussion of multi-fault localisation approaches. This review provides the necessary context and motivation for the design of our proposed method.

2.1 Spectrum-based fault localisation techniques

Spectrum-based fault localisation (SBFL) has emerged as a prominent approach for identifying software faults by analyzing program execution traces. Harrold et al. (2000) investigated the relationship between program spectrum information and program faults, revealing that behavioral anomalies in faulty programs can effectively reflect program abnormalities. Building on this insight, Abreu et al. (2007) proposed the Ochiai method, which identifies statements that are either not executed or produce unexpected results as potential fault locations. Following this, various suspiciousness formulas have been developed to quantify the likelihood of statements being faulty, such as Jaccard (2002), Tarantula (2002; 2005), Naish1 Naish et al. (2011), and GP02/GP13 Yoo (2012). Despite their varying formulations, these methods share common design principles that prioritize statements covered by more failed test cases (Steimann et al. 2013).

SBFL methods have been extended to diverse domains by Laghari et al. (2016), Passos et al. (2015), and Orban (2025). However, extensive evaluations (Landsberg et al. 2018; Abreu et al. 2009; Souza et al. 2016; Heiden et al. 2019) reveal that traditional SBFL methods, while effective for single-fault scenarios, face significant challenges in multi-fault environments (Keller et al. 2017; Digiuseppe and Jones 2011). In such cases, multiple faults introduce interference that complicates localisation and reduces accuracy (Yoo 2012). To address this fundamental challenge, ISOMFL employs clustering to group failed test cases according to their associated faults. This enables separate analysis of each fault’s diagnostic information, thereby preventing fault interactions from compromising localization accuracy.

2.2 Clustering-based multi-fault localisation

To address SBFL limitations in multi-fault scenarios, researchers have explored clustering-based approaches that group failed test cases by their associated faults. However, these approaches face three critical challenges: measuring distances between failed test cases, determining appropriate cluster numbers, and avoiding local minima from random initialization. Lee et al. (2016) proposed weighting test cases based on fault-revealing capabilities, but this struggles with high-dimensional spectra and cluster number determination. Naish et al. (2018) introduced genetic programming with hyperbolic indicators, though it suffers from high computational costs and parameter sensitivity. Fu et al. (2016) developed test case prioritization to reduce inspection effort, but it does not resolve similarity measurement challenges.

For similarity measurement, Naish et al. (2011) developed the J2 method using Jaccard distance, but it treats all statements uniformly, ignoring that highly suspicious statements should have greater influence. An et al. (2021) explored hypergraph modeling for complex relationships, but it faces severe scalability challenges. Gao and Wong (2018) proposed MSeer, which enhances k-medoids clustering by selecting actual data points as cluster centers. However, MSeer requires manual cluster number specification, which is unrealistic when fault counts are unknown. ISOMFL addresses this through dynamic cluster merging and splitting operations that automatically adjust cluster numbers based on intra-cluster variance and inter-cluster distances. This adaptive mechanism enables ISOMFL to outperform MSeer across most benchmark programs, particularly in average-case scenarios where fault distributions are complex.

2.3 Iterative and adaptive approaches

Iterative refinement strategies have been explored to improve effectiveness. Liu et al. (2016) introduced an iterative debugging method using decision trees, but it requires multiple manual iterations making it labor-intensive. Callaghan and Fischer (2023) explored iterative test suite reduction, demonstrating that eliminating redundant tests can enhance precision.

The iterative self-organizing clustering algorithm (ISODATA), introduced by Suzuki et al. (1987), offers a foundation for addressing clustering challenges. ISODATA enhances K-means through adaptive control strategies that dynamically adjust cluster numbers via merging, splitting, and deletion operations, automatically identifying patterns without prior knowledge of data distribution. However, direct application requires addressing domain-specific challenges including appropriate distance metrics and initialization strategies. Chen et al. (2017) proposed multi-objective feature selection (MOFES) for dimensionality challenges, but it faces difficulties with sparse coverage matrices and requires substantial labeled training data that may not be available for new projects. ISOMFL’s self-organizing clustering mechanism achieves adaptive cluster adjustment within a single automated process, without requiring intermediate developer feedback, as evidenced by our experimental results showing consistent performance across diverse fault scenarios.

2.4 Recent developments and research gaps

Recent research has explored various advanced techniques to enhance fault localization effectiveness. Yu et al. (2022) proposed context-based cluster fault localization, which leverages contextual information to improve clustering quality. Xie et al. (2022) introduced a universal data augmentation approach that enhances fault localization by generating synthetic training data. Wong et al. (2011 developed a crosstab-based statistical approach for better fault localization, while Le et al. (2015) demonstrated that combining information retrieval with spectrum-based techniques yields improved results. Kang, An, and Yoo (2024) proposed an explainable fault localization technique that provides natural language explanations to aid developer understanding. Yang et al. (2024) explored approaches for test-free fault localization, demonstrating the potential to bypass traditional coverage collection requirements. Rezaalipour and Furia (2024) addressed language-specific challenges through FauxPy, adapting fault localization to Python’s dynamic features, revealing that dynamic typing significantly reduces static analysis effectiveness and necessitates language-aware techniques.

Beyond fault localisation, the broader clustering literature has recently witnessed significant progress in parameter-free and autonomous clustering techniques. Yang et al. (2025) proposed Torque Clustering, an entirely parameter-free hierarchical method that autonomously determines the optimal number of clusters by identifying peaks in mass and distance, and further extended it to distributed scenarios with distributed torque clustering (Yang and Lin 2024), which preserves the parameter-free property across multiple sites. In a complementary direction, Fei et al. (2025) proposed the Bombing algorithm, a parameter-free density-based method that combines global and local density estimations to automatically detect the optimal number of clusters. These studies reflect a clear trend toward fully autonomous clustering, with which ISOMFL aligns in spirit by eliminating the need to specify the number of clusters in advance. However, ISOMFL retains a small number of threshold parameters that are deliberately preserved to exploit domain-specific characteristics of multi-fault localisation, including the suspiciousness ranking distribution of failed test cases and the tight integration with spectrum-based fault ranking formulas—characteristics that purely general-purpose autonomous methods cannot leverage. Investigating how to incorporate the parameter-free principles of Torque Clustering into the fault localisation context remains a promising direction for future work.We further note that established clustering validation frameworks such as the Clustering R package (Martos et al. 2023) have demonstrated the importance of evaluating candidate partitions through multiple internal quality measures—including the Silhouette coefficient, Dunn index, connectivity measures, and compactness/separation criteria—providing a methodological foundation for future validation of ISOMFL’s clustering strategy.

Zhang et al. (2025) proposed FSMFL, a search-based method that our experiments show performs comparably to ISOMFL in some scenarios but less consistently overall. However, broader surveys by Zakari et al. (2020) and de Souza et al. (2016) identify persistent research gaps including inability to manage high-dimensional spectra effectively, difficulties in dynamic cluster adjustment, and initialization sensitivity. Kochhar et al. (2016) further revealed gaps between academic research and industrial needs. ISOMFL directly addresses these challenges through three integrated innovations: an improved Footrule distance metric that assigns weights based on suspiciousness rankings, a dissimilarity matrix-based initialization that systematically identifies optimal cluster centers, and an iterative self-organizing mechanism with dynamic merging and splitting that enables automatic cluster number adjustment without prior fault count knowledge. Our experimental results in Section 4, validated on the Siemens Test Suite (2005) and Defects4J (2014), demonstrate consistent improvements across diverse programs and fault scenarios.

3 Multiple-fault localisation based on iterative self-organizing clustering

To address the multiple-fault localisation problem, we propose a software multiple-fault localisation method based on iterative self-organizing clustering, which consists of two phases. In the clustering analysis phase, the following steps are performed: first, spectral information is used to compute the suspiciousness sequence, and a vector representation of failed test trajectories is constructed based on this sequence. Next, an enhanced Footrule distance metric calculates the distances between vectors, while the dissimilarity matrix refines the initial clustering centers. Finally, the iterative self-organizing clustering algorithm dynamically adjusts the number of clusters to achieve an optimal configuration.In the multiple-fault localisation phase, the clusters generated in the first phase are used to localise multiple faults. Failed test cases within the same cluster are associated with the same fault location, enabling the creation of a fault-centered suspiciousness ranking table using the coverage information from failed test tracks within each cluster. This process assists development and maintenance teams in efficiently identifying and localising multiple faults in the software.The methodological framework is illustrated in Fig. 1.

  2. (2)

    Improving Footrule distance metric to calculate the distance between vectors

The performance of clustering algorithms largely depends on the calculation of distance measures between individuals. In order to better measure the distance of suspect sequences, this paper proposes an improved Footrule distance metric. Firstly, the original Footrule distance was introduced, and then it was described how to improve the Footrule distance metric by assigning greater weight to statements with high suspicion rankings. Footrule distance is a metric used to calculate the absolute distance between two rankings of the same size. The farther the distance, the greater the difference between the two rankings. Given \( n \) skeptical rankings \( Q_i (i = 1, 2, 3, ..., n) \), all of which have \( m \) statements, the original Footrule distance \( (Q_i, Q_j) \) is defined as:

$$\begin{aligned} \text {Distance}(Q_i, Q_j) = \sum _{1 \le k < m} |Q_i(s_k) - Q_j(s_k)| \end{aligned}$$
(1)

Among them, \( Q_i \) represents the ranking sequence of the \( i^{th} \) failed test case, \( s_k \) represents the \( k^{th} \) statement, and \( Q_i(s_k) \) represents the element of the \( i^{th} \) position ranking sequence corresponding to the \( k^{th} \) statement. Table 1 assumes the following three suspicion rankings \( (Q_1, Q_2, Q_3) \), corresponding to four statements \( (s_1, s_2, s_3, s_4) \). Each cell denotes the statement’s placement in the relevant ranking. Referring to the third column, it indicates that statement \( s_2 \) is ranked second in \( Q_1 \), but first in \( Q_2 \) and \( Q_3 \).

Based on the original Footrule distance, the values of Distance \((Q_1, Q_2)\), Distance \((Q_1, Q_3)\), and Distance \((Q_2, Q_3)\) are 4, 2, and 2, respectively. This means the distance between \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\) equals the distance between \(Q_2\) and \(Q_3\). However, in fault localisation, differences in higher-ranked statements should contribute more to the distance. The inconsistent pairs between \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\) are \(s_2\) and \(s_3\), which hold ranks 1 and 2 , whereas the inconsistent pairs between \(Q_2\) and \(Q_3\) are \(s_1\) and \(s_4\), which hold ranks 3 and 4 . Therefore, the distance between \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\) should be greater than the distance between \(Q_2\) and \(Q_3\).

The influence of suspicious degree values on the distance values between rankings was not considered in the original Footrule distance. To address this problem, we introduce a globally defined set \(\text {max}\) that identifies the least suspicious statements across all failed test cases. Formally, given a set of n ranking vectors \(\{Q_1, Q_2, \ldots , Q_n\}\) over m statements, the \(\text {max}\) set is defined globally as:

$$\begin{aligned} \text {max} = \left\{ k \;\big |\; 1 \le k \le m,\ \exists \, i \in \{1,\ldots ,n\},\ Q_i(s_k) = \max _{1 \le j \le m} Q_i(s_j)\right\} \end{aligned}$$
(2)

That is, a statement index k belongs to the \(\textrm{max}\) set if statement \(s_k\) holds the largest ranking value in at least one ranking vector. This set is computed once as a global preprocessing step before any pairwise distance calculation, ensuring that the same \(\textrm{max}\) set is applied consistently across all pairwise comparisons. As a result, the weight assigned to each statement position is independent of which specific pair of vectors is being compared, thereby guaranteeing the symmetry of the distance metric.For statements belonging to the \(\textrm{max}\) set, their ranking differences receive a reduced weight (\(\omega = 1/2\)), as these statements have low suspiciousness and their ranking variations are less relevant to fault discrimination.

For statements outside the \(\textrm{max}\) set, their ranking differences receive full weight , ensuring that disagreements among more suspicious statements contribute more significantly to the measured distance. The weight function and the improved Footrule distance formula are defined as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} \omega (k) = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1/2 & \text {if } k \in \textrm{max} \\ 1 & \text {otherwise} \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$
(3)
$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Distance}(Q_i, Q_j) = \sum _{1 \le k < m} \omega (k) \cdot |Q_i(s_k) - Q_j(s_k)| \end{aligned}$$
(4)

Among them, \(\omega (k)\) indicates the weight assigned to statement \(s_k\) based on the globally precomputed \(\textrm{max}\) set defined above, \(Q_i\) represents the ranking sequence, \(s_k\) represents the kth statement, and \(Q_i(s_k)\) represents the element in the ranking sequence corresponding to the kth statement. We now demonstrate the complete calculation using the improved Footrule distance with the example from Table 1.

We determine the global \(\textrm{max}\) set by identifying, for each vector, the statement with the largest ranking value . In \(Q_1\), the maximum ranking value is 4, achieved by \(s_1\). In \(Q_2\), the maximum ranking value is 4, achieved by \(s_4\). In \(Q_3\), the maximum ranking value is 4, achieved by \(s_1\) . Thus, \(\textrm{max} = \{1, 4\}\) . Statements in the \(\textrm{max}\) set receive reduced weight , while all other statements receive full weight. Therefore, \(\omega (1) = 1/2\), \(\omega (2) = 1\), \(\omega (3) = 1\), \(\omega (4) = 1/2\).

Using these weights, the pairwise distances are calculated as follows: \(D(Q_1, Q_2) = \frac{1}{2}|4-3| + 1 \cdot |2-1| + 1 \cdot |1-2| + \frac{1}{2}|3-4| = 0.5 + 1 + 1 + 0.5 = 3\); \(D(Q_1, Q_3) = \frac{1}{2}|4-4| + 1 \cdot |2-1| + 1 \cdot |1-2| + \frac{1}{2}|3-3| = 0 + 1 + 1 + 0 = 2\); \(D(Q_2, Q_3) = \frac{1}{2}|3-4| + 1 \cdot |1-1| + 1 \cdot |2-2| + \frac{1}{2}|4-3| = 0.5 + 0 + 0 + 0.5 = 1\).

The improved Footrule distance yields \(D(Q_1, Q_3) = 2 > D(Q_2, Q_3) = 1\), which differs from the original Footrule distance where both equal 2. This is because the disagreement between \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\) occurs at \(s_2\) and \(s_3\), which are outside the \(\textrm{max}\) set and thus retain full weight. In contrast, the disagreement between \(Q_2\) and \(Q_3\) occurs at \(s_1\) and \(s_4\), which belong to the \(\textrm{max}\) set and receive reduced weight. The improved metric correctly assigns less influence to ranking differences among low-suspiciousness statements, ensuring that distances are primarily driven by disagreements among highly suspicious statements. After calculating the distances between suspicion rankings, the ISOMFL method clusters entities with smaller distances into the same group.

  1. (3)

    Screening of initial clustering centers The original iterative self-organizing clustering algorithm randomly pre-selected \(N_c\) clustering centers. However, the allocation of initial clustering centers is incorrect, and the clustering results may only converge to local optima. To solve this problem, this article constructs a dissimilarity matrix, calculates the mean dissimilarity between data, and selects the data point with the highest dissimilarity as the clustering center. The specific steps are as follows:

Using the improved Footrule distance metric defined in Section 3.1(3) to calculate the distance between vectors as the degree of dissimilarity, the calculation formula is:

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Distance}(Q_i, Q_j) = \sum _{1<k<n} \omega (k) \cdot |Q_i(s_k) - Q_j(s_k)| \end{aligned}$$
(5)

Among them, \(\omega (k)\) is the weight function defined in Equation (3), determined by the globally precomputed \(\textrm{max}\) set; \(Q_i\) represents the ranking sequence, \(s_k\) represents the kth statement, and \(Q_i(s_k)\) represents the element in the ranking sequence corresponding to the kth statement.

Build a dissimilarity matrix to store the dissimilarity between objects, i.e.

$$\begin{aligned} M = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \textrm{dis}(Q_1, Q_2) & \cdots & \textrm{dis}(Q_1, Q_n) \\ \textrm{dis}(Q_2, Q_1) & 0 & \cdots & \textrm{dis}(Q_2, Q_n) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \textrm{dis}(Q_n, Q_1) & \textrm{dis}(Q_n, Q_2) & \cdots & 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$
(6)

Calculate the mean dissimilarity, calculate the average distance between each data point \( Q_i \) and each object in the dataset, denoted as \( \textrm{adis}(Q_i) \), and the formula is:

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{adis}(Q_i) = \frac{1}{n} \sum _{j=1}^n \textrm{dis}(Q_j, Q_i) \end{aligned}$$
(7)

Calculate the overall dissimilarity of the dataset. The overall dissimilarity of the dataset depends on the distribution of its data points. The overall dissimilarity of the dataset is denoted as \( \textrm{Tids} \), which reflects the sparsity of the dataset. The formula is:

$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Tids} = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum _{i=1}^n \sum _{j=1}^n \textrm{dis}(Q_i, Q_j) \end{aligned}$$
(8)

To begin, pick a sample from the dataset to serve as the initial clustering center point. Then, calculate the distance between all samples in the dataset and the cluster center point, and identify the sample farthest from the cluster center point, selecting it as the second initial cluster center point. Then, calculate the distance between the sample and all selected cluster center points. If the distance between the sample and any cluster center point is greater than the average distance between all samples in the dataset and the cluster center point, the sample can become a new cluster center point. If a cluster center point that meets the requirements cannot be found, select the sample that is next to the second cluster center point for judgment until all \( N_c \) initial cluster center points are selected.

  1. (4)

    Iterative self-organizing clustering Perform initial clustering according to the nearest neighbor rule. Input the vector set \( Q \) of the failed test trajectory constructed in (1) = {\( Q_1, Q_2, Q_3, \ldots , Q_n \)}, and input the pre-selected \( N_c \) initial clustering centers {\( Z_1, Z_2, \ldots , Z_{N_c} \)}; Calculate the improved Footrule distance between each failed trajectory vector and each pre-selected cluster center, and allocate the failed trajectory vector to neighboring clusters \( S_j \) based on the distance between each failed trajectory vector and the cluster center. If the vector satisfies formula 8, then \( Q \in S_j \).

    $$\begin{aligned} \Vert Q - Z_j\Vert = \min \{\Vert Q - Z_i\Vert , i = 1, 2, \ldots , N_c\} \end{aligned}$$
    (9)

After completing the discrimination of all data, calculate the number of samples \( N_j \) within each cluster, compare \( N_j \) with the parameters \( \theta _N \), and when the number of samples within the cluster is less than \( \theta _N \), it is determined that the cluster contains an insufficient number of samples. Remove \( N_c - 1 \) of this class, and recalculate the distance between the data in the cluster and its other cluster centers. Reassign based on the distance. Due to the removal of a cluster and the reassignment of samples within the cluster to other classes, each updated cluster needs to recalculate and distinguish its cluster center. The selection of the cluster center uses the centroid of the data as the cluster center within the cluster. At this time, the correction of each cluster center is shown in formulas 9.

$$\begin{aligned} Z_j = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{Q \in S_j} Q, \quad j = 1,2,\ldots ,N_c \end{aligned}$$
(10)

After completing the initial clustering of the ISOMFL algorithm, cluster splitting and cluster merging will be performed. If the number of samples in a cluster is too small, or if the distance between two clusters is very close, they need to be merged; On the contrary, if the number of samples in a certain cluster is too large or its internal variance value is too large, then the cluster splitting operation needs to be carried out. Criterion: If the current number \( N_c \) is less than \( K/2 \), it is determined that the number of clusters is too small and a splitting operation is performed; If the current number \( N_c \) is greater than \( 2K \), it is determined that there are too many clusters and a merge operation is performed.

Merge operation

Calculate the distance between all cluster centers. The distance between the centers of \(S_{i}\) and \(S_{j}\) classes is calculated as shown in formula 10.

$$\begin{aligned} D_{ij} = \left\| Z_{i} - Z_{j}\right\| , \quad i = 1, 2, \ldots , N_{c} - 1, \quad j = i + 1, \ldots , N_{c} \end{aligned}$$
(11)

Compare all \(D_{ij}\) values with the threshold, and arrange the \(D_{ij}\) values below the threshold in ascending order, \(\left\{ D_{i_{1}j_{1}}, D_{i_{2}j_{2}}, \ldots , D_{i_{L}j_{L}}\right\} \), where \(D_{i_{1}j_{1}}< D_{i_{2}j_{2}}< \ldots < D_{i_{L}j_{L}}\).

If two clusters with a distance of \(D_{i,j}\) are merged, a new cluster center is obtained as shown in formula 11. Each time a pair of clusters is merged, the number of cluster centers \(N_{c}\) will decrease by 1.

$$\begin{aligned} Z_i^* \!=\! \frac{1}{N_{i_i} \!+\! N_{j_i}} \left( N_{i_i} Z_{i_i} \!+\! N_{j_i} Z_{j_i} \right) , \quad l \!= \!1,2,\ldots ,L \end{aligned}$$
(12)

Splitting operation

Determine the standard deviation vector for the distances between data points within individual clusters, along with the standard deviation of each data point in every dimension. For Class \( S_j \), there is \(\sigma _j = \left( \sigma _{j1}, \sigma _{j2}, \ldots , \sigma _{jn} \right) ^T\) where each component is:

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma _{ji} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_j} \sum _{\zeta _k \in S_j} \left( Q_{ki} - z_{ji} \right) ^2} \end{aligned}$$
(13)

Among them, \( j = 1, 2, \ldots , N_c \) is the number of clusters; \( i = 1, 2, \ldots , n \) is the dimension, that is, \( Q \) is the n-dimensional pattern vector, that is, the number of features used in recognition and classification is \( n \). \( Q_{ki} \) is the \( i^{\textrm{th}} \) component of \( Q_k \) in the \( k^{\textrm{th}} \) sample of class \( S \), \( Z_{ji} \) is the \( i^{\textrm{th}} \) component of the cluster center \( Z_j \) in the \( S_j \) class, and \( \frac{\sigma _{ji}}{J} \) is the standard deviation of the \( i^{\textrm{th}} \) component of the \( S_j \) sample.

Find the maximum component of each standard deviation vector. The maximum component of \(\sigma _j\) is denoted as \(\sigma _j^{\max }\), \(j = 1, 2, \ldots , N_c\).

For each \( j = 1, 2, \ldots , N_c \), if \( \sigma _j^{\max } > \theta \), then it indicates that the standard deviation of \( S_j \) class samples in the corresponding direction is greater than the allowed value. Split \( Z_j \) into two new cluster centers \( Z_j^+ \) and \( Z_j^- \), and add 1 to \( N_c \).

  1. (5)

    Theoretical Properties and Design Justification The improved Footrule distance defined in Equations(3) and(4) constitutes a valid distance metric, as it satisfies the three fundamental properties of non-negativity, symmetry, and the triangle inequality. For non-negativity, since \(\omega (k) > 0\) and \(|Q_i(s_k) - Q_j(s_k)| \ge 0\) for all k, we immediately have \(D(Q_i, Q_j) \ge 0\), with equality if and only if \(Q_i = Q_j\). Symmetry is guaranteed by the global computation of the \(\textrm{max}\) set, which is independent of the specific pair being compared and thus assigns identical weights regardless of argument order, yielding \(D(Q_i, Q_j) = D(Q_j, Q_i)\). Finally, the triangle inequality \(D(Q_i, Q_l) \le D(Q_i, Q_j) + D(Q_j, Q_l)\) follows directly from the standard triangle inequality for absolute values combined with the positivity of \(\omega (k)\).

The choice of binary weights (\(\omega = 1/2\) or \(\omega = 1\)) is motivated by interpretability and computational efficiency. The binary scheme offers two practical advantages. First, it produces a parameter-free weighting rule that does not require dataset-specific tuning. Second, it enforces a clear separation between high-suspicion and low-suspicion statements, which directly aligns with the fault-localisation objective. Continuous alternatives such as inverse-rank, linearly decaying, or exponentially decaying weights could provide finer discrimination, but typically require additional design choices such as the decay rate.

The cluster splitting and merging rules are similarly grounded in clear theoretical motivations: the splitting criterion is triggered when \(N_c < K/2\), indicating possible over-aggregation, and is performed only when the maximum component of the standard deviation vector \(\sigma _j\) exceeds the threshold \(\theta _S\)—a condition rooted in the classical principle of variance minimisation, since high intra-cluster variance along any dimension indicates heterogeneous samples likely corresponding to different faults, and splitting along the dimension of maximum variance maximally reduces the resulting intra-cluster dispersion; conversely, the merging criterion is triggered when \(N_c > 2K\), indicating possible over-fragmentation, and merges cluster pairs whose inter-cluster distance \(D_{ij}\) falls below the threshold \(\theta _C\), processed in ascending order of \(D_{ij}\) to minimise the risk of erroneously combining truly distinct fault clusters. The thresholds \(\theta _N\), \(\theta _S\), and \(\theta _C\) are configured based on the structural characteristics of the dataset, and the interplay between splitting and merging creates a self-correcting mechanism: over-aggregation triggers splitting, over-fragmentation triggers merging, and the iteration converges when both criteria are simultaneously satisfied, enabling ISOMFL to handle diverse fault distributions without requiring the exact fault count as input.

3.2 Multiple-fault localisation phase

After clustering the failed test trajectories in the initial phase Fig. 3, ISOMFL combines the failed test trajectories within each cluster with all successful test trajectories to construct a new coverage information spectrum. The suspicion formula is then applied to calculate the suspicion values of program statements, resulting in the generation of a fault report. This study employs the Ochiai formula, a widely recognized and effective method, for calculating suspicion values and producing fault reports.

3.3 Computational complexity analysis

In this subsection, we analyse the complexity of ISOMFL. The computational complexity of ISOMFL mainly comprises single fault detection, fault ranking, and clustering. The computational complexity of single fault detection is \(O\left( n \cdot T\right) \), where \(T\) is the running time of the programme and \(n\) is the number of faults to be detected. The computational complexity of fault ranking is \(O\left( m \cdot R\right) \), where \(R\) is the operation time of the statistics and ranking, and \(m\) is the number of fault localisations. During the iterative self-organizing clustering process, constructing vector representations of failed test trajectories costs \(O(n^{2})\), and screening initial cluster centers via the dissimilarity matrix costs \(O(n^{3})\). In summary, the total computational complexity of ISOMFL is \(O(n \cdot T + m \cdot R + n^{2} + n^{3})\).

Although the cubic term \(O(n^3)\) is theoretically dominant, its practical impact is limited because n denotes the number of failing test cases rather than the total test suite size. In typical multi-fault localisation scenarios, only a fraction of tests fail for any given fault combination, so n remains substantially smaller than the full test suite—ranging from a few dozen to several hundred in our benchmarks, well within tractable limits. The dissimilarity matrix M requires \(O(n^2)\) storage, which remains manageable on modern hardware even at the order of thousands, making computation time rather than memory the dominant concern for large n.

To mitigate the cubic cost in industrial settings, several approximation strategies are applicable: sampling-based methods reduce the effective n by computing the dissimilarity matrix on a representative subset; sparse matrix representations exploit the natural sparsity of high-dimensional ranking vectors; locality-sensitive hashing approximates nearest-neighbor relationships in sub-quadratic time; and incremental clustering updates the matrix only as new failing test cases arrive. 4 Experimental results

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method, this chapter compares ISOMFL with five baselines using three evaluation indicators. MSeer (2012) improves the K-medoids clustering algorithm to better identify relationships between failed test cases and fault locations. J2 (Naish et al. 2011) is a classic method that uses Jaccard distance between suspicion sequence vectors of failed test cases to group them by similarity. HAC-Avg is a hierarchical agglomerative clustering baseline with average linkage that uses the same improved Footrule distance as ISOMFL, with the cluster number set to the true fault count. 4.3 Evaluation metric

To evaluate the effectiveness of the fault-localisation method, we use the following three criteria from different perspectives.

  1. (1)

    Average Number of Statements Examined (ANSE). ANSE represents the average number of statements that need to be examined to localize all faults in the multiple-fault version of the program. Given a program has multiple-fault versions, ANSE is calculated as follows:

    $$\begin{aligned} ANSE = \frac{\sum _{i=1}^{x} \text {Method}(i)}{x} \end{aligned}$$
    (14)

    where \( \textrm{Method}(i) \) in Formula (13) indicates the total number of statements to be checked when the method localizes all faults of the \( i^{\textrm{th}} \) version of the program and \( x \) indicates the number of versions. Given a program \( P \), \( M(i) \) and \( N(i) \) indicate the total number of statements to be checked for Method \( M \) and Method \( N \), respectively, to localize all faults of the \( i^{\textrm{th}} \) multiple-fault version of the program. If \( \textrm{ANSE}(M) < \textrm{ANSE}(N) \), Method \( M \) is more effective than Method \( N \).

  2. (2)

    T-EXAM. In previous studies, EXAM (Wong et al. 2011) was used to measure the percentage of statements that need to be inspected before the first fault is localized. In this paper, we used the extended metric T-EXAM proposed by Gao et al. (2018) The following Formula (14) specifies the calculation of this metric to evaluate the effectiveness of the multiple fault localisation technique:

    $$\begin{aligned} T - EXAM = \sum _{i=1}^{\mu } \sum _{j=1}^{\tau } EXAM_{ij} \end{aligned}$$
    (15)

    where \(\mu \) denotes the number of iterations; \(\tau \) indicates the total number of fault-centered clusters produced in each iteration; and \(\textrm{EXAM}_{i,j}\) represents the percentage of statements that need to be examined for localizing the \(j^{\textrm{th}}\) fault in the \(i^{\textrm{th}}\) iteration.

  3. (3)

    Top-N. As widely used in fault localisation work, this metric indicates the total number of faulty versions of program with at least one fault element in the ranked list of Top-N (Le et al. 2015). As revealed by Kochhar et al. (2016), only the top-ranked program entities are considered and checked during fault localisation. Therefore, Top-N (N = 5, 10, 20, 30) is exploited in our experimental study as an evaluation metric.

    (9) Sensitivity Analysis of the Weighting Coefficient

To validate the choice of \(\omega = 1/2\) in the improved Footrule distance, we conducted a sensitivity experiment over a range of discrete weight values and one continuous weighting scheme. All experiments were conducted on grep, time, and chart under the 4-fault average-case scenario, consistent with the rest of Section 4.4. Five discrete weight values were evaluated, namely \(\omega \in \{0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.8\}\), where smaller values impose stronger downweighting on statements in the max set. We also evaluated the unweighted variant \(\omega = 1\) and an inverse-rank weighting scheme defined as \(\omega (k) = 1/\textrm{rank}(s_k)\), which assigns a continuously decaying weight based on the suspiciousness ranking of each statement. The results are summarized in Table 20.

The results reveal three consistent patterns. First, all weighted variants outperform the unweighted baseline \(\omega = 1\), confirming that downweighting low-suspiciousness statements improves localisation accuracy. Second, ANSE varies non-monotonically with \(\omega \) across the discrete values and reaches its minimum at \(\omega = 0.5\) on all three programmes. Both very small weights such as \(\omega = 0.2\) and weights close to one such as \(\omega = 0.8\) lead to noticeable degradation, indicating that overly aggressive downweighting discards useful discrimination information while overly mild downweighting fails to suppress the influence of low-suspiciousness statements. Third, the inverse-rank scheme achieves slightly lower ANSE than \(\omega = 0.5\) across the three programmes, with relative improvements of 0.59%, 0.53%, and 0.55% on grep, time, and chart respectively. While this confirms that finer-grained continuous weighting can provide additional gains, the magnitude of the improvement is consistently below 1%, and inverse-rank weighting introduces additional computational overhead due to per-statement weight calculation as well as additional design choices regarding tie-breaking and decay form.

These findings support \(\omega = 1/2\) as a robust and pragmatic default. The binary scheme delivers near-optimal accuracy across diverse fault distributions, requires no dataset-specific tuning, and avoids the additional design parameters and runtime cost of continuous alternatives. Investigating whether more sophisticated continuous weighting schemes can yield larger gains while preserving simplicity remains a promising direction for future work.

4.5 Threats to validity

In this section, we examine potential factors that could affect the reliability of our empirical research.

External Validity: External validity concerns whether the results of our experiments can be generalized to other subjects. To ensure the representativeness of our findings, we selected six object programs as benchmarks. However, we acknowledge that empirical studies are inherently imperfect, and unforeseen challenges or bugs may arise during experiments. In future work, we plan to expand the scope of our experimental subjects to enhance generalizability.

Internal Validity: This study demonstrates strong internal validity, which relies on the accuracy of results derived from our unique approach. To address potential shortcomings in tool implementation, we carefully curated our data and validated our approach using elementary programs. A further internal validity concern relates to the construction methodology of multi-fault versions described in Section 4.2, where faults were randomly drawn from the pool of single-fault versions and embedded into the same program, following the standard practice in prior multi-fault localisation work. We did not apply explicit filters to exclude potentially interacting faults, since such filtering is not standard in the literature and would compromise comparability with baseline methods. To mitigate the impact of any individual combination, we generated 30 distinct multi-fault versions for each fault count, and the results reported in Tables 3–9 represent averages across all generated versions. Although different random seeds could in principle produce slightly different absolute values, all compared methods were evaluated on identical fault combinations within each experimental run, ensuring that the relative performance comparisons remain robust.

Conclusion Validity: The validity of our conclusions is supported by the enhanced iterative self-organizing clustering algorithm and the influence of experimental parameter settings on the observed results.

Construct Validity: Construct validity pertains to whether the performance metrics used in our study accurately reflect real-world scenarios. In practice, programs often contain multiple faults. We believe that ANSE is a reasonable metric for measuring the average number of statements checked to locate all faults, while T-EXAM provides a more suitable and rational metric for assessing the efficiency of identifying the first fault compared to the original EXAM. To verify that ISOMFL is distinct from the other three multiple-fault localisation methods, we conducted in-depth experiments.We recognize that selecting an effective clustering analysis approach to improve multiple-fault localisation remains an underdeveloped area. As part of our future work, we aim to explore better clustering methods to further enhance the effectiveness of ISOMFL.

Boundary Behavior under Sparse Failing Test Cases : A further limitation of ISOMFL concerns its boundary behavior when the number of failing test cases associated with a particular fault is extremely small. As a clustering-based method, ISOMFL relies on having a sufficient number of failing test cases to form meaningful clusters that separate distinct faults. When a fault triggers only a handful of failing test cases, the dissimilarity matrix initialisation may not provide enough distinguishing information, and such sparse test cases are likely to be absorbed into larger clusters associated with other faults rather than forming their own dedicated cluster. In fact, the minimum cluster size threshold \(\theta _N\) introduced in Section 3.1(4) is explicitly designed to remove undersized clusters and reassign their members to the nearest remaining clusters, which further reduces the likelihood that a fault triggering very few failing test cases can be isolated into its own cluster. This behavior is an inherent characteristic of clustering-based multi-fault localisation methods in general, not a limitation unique to ISOMFL. The empirical per-fault failure distribution reported in Table 18 provides direct context for assessing the practical scope of ISOMFL. Across all six programmes, the mean number of failing test cases per fault ranges from 13.47 to 67.32, indicating that most faults trigger a sufficient number of failing tests for clustering. However, the per-fault minimum drops to as low as 2 on gzip and flex, confirming that sparsely-failing faults do occur in practice. Such cases delineate the boundary of ISOMFL’s effective operating range and motivate the hybrid strategies discussed above as future work. Investigating hybrid strategies that combine clustering-based localisation with single-fault SBFL techniques for faults with very few failing test cases, and systematically reporting the distribution of failing test case counts, represent important directions for future work to better characterise the practical applicability of ISOMFL.

5 Conclusion

We present a novel software multi-fault localisation method based on iterative self-organizing clustering, designed to address challenges in existing clustering analysis techniques. The proposed method begins by constructing vector representations of failed test cases and enhancing the Footrule distance formula to more accurately calculate distances between these vectors. An improved iterative self-organizing clustering algorithm is then applied to identify optimal initial clustering centers using a dissimilarity matrix. The failed test cases are subsequently combined with successful test cases to generate a program spectrum. Using a spectrum-based fault localisation formula, multiple statement ranking lists are produced to identify fault locations. To evaluate the effectiveness of this approach, experiments were conducted on six object programmes with multiple fault versions, and ISOMFL was compared against five baselines spanning classical clustering , dedicated multi-fault localisation methods, and a recent state-of-the-art technique . The results show that the proposed method achieves consistently competitive localisation performance compared with all five baselines, outperforming them in the majority of fault scenarios while exhibiting more robust and stable behaviour across diverse fault distributions.
